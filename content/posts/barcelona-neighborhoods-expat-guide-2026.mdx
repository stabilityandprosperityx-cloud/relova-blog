[Barcelona](/blog/lisbon-vs-barcelona-expat-guide-2026)'s neighborhoods (barris) vary widely enough in price and character that picking one on the strength of a weekend visit is a common and avoidable mistake. Here's a working breakdown of the areas expats actually end up choosing between.

## Eixample: central, grid-planned, and priced accordingly

Eixample — the sprawling 19th-century grid designed by Ildefons Cerdà, home to Gaudí's Sagrada Família and Casa Batlló — is Barcelona's most central and internationally recognizable district, split informally into Dreta de l'Eixample and Esquerra de l'Eixample (the "left" half, also the heart of the city's LGBTQ+ scene, sometimes called Gaixample). It commands the highest rents on this list, typically EUR 1,300-2,000 for a one-bedroom, reflecting its wide, walkable boulevards and proximity to nearly everything.

## Gràcia: village character inside the city

Gràcia was its own independent town before Barcelona annexed it in the 19th century, and it still feels that way — small squares (Plaça del Sol, Plaça de la Vila de Gràcia), narrow pedestrian streets, and a strong independent, slightly bohemian identity that draws a lot of long-term expats and creatives specifically for that village atmosphere. Rents run somewhat below Eixample, typically EUR 1,100-1,600 for a one-bedroom, for a noticeably different, less grid-like living experience just a short walk or metro ride from the center.

## Poblenou: the tech and startup district

Poblenou, a former industrial and textile manufacturing zone on the coast east of the center, has been substantially redeveloped over the past two decades into Barcelona's answer to a tech district — coworking spaces, startups, and a growing population of remote workers, alongside genuinely good beach access at Bogatell and Nova Icària. Rents typically run EUR 1,100-1,700, competitive with Gràcia despite being newer and less traditionally "Barcelona" in feel.

## Sant Andreu and Sarrià-Sant Gervasi: budget and upscale bookends

Sant Andreu, further from the center in the north of the city, is one of the more affordable options with real metro connectivity, typically EUR 800-1,200 for a one-bedroom — a solid choice for anyone prioritizing budget over walkability to the tourist core. At the other end, Sarrià-Sant Gervasi, in the hills toward Tibidabo, is Barcelona's most affluent residential district — leafy, quiet, favored by families and long-term professionals, with several of the city's top international schools — and prices reflect that, typically EUR 1,400-2,200 for a one-bedroom.

## Born and Barceloneta: worth naming, but rarely the practical first pick

The Gothic Quarter, El Born, and beachfront Barceloneta round out the neighborhoods most first-time visitors fall for — narrow medieval streets, a genuine beach within the city itself, and a concentration of tapas bars and boutique shops. Long-term expats tend to move away from these areas once the novelty wears off: heavy tourist footfall, short-term rental competition that pushes prices up unpredictably, and older housing stock with the same practical limitations (stairs, noise, limited storage) as Lisbon's Alfama. They're worth a short-term stay while you get oriented, but most people relocating for the long haul end up in Eixample, Gràcia, or Poblenou instead once they've actually lived in the city for a few weeks.

## Getting around, and a rent-control wrinkle worth knowing

Barcelona's metro network is dense enough that most of these neighborhoods connect to the center in 15-25 minutes, which matters when weighing a cheaper, further-out option against a pricier central one — the time cost is often smaller than the price difference suggests. Separately, Barcelona and the wider Catalonia region have implemented rent-containment measures in recent years (capping increases in designated "stressed" market zones), and enforcement and scope have shifted periodically with legal challenges, so if you're negotiating a renewal or comparing an asking price against the area average, it's worth checking the current Catalan government rent index (índex de referència de preus de lloguer) rather than relying on last year's figures.
