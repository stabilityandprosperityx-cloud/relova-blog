The single most useful thing to know before choosing a destination as an engineer is that your degree and your license are two separate problems, solved by two different systems. The **Washington Accord**, signed by 23 member bodies including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, gives mutual recognition of *accredited engineering degrees* between signatory countries — it does not hand you a license. Actually practicing under a protected title still requires local registration: a **PE** in the US, **CEng** through the UK's Engineering Council, **P.Eng** through a provincial body in Canada, or **CPEng** in Australia, each with its own experience-verification and, often, a professional practice exam.

## Germany rewards mechanical and electrical engineers with its fastest PR clock

On the **EU [Blue Card](/blog/move-to-belgium-guide-2026)**, Germany's 2026 salary threshold is **€50,700** for standard roles, but shortage occupations — which explicitly include most engineering disciplines — clear the bar at **€45,934.20**, and mechanical/electrical engineers commonly land contracts in the **€55,000–80,000** range depending on region and seniority. The real draw is timing: [Blue Card](/blog/move-to-germany-guide-2026) holders can reach permanent residence in as little as **21 months** with B1 German, one of the fastest tracks in the EU. Degree recognition runs through the **anabin** database, and Washington Accord graduates generally clear the assessment without extra exams.

## Canada trades a slower salary ceiling for the fastest citizenship path

Canada's Express Entry system and provincial nominee programs (PNP) put electrical and civil engineers in a **CAD 75,000–120,000** band depending on province and specialization, and **P.Eng** licensing runs through each province's engineering regulator rather than a single national body — Ontario's PEO and Quebec's OIQ have different documentation requirements, so pick your target province before assuming your credentials transfer cleanly. What Canada offers that few competitors do is a genuinely fast route to citizenship: **three years** of permanent residence, full stop, no extra hoops.

## Australia and the UK both reward Washington Accord graduates directly

Australia's **Engineers Australia** accepts a **Competency Demonstration Report (CDR)** in lieu of re-examination for accredited-degree holders, and civil engineers on skilled pathways commonly see **AUD 90,000–130,000**, with permanent residency achievable in **2–4 years** via the Subclass 189 skilled independent visa. The UK moves faster on paper — Skilled Worker visa decisions in as little as **3–8 weeks** — with structural and civil roles around **£41,700–70,000** and CEng registration recognized globally through the Engineering Council's international accords, though UK salary growth has lagged the other Anglophone options in recent years.

## The Gulf trades a residency path for tax-free cash

The **UAE** and **Saudi Arabia** run on a different model entirely: no permanent-residency pipeline for most employer-sponsored engineers, but tax-free compensation that can hit **USD 60,000–120,000** for mechanical and project engineers, often with employer-provided housing on top. This suits a **3–7 year** savings-focused assignment far better than a long-term settlement plan — plan the exit before you plan the arrival.

## Match the discipline to the market, not just the country

A civil engineer chasing Gulf mega-project work, a semiconductor engineer eyeing Singapore's fast Employment Pass processing and roughly **two-year** PR timeline, or an offshore engineer targeting Norway's **NOK 600,000–900,000** bands are solving three different problems even though all three fit under "engineer abroad." Confirm the discipline-specific licensing body and current threshold before assuming a generic "engineers can move anywhere" narrative applies to your specific registration.
