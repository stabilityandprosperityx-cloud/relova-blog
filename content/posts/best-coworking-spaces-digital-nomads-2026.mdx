Coworking pricing tracks local cost of living more closely than it tracks quality — a desk in Tbilisi and a desk in Berlin can offer near-identical fiber speeds and meeting-room access, but the monthly bill differs by a factor of three or four. In Tbilisi, spaces like Terminal and Fabrika charge roughly $80–150 a month for a hot desk, and the city's dense expat/nomad scene means informal networking often happens as much in cafes as in the coworking common areas. Bali's Canggu and Ubud clusters — Dojo, Outpost, Tropical Nomad — run closer to $150–250 monthly, reflecting both higher demand from a much larger nomad population and better on-site amenities like pools and on-site cafes.

## Europe costs more, and buys you EU access

Lisbon's coworking scene (Second Home, Heden, Cowork Central) sits at €200–350 a month, a price that reflects both Portugal's EU membership and the sheer volume of nomad-visa holders who've relocated there since the D7 and digital nomad visa routes opened up. Berlin runs even higher at €250–400, driven by a mature startup ecosystem and genuinely excellent 24/7 access spaces like St Oberholz and Betahaus. Both cities are worth the premium if your work benefits from EU-timezone overlap or in-person networking with funded startups — but if cost is the binding constraint, that premium buys less marginal value than it looks like on paper.

## Day passes and flexible options

Almost every major coworking chain and independent space sells single-day access, typically $5–30 depending on the city, which is the right call for the first week or two in any new base before committing to a monthly membership. Global networks — WeWork, Spaces, and regional chains like Justco in Southeast Asia — offer multi-city passes that can be worth it if you move between their footprint cities every few weeks, but for a single-city stay of a month or more, a local independent space almost always beats a global chain on price without sacrificing much on wifi reliability or community.

## What actually determines whether a space is worth it

Price is the number everyone anchors on, but it's rarely the variable that determines whether a coworking membership was worth it in hindsight. Internet reliability under real load — not the advertised speed, but how the connection holds up when forty people are on a video call simultaneously — separates good spaces from frustrating ones, and it's worth asking to trial a day before committing to a month. Meeting room availability matters more than most first-time nomads expect, especially for anyone doing client calls regularly; a space that's technically cheaper but has a two-person phone booth shared across sixty members creates its own kind of cost. Community programming — regular meetups, skill-shares, demo nights — is the intangible that turns a workspace into a support network, and it's usually visible from a space's event calendar or Instagram before you ever visit in person.

## Matching the space to the season of your trip

A first week or two in any new city is the wrong time to commit to a monthly coworking membership — day passes exist specifically for this trial period, and most nomads who skip straight to a monthly plan end up locked into a space that doesn't fit their actual working rhythm. Longer stays of two months or more generally justify the monthly commitment, both for the cost savings and for the compounding value of becoming a recognized regular in a space's community. Seasonal demand spikes matter too: Bali's coworking spaces fill up noticeably during the European winter as northern-hemisphere nomads flee the cold, and booking a desk (not just showing up) becomes worthwhile during those peak months in a way it isn't during shoulder season.
