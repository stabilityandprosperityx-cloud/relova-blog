International schooling is often the single biggest recurring line item in a family's relocation budget, and it varies enormously by destination — not just in cost, but in how far ahead you need to apply.

## The Gulf and Singapore: high fees, high quality, long waitlists

In the UAE, international school fees (regulated and published annually by the Knowledge and Human Development Authority in Dubai and ADEK in Abu Dhabi) span a wide range depending on curriculum and prestige, commonly AED 25,000-90,000 a year — British and American curriculum schools with strong university placement records sit at the top of that band, while more modest options sit at the bottom. Singapore runs a comparable structure: fees at established international schools (Singapore American School, UWC South East Asia, Tanglin Trust, and similar) commonly run SGD 20,000-55,000 a year, and several of the most sought-after schools maintain waitlists that stretch one to two years, meaning families relocating for a specific start date often need to apply well before the move itself is finalized, sometimes before a work visa is even approved.

## Portugal and lower-cost European options

Portugal offers a noticeably gentler cost curve: international and bilingual schools in [Lisbon](/blog/move-to-lisbon-family-guide-2026), Porto, and the Algarve commonly charge EUR 8,000-18,000 a year, well below Gulf or Singapore levels, making it one of the more accessible countries for families wanting an English-language or IB curriculum without Gulf-level fees. Spain and Italy sit in a broadly similar range in their major cities, though Madrid and Milan international schools at the premium end can run higher.

## Germany: the free public alternative, with a real tradeoff

Germany is worth calling out separately because it offers something most relocation destinations don't: genuinely free public schooling, funded through general taxation, open to resident foreign children regardless of citizenship. The tradeoff is real, though — public schools teach in German, and a child arriving without German fluency will typically need language-support classes (Willkommensklassen, or "welcome classes," exist in most cities specifically for this) before mainstreaming into regular instruction, a process that can take a year or more depending on age and prior exposure. Families who want to avoid that transition entirely opt for Germany's (paid) international schools instead, which run comparable to Portugal's range in most cities.

## What curriculum choice actually means long-term

Beyond cost, the curriculum a school follows matters more than most families weigh it initially. British (IGCSE/A-Level), American (AP-based), and International Baccalaureate (IB Diploma) tracks all lead to different university application pathways, and switching a child between systems mid-way through secondary school — say, moving from an American curriculum school to a British one after a relocation — can create real gaps or redundancies in coursework. If there's a reasonable chance of a second international move during your child's school years, an IB or British-curriculum school is generally considered the more portable choice, since both have a larger global footprint of comparable schools than an American-curriculum school does outside a handful of hub cities.

## Planning around waitlists, not just budget

The biggest planning mistake families make is treating school admission as a formality that happens after the visa and housing are sorted. In the Gulf, Singapore, and several major European hubs, top-tier international schools can have waitlists of one to two years, and popular schools in a given city fill entry-year seats (often kindergarten or first grade) far faster than mid-year transfer seats. If a specific school matters to your relocation timeline, start that application in parallel with your visa process, not after it — a secured school place has, in practice, occasionally sped up visa sponsorship conversations with employers who want a smooth family relocation.
