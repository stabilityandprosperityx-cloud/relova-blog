The single most expensive mistake expats make with money isn't a bad exchange rate — it's carrying a card that charges a 3% foreign transaction fee on every purchase made outside your home country, silently, for years. Fix that first, before worrying about lounge access or points multipliers, because the FX fee compounds on every grocery run, rent payment, and flight home.

## The debit-card foundation: fee-free ATM access

Before credit cards, sort out cash access. **Charles Schwab's Investor Checking account** remains the standard recommendation among long-term expats because it refunds 100% of ATM fees worldwide, charges no foreign transaction fee, and requires no minimum balance — you open a linked brokerage account (no funding required) to qualify. It won't build credit or earn points, but it solves the "I need local cash in a country whose banking system doesn't know me yet" problem better than almost anything else available to U.S. citizens.

## Mid-tier travel cards: the workhorses

**Chase Sapphire Preferred** (roughly USD 95/year) is the default starting card for expats who still file and spend partly in USD: no foreign transaction fees, a straightforward points program (Chase Ultimate Rewards) that transfers to a wide airline and hotel partner network, and built-in trip cancellation and rental car insurance that matters more once you're regularly crossing borders. **Capital One Venture** (also around USD 95/year) is the simpler alternative — a flat 2x miles on every purchase with no bonus-category bookkeeping, which suits people who don't want to think about which card to use for which purchase category.

## Premium cards: when the fee pays for itself

**American Express Platinum** carries a steep annual fee (around USD 695) that only makes sense if you actually use what it buys: Priority Pass and Centurion Lounge access, elevated hotel status with Marriott and Hilton, and airport-specific credits. For an expat who flies internationally more than a handful of times a year, the lounge access alone — a quiet place to work during a long layover, free food and wifi — can offset the fee; for someone who flies twice a year, it almost never does the math. **Priority Pass**, the lounge network underlying many of these premium cards, now covers 1,400+ lounges globally, which is the real reason cardholders keep renewing rather than the card's other perks.

## Matching the card to your visa and residency status

One detail people overlook: a U.S.-issued card doesn't stop working just because you move, but it does start looking suspicious to fraud algorithms if your spending pattern shifts overnight to a new country. Call your issuer before departure, set a travel notice if your card still supports one, and keep a second card from a different network (Visa plus Mastercard) as a backup — a surprising number of card declines abroad are network-outage or merchant-acceptance issues, not account problems. If your relocation includes opening a local bank account, treat the international card as a bridge for your first three to six months, not a permanent solution, since local cards typically clear local transactions faster and avoid currency-conversion markup entirely.

## Building credit history that survives a move

One thing almost no card comparison mentions: your credit history doesn't automatically travel with you. A strong U.S. or UK credit score means nothing to a bank in Portugal, Mexico, or Singapore assessing you for a local mortgage or even a local credit card — most countries run entirely separate credit bureaus with no cross-border data sharing. The practical fix is to keep at least one home-country card open and lightly active even after you move (a small recurring subscription charged to it is enough), both to preserve that credit history for whenever you return, and because closing your oldest accounts right before or during a move can itself dent your score at the exact moment you might need it for a final home-country loan or lease guarantee. Separately, start building local credit as early as your visa status allows — a local secured card or a utility account paid consistently is often the fastest on-ramp to a country's credit system, and getting a head start in your first year saves friction when you eventually want local financing.
