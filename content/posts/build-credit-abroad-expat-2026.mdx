Credit history does not cross borders. A spotless 20-year record in your home country is invisible to a lender in your new one, which means most expats — regardless of income — start every new country as a thin or empty file, and the local scoring scale is not even the same shape as the one you left. The US runs **FICO**, 300–850, with "good" generally starting around **670**. The UK's Experian score runs 0–999, a completely different range that confuses people who assume a "700" means the same thing everywhere. Germany's **SCHUFA** score is a percentage, 0–100, where 100 is the cleanest band and anything below roughly 97–98% can already complicate a lease or phone contract, since German landlords and carriers check it routinely.

## Secured cards are still the fastest legal shortcut in the US

A secured credit card — where your own deposit becomes the credit line — is the most reliable way to open a US file from zero, typically requiring a **USD 200–500** deposit and reporting to all three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) within one to two billing cycles. Issuers like Discover, Capital One, and Chime's Credit Builder product accept applicants with no US Social Security history in some cases via an **ITIN** (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number), which you can apply for through the IRS alongside your first US tax filing. Six to twelve months of on-time payments on a secured card, kept under roughly 30% utilization, is usually enough to graduate to an unsecured card and a real score.

## Rental payments can become a tradeline if the landlord opts in

Programs under **Experian RentBureau** and similar rent-reporting services let on-time rent payments show up as a credit tradeline, but only when the landlord or property manager has enrolled — most individual landlords have not, so ask directly, or use a third-party rent-reporting service (several charge a small monthly fee) that will report on your behalf even if the landlord never signs up. This matters disproportionately for expats, since rent is often the single largest recurring payment you make in year one and would otherwise contribute nothing to your file.

## Cross-border credit portability exists, but only for a handful of corridors

**Nova Credit** is the most established cross-border credit-history translator, letting US issuers view credit history imported from a limited set of countries — historically including the UK, Australia, India, and a few others — so a newcomer from one of those markets can sometimes qualify for a mainstream card without a US file at all. Coverage is issuer-specific and has changed over time, so confirm with the card issuer directly whether your home country is currently supported before assuming this route is available; do not close home-country cards on the assumption that portability will substitute for them.

## Germany treats SCHUFA as a gatekeeper, not just a credit tool

Unlike the US, where a thin credit file mostly affects loan pricing, a weak or negative SCHUFA score in Germany can block a rental application or a mobile contract outright, since landlords and carriers request a SCHUFA-Bonitätsauskunft as standard practice. The fastest way to build a clean score as a new arrival is the boring path: open a German bank account (Girokonto) immediately, get a phone contract on a plan the provider actually approves rather than pushing for the cheapest one, and pay every bill on time — SCHUFA rewards a longer, dull payment history far more than any one large purchase.

## Do not let the old file go dark

If you plan to return to your home country eventually, keep at least one credit product open and lightly used there — a dormant card or account can be closed by the issuer after a year or two of inactivity, and rebuilding a second time is no easier than the first. A calendar reminder for one small recurring charge on a home-country card is a cheap insurance policy against having to start from zero twice.
