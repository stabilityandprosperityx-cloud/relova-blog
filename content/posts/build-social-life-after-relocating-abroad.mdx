Loneliness is not a personal failure after a move — it is a measured pattern. Barclays International Banking reports that **44%** of expats in **France** name loneliness as their main obstacle abroad. InterNations **Expat Insider 2026** finds that **40%** of expats globally say making local friends is hard; in bottom-ranked social environments such as **Norway**, that figure reaches **72%**. Globally only **54%** are satisfied with their social life; in Norway the share drops to **25%**.

## What the surveys actually show

Across destinations, **37%** of expats describe their circle as **mostly other expats**. Where locals are rated especially friendly and **73%** say making local friends is easy, about **34%** of expats report a **mostly local** circle — roughly double the **17%** global average for that description. Soft landing and local openness are not vibes; they show up as measurable network composition.

WeRoad’s **2026** study (**5,000+** respondents across Italy, Spain, France, Germany, and the UK) adds a sharper edge: **66%** say making new acquaintances feels harder than before (up from **58%** a year earlier). Yet **83%** say they are more open to meeting people while travelling or in a new setting than in old routines. Novelty helps — if you use it before the new city becomes another closed loop of work-from-home and grocery runs.

## What actually works (according to expat communities)

Skip the fantasy of a single “networking night.” Mechanisms that recur in successful stories:

- **Regular shared activity** — sports clubs, language exchanges, coworking memberships — beats one-off mixers because repetition creates natural follow-ups  
- **Community platforms** built for this problem — InterNations alone grew past **6+ million** members around expat connection  
- **Language effort** — in more than half of survey contexts, expats themselves name the language barrier as the #1 obstacle to local friendship; even basic local language unlocks invitations that English-only life never sees  

Expat-only circles are a valid bridge. They become a trap only when they permanently replace any local contact surface.

## Why the first months matter disproportionately

Loneliness does not decline on a smooth curve. People who fail to form local ties in the first **6–12 months** are less likely to build them later — early patterns calcify. That is the uncomfortable finding behind the cheerful advice: calendar social infrastructure while you are still booking wifi and residence appointments.

Treat the first season as a social installation window: one recurring weekly activity, one language habit, one community platform, and a bias toward showing up twice. The data will not make friends for you — but it does explain why waiting until “life settles” often means the settling never includes people.


Practical week-one installs: join one recurring activity before you finish unpacking, message two community-platform members with a specific invite (coffee after the language exchange), and schedule language practice three short sessions per week rather than a vague “I’ll study later.” Track social contacts like you track visa tasks for the first quarter — not forever, just until habits exist.

Also separate professional networking from friendship. Coworking acquaintances can become friends, but only if you add non-work context. Likewise, expat parents’ school-gate chat is a powerful channel for families — show up early and often during the first term when seating charts and playdates are still forming.


Measure progress with crude metrics for ninety days: conversations started, second meetings booked, local-language hours practiced. If all three stay near zero by month three, change the channel — different club, different neighbourhood cafe, different platform — rather than waiting for the city to become friendlier on its own.


Document decisions as you go: which visa category you chose and why, which temporary address you booked, which school waitlists you joined. Future-you will need that trail when a consulate, landlord, or insurer asks for a timeline. Relocation amnesia is real after the adrenaline fades — write the rationale while it is fresh, in the same folder as your scans.


Protect energy without isolating: one deep recurring commitment outperforms five shallow events you cancel. Pair social goals with existing errands — language cafe after grocery run, club training on the commute home — so friendship-building is not a separate unpaid second job on an already heavy relocation calendar. Small consistent contact beats rare spectacular nights out.
