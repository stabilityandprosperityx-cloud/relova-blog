Buying a vehicle abroad is rarely the hard part — the hard part is whether your license, and by extension your insurance, actually holds up the day something goes wrong. That question has a different answer in nearly every country, and it is worth settling before you sign anything at a dealership.

## Thailand: easy to buy, easy to get the license claim denied

A new Honda Click scooter — the default choice for most expats in Thailand — typically prices around **THB 55,000** (roughly $1,500), and buying one as a foreign resident is genuinely simple: a work permit or long-term visa and a Thai address are usually enough at most dealerships. The trap is insurance, not purchase. Riding on an International Driving Permit works for the first while you're on a tourist entry, but insurers increasingly expect a **Thai driving license** once you're a longer-term resident, and a claim after an accident can be reduced or denied outright if the insurer determines you should have converted and didn't. Get a Thai license — the process runs through the Department of Land Transport and typically requires a residence certificate — before you assume your policy will actually pay out.

## UAE: a defined conversion list, not a universal rule

The UAE runs one of the more structured systems in the region: **57 countries** are on the list eligible for **direct license conversion without a driving test**, including all five other GCC states (Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman) alongside most of the US, UK, EU, and a broad set of other nations. For anyone on that list, conversion through the Roads and Transport Authority is same-day, requiring your original license, passport, residence visa, and a recent eye test, with fees running roughly **AED 950–1,500**. There's no single hard deadline publicized for converting after residency starts, but the practical guidance is to do it as soon as your Emirates ID and residence visa are issued — driving on a foreign license without conversion becomes a genuine liability and insurance risk the longer residency continues, not just a paperwork inconvenience.

## The EU's exchange window is measured in months, not years

Most EU countries give a new resident a grace period, commonly **six to twelve months** depending on the specific country, to exchange a non-EU foreign license for a local one before the foreign license stops being valid for driving there at all. The exact window and process vary meaningfully by country — some EU states, and non-EU countries like the UK, run bilateral exchange agreements with specific non-EU countries that skip the local test entirely, while others require a full local driving exam if no such agreement exists with your country of licensing. This is one to verify country by country the moment residency status is confirmed, since missing the window can mean starting from a learner's permit rather than a straightforward paper exchange.

## Buying versus renting long-term

For anyone spending under a year somewhere, a long-term motorbike or car rental commonly beats ownership once you count registration, mandatory insurance, and resale hassle at departure — ownership only pencils out once you're confident you'll be in the country long enough to recoup those fixed costs. Georgia is a frequent exception worth knowing about: it's historically allowed foreign licenses to remain valid for driving for an unusually long stretch of residency compared to most countries, though this is exactly the kind of rule that is worth reconfirming directly with Georgia's Patrol Police Service before relying on it, since policy in this area shifts periodically across the region.
