Buying property in [Portugal](/blog/portugal-golden-visa-requirements-2026) follows a fixed legal sequence, and skipping a step — or hiring a lawyer who is also the seller's agent — is how foreign buyers end up with expensive surprises. Here's the process as it actually runs in 2026, cost by cost and step by step.

## Step one: the promissory contract and NIF

Before you can buy anything in [Portugal](/blog/portugal-golden-visa-fund-investment-2026), you need a **NIF** (Número de Identificação Fiscal), the Portuguese tax number, which you can get through a Portuguese consulate, in person at a Finanças office, or via a fiscal representative if you're not yet resident. Once you've chosen a property, the standard next step is the **Contrato de Promessa de Compra e Venda (CPCV)** — a promissory purchase agreement — signed with a deposit, typically 10–30% of the purchase price. This contract is legally binding: if the buyer walks away, the deposit is usually forfeited; if the seller backs out, they typically owe double the deposit back. This is the point where hiring an independent lawyer (not one recommended by the selling agent) to review title, check for liens, and confirm the property's legal status pays for itself.

## Step two: the taxes due at completion

Two taxes fall due before or at the final deed. **IMT (Imposto Municipal sobre Transmissões)**, the property transfer tax, is progressive and depends on the property's value, type, and whether it's a primary or secondary residence — rates commonly range from 0% on lower-value primary homes up to around 8% at the top end for higher-value or non-primary purchases, with a flat higher rate applied to property bought through offshore-registered companies. **Stamp duty (Imposto do Selo)** adds a flat 0.8% of the purchase price, payable on the deed. Both are calculated on whichever is higher: the declared purchase price or the property's official tax value (VPT).

## Step three: the escritura (final deed)

The **escritura pública de compra e venda** is signed in front of a notary (or, increasingly, at a Casa Pronta one-stop office that bundles notary, land registry, and tax payment into a single appointment). Notary and registration costs typically add roughly EUR 700–1,500 depending on the property and complexity, on top of the IMT and stamp duty already paid. After signing, the property is registered at the Conservatória do Registo Predial (land registry) in the buyer's name — this registration, not the signing itself, is what makes ownership legally complete and searchable.

## Financing as a non-resident

Non-resident buyers can get mortgages from Portuguese banks, but terms are tighter than for residents: loan-to-value ratios commonly cap around 60–70% of the property's appraised value (versus up to 80–90% for residents), meaning a larger cash down payment, and banks will want proof of stable foreign income, tax returns, and sometimes a local bank account already open before approving. Rates and terms vary enough between banks (Millennium BCP, Santander Totta, and Novobanco are common starting points for foreign applicants) that most non-resident buyers use a mortgage broker who works specifically with foreign clients rather than shopping banks individually.

## Due diligence before you sign anything

Beyond the standard legal review, a handful of Portugal-specific checks are worth insisting on. Confirm the property's **licença de utilização** (usage license), which certifies the building is legally registered for the use you intend — a surprising number of older or rural Portuguese properties were built or extended without full permitting, which can complicate resale or renovation later. Check the **caderneta predial** (the tax registration record) matches the actual property boundaries and description in the land registry; discrepancies between the two are common in older properties and rural land in particular, and can stall a sale at the notary stage if not resolved beforehand. Finally, if buying in a building with shared areas, ask for the condominium's recent meeting minutes and any pending special assessments (*quotas extraordinárias*) — a beautifully renovated apartment can come with an inherited bill for a roof or elevator repair the previous owner voted for but never disclosed.
