**Cape Town vs [Nairobi](/blog/move-to-nairobi-kenya-guide-2026) vs [Accra](/blog/move-to-accra-guide-2026) expats 2026** are three genuinely different bets on Africa: Cape Town for lifestyle and scenery with a real infrastructure catch, Nairobi for tech-sector energy and a fast-maturing remote-work visa route, and Accra for political stability and a lower cost floor. None is a clear universal winner — the right pick depends heavily on what you're optimizing for.

## Cape Town: mountain views with an infrastructure asterisk

Cape Town's rental market runs ZAR 12,000–20,000 for a one-bedroom (roughly $650–1,100), which buys genuinely stunning surroundings — Table Mountain, the Atlantic seaboard, a wine region within an hour's drive — at a fraction of what comparable scenery costs in Europe. The catch that every serious Cape Town guide has to mention is load-shedding: scheduled rolling power cuts that, while less severe than their 2022–2023 peak, still recur periodically and mean anyone working remotely needs to budget for a UPS or backup power setup as a genuine line item, not an afterthought. Fiber internet in Cape Town is otherwise excellent and among the best in Africa, so the constraint is really about power continuity rather than connectivity itself.

## Nairobi: Silicon Savannah and a new visa route

Nairobi's rental stock runs KES 60,000–100,000 for a one-bedroom (roughly $460–770) and pairs with the city's reputation as East Africa's tech hub — "Silicon Savannah" isn't just marketing, it reflects a genuinely dense concentration of startups, accelerators, and multinational tech offices that makes Nairobi one of the more career-relevant African bases for anyone in the industry. Kenya has also introduced a digital nomad visa (Class N permit), though as of this writing the government has not finalized and gazetted the specific minimum income threshold — early reporting cited figures anywhere from roughly $1,000 a month to considerably higher annual thresholds, so treat any specific number you see, including in this article's prior version, as provisional until Kenyan immigration publishes the finalized regulation.

## Accra: the stability play

Accra's rents are the most affordable of the three at GHS 3,000–6,000 for a one-bedroom (roughly $200–400), and the city's appeal rests heavily on Ghana's reputation as one of West Africa's most politically stable democracies — a meaningful factor for anyone weighing a longer-term base or planning to set up a regional business entity rather than just a personal relocation. Ghana's investment-based residency route has historically required minimum capital in the low hundreds of thousands of dollars depending on whether the business is wholly foreign-owned or a joint venture with a Ghanaian partner, though Ghana has signaled it may simplify or reduce these thresholds as part of ongoing investment-law reform — worth confirming current figures directly with Ghana's Investment Promotion Centre before planning around any specific number, since this is an area actively in flux.

## Weighing the three against each other

If you're choosing purely on rent, Accra wins outright and Nairobi comes in second, with Cape Town the most expensive of the three despite still being cheap by Western European or North American standards. If you're choosing on remote-work career relevance, Nairobi's tech ecosystem gives it an edge that neither Cape Town nor Accra currently matches, even accounting for Cape Town's own growing startup scene. If you're choosing on day-to-day ease and scenery, Cape Town remains the most immediately liveable for someone used to Western infrastructure, provided you budget properly for power backup. And if long-term political and regulatory predictability is your main filter — particularly relevant if you're setting up a business rather than just relocating personally — Ghana's reputation for stability gives Accra a real edge that's worth more than its lower rent alone suggests. None of these trade-offs are hypothetical: talk to people already living in each city before committing, since regional realities shift faster than published guides can keep up with.
