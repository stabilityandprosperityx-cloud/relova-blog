"Cheapest in Europe" covers a wide spread — a single-person monthly budget under EUR 1,500 is realistic across the Balkans and the Caucasus, but the gap between the cheapest of these cities and the most expensive on this list is still several hundred euros a month, and visa access differs sharply by country too.

## The Caucasus outlier: Tbilisi, [Georgia](/blog/how-to-move-to-georgia-country)

Tbilisi is arguably the cheapest capital on this list with genuine big-city infrastructure: a realistic single-person monthly budget covering a one-bedroom rental, food, and transport commonly runs EUR 700-1,200. Georgia is not an EU member and sits outside Schengen, but it has historically offered one of the most generous visa-free stays in the region for many Western passports (commonly cited around a full year, though this has been adjusted periodically, so confirm current terms for your passport before assuming a full-year stay). Georgia also markets its own remote-worker and individual-entrepreneur tax registration options, which is worth researching separately if freelance income is involved.

## The Western Balkans: Tirana and Belgrade

Tirana, Albania's capital, runs a monthly budget in the EUR 700-1,100 range for a single person, and Albania offers visa-free entry to many nationalities for extended tourist stays alongside a Type D visa and Unique Permit route for longer-term residence (see our dedicated Albania guide for the specifics). Belgrade, Serbia's capital, runs slightly higher at roughly EUR 800-1,300 monthly, reflecting its larger economy and denser expat and digital-nomad scene; Serbia is also outside the EU and Schengen but grants visa-free entry to a broad range of passports for tourist stays, with residence permits available through employment, business registration, or property ownership.

## North Macedonia: the cheapest of the group

Skopje, North Macedonia's capital, tends to undercut even Tirana, with realistic monthly budgets commonly landing around EUR 600-900 — the lowest on this list. North Macedonia is an EU candidate country, not a member, and like its neighbors sits outside Schengen, so plan visa and residence logistics separately from any "EU access" assumption.

## The EU-member comparison: Budapest and Kraków

For readers who want EU and (in Kraków's case, Schengen) membership without Western European prices, Budapest and Kraków sit at the higher end of this "cheap Europe" list, with realistic monthly budgets commonly in the EUR 1,000-1,500 range — still well under London, Paris, or Amsterdam levels, but meaningfully pricier than the Balkans or Caucasus options above. Both cities benefit from EU-wide freedom of movement for EU/EEA citizens and established (if separately regulated) work-permit routes for non-EU nationals.

## What holds these cities together

All five hubs listed here have reasonably reliable high-speed internet in their central neighborhoods (100 Mbps+ fiber or cable is common, though quality varies by building and provider), a real coworking and cafe culture, and enough of an existing remote-worker or expat community that finding furnished short-term rentals on arrival isn't a struggle. None of that substitutes for confirming your specific visa route before booking a one-way ticket, since "cheap" and "easy to move to" are two different questions.

## What "cheap" actually includes, and what it doesn't

These budgets generally cover rent for a modest one-bedroom, groceries, local transport, and everyday incidentals for a single person living reasonably but not extravagantly — they typically don't include private health insurance (worth budgeting EUR 50-150+ a month depending on age and coverage level in most of these markets), visa or residence permit fees, or the upfront costs of furnishing an apartment and covering a security deposit, which can add the equivalent of two to three months' rent to your first month in any of these cities. Dining out, imported goods, and anything priced in euros in a non-euro economy (common in Tbilisi, Tirana, Belgrade, and Skopje, where hotels and some rentals quote in EUR even though the local currency is different) will also run noticeably higher than the local-price baseline.

## A note on healthcare and safety nets

None of these five cities offer the kind of automatic public healthcare access that many arrivals are used to from home unless you become a contributing resident with local employment or a specific visa category that includes it — private international health insurance is close to a universal recommendation for expats in all five markets, and it's worth pricing that into your "cheap" budget rather than treating it as optional. Emergency and specialist care quality varies more between these cities than the rent figures might suggest, with Budapest and Kraków generally offering more developed private healthcare infrastructure than the smaller Balkan and Caucasus capitals, which is a real trade-off against their higher living costs.
