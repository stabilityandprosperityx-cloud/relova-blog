**Cost of living Europe 2026** varies so widely across the continent that a single "Europe" budget figure is close to meaningless — the real gap between a one-bedroom in Zurich and one in Tbilisi is roughly tenfold, and that spread shapes almost every other relocation decision that follows it.

## The expensive tier: Zurich, London, and Amsterdam

Zurich sits at the top of nearly every European cost ranking, with a central one-bedroom typically running CHF 2,500–3,500 a month (roughly $2,800–3,900) — a figure that reflects Switzerland's high wages as much as its high costs, since salaries there are proportionally elevated too. London runs £1,800–2,800 for a comparable flat (about $2,300–3,550), with the range depending heavily on borough: Zone 1 central boroughs sit at the top of that band, while outer zones with a longer commute bring real savings. Amsterdam lands at €1,800–2,500 (about $1,950–2,700), driven by a persistently tight rental market and, for non-EU arrivals, the added friction of needing Dutch payslips to access much of the private rental stock at all.

## The mid tier: Paris, Prague, and Athens

Paris runs €1,400–2,200 for a one-bedroom (about $1,500–2,400), with substantial variation by arrondissement — central districts command a real premium over the outer ring, and rent control policies mean listed prices don't always reflect what long-term tenants actually pay. Prague offers a noticeably gentler mid-tier option at €900–1,350 (about $970–1,450), combining EU membership and Schengen access with costs well below Western Europe's major capitals, which has made it an increasingly popular base for remote workers who want EU-adjacent convenience without London or Amsterdam pricing. Athens sits similarly at €700–1,200 (about $750–1,300), reflecting Greece's generally lower cost base relative to northern Europe, even as its digital nomad visa income requirements (around €3,500 a month) assume a considerably higher earning level than the rent alone would suggest.

## The budget tier: Budapest and Tbilisi

Budapest runs €500–800 for a one-bedroom (about $540–860), delivering some of the best cost-to-infrastructure value in Europe — reliable internet, a well-developed café and coworking culture, and a genuinely walkable historic core, all at a fraction of Western European prices. Tbilisi, technically outside the EU but a frequent point of comparison for cost-focused movers, runs even lower at roughly $350–600, helped by Georgia's exceptionally open visa policy that lets most nationalities stay visa-free for up to a year.

## Building a realistic budget beyond rent

Rent is only the anchor of a real monthly budget, and food costs alone vary enormously by city — a grocery-heavy diet might run €150 a month in Tbilisi or Budapest but push past €500 in Zurich or London for the same shopping list, before accounting for how often you eat out. The practical approach to budgeting a European move is building three separate models — lean, realistic, and stress-case — for your specific target city rather than relying on any single averaged number, and keeping a 15–25% contingency on top for the setup costs (deposits, agency fees, initial furnishing) that inevitably run higher than the spreadsheet estimate.

## Transit and utilities add up differently by city

Public transport and utilities are the other line items that shift the real cost picture more than people expect. A monthly transit pass runs close to CHF 90 in Zurich and around £180 for a Zone 1–2 Travelcard in London, both real costs on top of already-high rent, while Prague and Budapest offer citywide monthly passes for the equivalent of roughly $25–35 — cheap enough that some residents skip owning a car entirely without feeling the trade-off. Utilities (electricity, heating, water, internet) also vary by climate and building age as much as by country: older, poorly insulated housing stock in cities like Prague or Athens can mean higher winter heating bills than the base rent difference would suggest, so it's worth asking specifically about a unit's insulation and heating system rather than assuming lower rent automatically means a lower total monthly cost.
