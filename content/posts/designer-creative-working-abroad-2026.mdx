Immigration systems are built around employment contracts and salary slips, which makes them a poor fit for how designers, illustrators, and other creatives actually get paid — a mix of project invoices, retainers, and royalties that rarely looks like a single payslip. The visa routes that work best for this profile are the ones that were explicitly designed around self-employment rather than adapted from an employee-sponsorship template, and the paperwork they ask for reflects that difference directly.

## Freiberufler paperwork: client letters over payslips

Germany's **freelance residence permit** (the Freiberufler route, which also covers artists and language teachers under near-identical rules) is the clearest example of an immigration system built for creative self-employment. Rather than a fixed salary threshold, [Berlin](/blog/berlin-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026)'s immigration office (LEA) wants a **revenue forecast (Ertragsvorschau)** projecting your income and expenses, plus **at least two letters of intent** from prospective clients describing the scope and expected compensation of upcoming work — essentially, proof of a real pipeline rather than proof of past income. Berlin-specific guidance also expects net income to comfortably clear rent, health insurance, and roughly **€563 a month** in additional living costs; savings of **€3,000–5,000** are commonly cited as reassurance funds in Berlin specifically (other German cities often expect closer to **€10,000**). The application fee is a modest **€100**, but processing runs anywhere from **4 weeks to 5 months** depending on the office's backlog and how complete your file is on first submission — and the permit, once granted, is typically valid for up to **three years**.

## Rate math across three very different markets

UX and product design freelance rates on strong international marketplaces commonly run **USD 50–120 an hour**, but that range means something completely different depending on where you're billing from and where your clients sit. A designer billing US or Western European clients while based in Berlin, Lisbon, or [Bali](/blog/moving-to-bali-guide-2026) is effectively capturing developed-market rates against a lower cost base — the entire arbitrage that makes location-independent creative work attractive in the first place. That arithmetic collapses the moment you start taking on local clients paying local rates, which is worth remembering before assuming your visa country's cost of living automatically translates into comparable local earning power if your international client pipeline ever dries up.

## Bali's E33G: real access, hard ceiling

Indonesia's **E33G remote-worker visa** — the main legal route for creatives basing themselves in Bali specifically — requires **USD 60,000 in annual foreign-sourced income** (roughly USD 5,000 a month) and grants a **one-year stay that is explicitly non-extendable**: when it expires, you leave and reapply from scratch rather than renewing in-country. There's a tax wrinkle worth flagging clearly: Indonesia can treat E33G status itself as evidence of intent to reside, which may trigger Indonesian tax residency regardless of your actual day count in the country — meaning holders should generally register for an **NPWP** (Indonesian tax ID) and file annual returns, using foreign tax credits where applicable, rather than assuming the visa is tax-neutral by default. This is genuinely worth a consultation with an Indonesia-specific tax adviser before you commit to a full year on the island; the interaction between residency-by-intent rules and your home country's tax treaty is not something to guess at.

## Portfolio visas want proof, not promises

Across every creative-friendly visa route — Germany's Freiberufler permit, Portugal's D7/D8 tracks, or Bali's E33G — the common thread is that immigration officers want documented evidence, not aspirational statements. A polished portfolio matters for landing clients, but for the visa file itself, what actually moves an application forward is signed client letters, existing invoices, bank statements showing a consistent income pattern, and (for Berlin specifically) a coherent, numbers-based revenue forecast rather than a mood board of future ambitions. Build that document trail for several months before you apply, not the week before — a thin, last-minute paper trail is the single most common reason creative-visa applications stall or get rejected outright.
