**Digital nomad taxes** confuse people because travel feels borderless while tax systems are stubbornly local. The most common mistake is treating the **183-day rule** as a universal safe harbor. It isn't. Most countries do use roughly six months of physical presence as one test for tax residency, but it is a heuristic, not a global statute — and even where it applies, tie-breaker rules under double-tax treaties look past the day count to where your permanent home is, where your family lives, and where your "center of vital interests" sits. A nomad who spends four months each in three countries can still end up tax-resident somewhere, usually their last country of prior residence, simply because they never established a cleaner claim elsewhere.

Georgia is one of the most cited "low-tax base" options because of its Individual Entrepreneur small-business status, which taxes qualifying turnover at just **1%** up to roughly **GEL 500,000** in annual turnover for the small-business regime (older summaries citing a ~100,000 GEL ceiling for a related but distinct micro-business status persist online, so it's worth checking the current thresholds on the Revenue Service site or with a local accountant before assuming a number). The catch is that this status taxes Georgian-registered entrepreneurial income; it doesn't by itself establish where you are personally tax-resident for other purposes, and most nationalities still need to think about their home country's exit rules separately.

The UAE remains the highest-profile **0% personal income tax** jurisdiction, and that headline is accurate for salaries and most personal income. What the 0% figure hides is the cost of getting there: freezone or mainland company setup fees, mandatory health insurance, and — if you want a residency visa rather than just tourist entry — either employment sponsorship, a freelance permit, or the Virtual Working Programme, which itself now requires six months of bank statements as of a 27 January 2026 tightening. Corporate tax also exists in the UAE for many business structures at 9% above a profit threshold, so "the UAE is tax-free" is really shorthand for "no personal income tax," not "no tax at all."

Panama runs a **territorial tax system**: income earned outside Panama is generally not taxed by Panama, which is why it appears on so many nomad shortlists. But territorial taxation is not the same as automatic residency — Panama's Friendly Nations Visa and other permits have their own income and deposit requirements, and simply being physically present doesn't grant you the territorial benefit without formal tax residency status. Paraguay is frequently mentioned for a flat **10%** rate framing, though the practical reality (what counts as Paraguayan-source income, and how strictly it is enforced for nomads who barely touch the country) is less battle-tested than Panama's system, so treat any flat-rate claim about Paraguay as a starting point for research rather than a settled fact.

One area that trips up EU-facing freelancers specifically is **VAT on digital services sold to consumers (B2C)**. Since the EU's VAT rules for electronically supplied services took effect, sellers generally must charge VAT at the rate of the buyer's country of residence, not the seller's — which means a nomad based in Georgia selling a course to a customer in Germany may still owe German VAT registration or use the EU's One-Stop-Shop mechanism, depending on structure. This is one of the more commonly missed details because it has nothing to do with personal residency at all; it's a transaction-level rule tied to where the customer sits.

The practical approach for most digital nomads is to pick one country to anchor formal tax residency in — ideally one with a low or territorial regime and a straightforward path to a tax residency certificate — rather than trying to stay "residency-free," which usually just means your home country claims you by default. Keep a dated log of days in each country, retain lease agreements or utility bills as evidence of your anchor country, and get a real accountant in that jurisdiction before you file anything. None of the rates above are guarantees; they are starting points that change with each country's budget cycle, and Georgia, the UAE, Panama, and Paraguay have all adjusted thresholds or enforcement in the last two years.
