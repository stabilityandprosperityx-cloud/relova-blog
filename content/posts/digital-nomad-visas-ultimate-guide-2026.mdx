## Not All "Digital Nomad Visas" Are the Same Product

More than seventy jurisdictions now market a digital nomad or remote-work visa, but the marketing outruns the reality. Some of these "visas" are simple tourist-visa extensions with a new label; others are genuine multi-year tax residencies with family reunification rights. The income floor alone varies by a factor of forty across programs, which tells you these are not interchangeable products — they're solving different problems for different governments.

## The Low-Barrier Options: Montenegro, Serbia, and Bali

At the accessible end, **Montenegro's** digital nomad residence permit asks for roughly **€800 a month** in demonstrable income and grants stays of up to two years, making it one of the cheapest entry points into a genuine (if non-EU) residence permit in Europe. **Serbia** goes even further on friction: its program carries a comparatively small application fee — commentary online often cites a figure near €90 — and a fast, low-bureaucracy process, though Serbia's permit does not itself carry EU mobility. On the Southeast Asia side, **Bali's E33G** remote-worker visa markets stays of up to five years against an income test of roughly **$2,000 a month**, a relatively low bar for a program with that duration.

## Portugal's D8 and Estonia's EU-Adjacent Routes

**Portugal's D8** sits in the middle: renewable, income-tested at roughly **€3,480 a month** (about four times Portugal's minimum wage, which is how the government benchmarks it), and — critically — it is a genuine residence permit that counts toward Portugal's five-year path to permanent residency and citizenship, something the Bali or Serbia programs don't offer. **Estonia's** [digital nomad visa](/blog/digital-nomad-visa-guide) is EU-adjacent in spirit but issued nationally, with income requirements that are periodically revised, so treat any figure you see as a floor to verify rather than a fixed number for 2026.

## High-Income Programs: South Korea and the UAE

At the top of the range, **South Korea's** F-1-D [digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-albania-guide-2026) requires an income level around **$84,000 a year**, roughly double the country's average salary, which effectively restricts the program to senior remote employees and established freelancers rather than early-career nomads. The **UAE's** remote-work options run differently again: rather than a flat income figure, the country offers sponsor-based structures with terms from one to ten years depending on the specific visa category (employment sponsorship, Golden Visa, or the Virtual Working Programme), and the UAE charges **0% personal income tax** regardless of which route you use.

## Georgia: No Formal Visa, Minimal Friction

**Georgia** is the outlier worth flagging separately: it doesn't operate a formal "digital nomad visa" at all for most passports. Instead, citizens of roughly 90-plus countries simply get up to a year of visa-free entry, and anyone earning remotely can register as an Individual Entrepreneur to access Georgia's 1% small-business tax rate on qualifying turnover. That combination — no visa application, minimal tax, no income floor to prove upfront — is why Georgia routinely tops "easiest country to try nomad life in" lists, even without a dedicated visa product.

## What a Visa Actually Converts Into

The number that matters more than any headline income floor is what the visa actually converts into. A program that's really an extended tourist stay (no path to permanent residency, no ability to bring dependents, no local tax registration) is a different product from one that functions as genuine long-term residency. Before comparing sticker fees, check three things for any program you're evaluating: whether time on the visa counts toward permanent residency, whether you can sponsor a spouse or children under the same permit, and whether holding the visa creates a corporate tax nexus for your home-based employer or client relationships — a detail many remote employees don't realize their company needs to worry about until HR flags it.

## Processing Times and Building a Buffer

Processing time is another dimension that gets glossed over in marketing but matters enormously in practice. Some programs, particularly Serbia's and Georgia's non-visa route, can be sorted in a matter of weeks. Others, especially those routed through consulates that process a high volume of applications or require in-country biometrics appointments, can stretch to several months — long enough that you need a bridging plan (extended tourist stay, a different interim country) rather than assuming you can simply fly out the moment your old lease ends. It's worth building a buffer of at least one to two months into any relocation timeline built around a digital nomad visa, regardless of which country you're targeting.
