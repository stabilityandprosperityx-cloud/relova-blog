[Dubai](/blog/dubai-vs-singapore-expat-guide-2026) remains a default Gulf base for British professionals chasing a tax-free salary and English-language administration, and the British community there is large enough to be its own ecosystem — commonly estimated at over **100,000 people** across the emirates, with clubs, schools, and church and cultural groups built around it. But the UK-specific parts of this move sit almost entirely on the tax and pension side, not the visa side, since [Dubai](/blog/move-to-dubai-freelancer-guide-2026)'s employment and freelance visa routes work the same for a British passport as for most other Western nationalities.

The headline draw is real: the UAE charges **0% personal income tax**. What catches people out is assuming that moving physically to [Dubai](/blog/move-to-dubai-guide-2026) automatically switches off UK tax liability. It doesn't — HMRC only treats you as non-resident once you pass its **Statutory Residence Test (SRT)**, a multi-factor test that weighs days spent in the UK, the number and strength of your UK "ties" (family, accommodation, work), and whether you have a full-time job abroad. Get this wrong and HMRC can still tax UK-source income, or in some cases your worldwide income, even while you're drawing a tax-free [Dubai](/blog/move-to-abu-dhabi-guide-2026) salary. The safest approach is to track every UK day from the date you leave, keep evidence of your Dubai tenancy and employment contract, and run the SRT calculation properly rather than assuming "I moved abroad" is sufficient on its own.

**National Insurance** is the detail most UK expats underweight. Once you stop UK employment, you can keep your NI record alive with **voluntary Class 2 contributions**, priced at around **£3.45 a week** — a genuinely cheap way to protect your entitlement to the UK State Pension and other contributory benefits. Skipping this for a few years abroad is a common, quietly expensive mistake, since gaps in your NI record reduce your eventual pension regardless of how much you're earning tax-free in Dubai. On the pension side itself, the UK State Pension is paid to UAE bank accounts, but — unlike pensioners in EU countries or places with a reciprocal uprating agreement — payments to the UAE are **not automatically uprated** each year in line with inflation or wage growth, so the real value of a frozen UK pension erodes over time if you stay long-term. This is worth factoring into any retirement-in-Dubai plan, and it's a materially different situation from, say, a UK pensioner in an EU country under current arrangements.

On schooling, British-curriculum international schools are widely available in Dubai and typically charge **AED 40,000–80,000 a year** per child, a wide range driven by whether the school follows the full English National Curriculum through to A-Levels or a hybrid IB/British offering, and by its reputation and waitlist length — the better-regarded schools fill up a year or more in advance, so this is worth sorting before you finalize a moving date, not after.

Health insurance is mandatory for every Dubai resident visa and runs roughly **$200–500 a month** for comprehensive private cover, a cost that scales with age, pre-existing conditions, and whether you want coverage that includes maternity or dental. The NHS is not available to non-resident Britons living in Dubai, so this insurance is not optional in the way it might feel for someone used to UK healthcare — treat it as a fixed cost of the move, and make sure any policy you buy explicitly lists the UAE as a covered territory.

Visa mechanics themselves — employer sponsorship, freelance freezone permits, or the Virtual Working Programme for remote employees of UK companies — are identical regardless of nationality, so for the visa process itself, the general Dubai guides below cover the ground; this piece focuses on what's genuinely different because you're coming from the UK.
