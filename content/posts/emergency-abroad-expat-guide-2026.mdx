Emergencies abroad are less about heroics and more about pre-loaded systems. This is a practical action guide: what to set up **before you leave**, what to do **during an incident**, and how to handle **special cases** when weather, conflict, arrest, or lost documents enter the story.

## Before you leave: registration, insurance, documents

If you are a US citizen, enrol in **STEP (Smart Traveler Enrollment Program)** — the State Department’s free registration for trips and longer stays. Enrolment lets the nearest embassy send security alerts and reach you in a crisis. In large evacuations after disasters or conflict, STEP registrants are the people who get priority information about charter evacuation flights. Non-US readers should find their foreign ministry’s equivalent consular registration; most countries run a similar tool under a different name.

US embassies and consulates help roughly **200,000 Americans a year** who face crime, accidents, illness, or families trying to locate them. That volume exists because people prepare unevenly. Close the gap on your side:

- Store digital and paper copies of your passport **separately** from the original. Carry a copy day-to-day; leave the original in a hotel safe when local rules allow.
- Photograph insurance cards, prescriptions, and emergency contacts.
- Save local emergency numbers before landing — not every country uses **911**. Much of the EU, Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland use **112**. New Zealand uses **111**. Singapore splits **999** (police) and **995** (ambulance).
- Buy travel medical cover that matches your activities, then decide whether you also need **medical evacuation insurance** (Medjet-style products). Evacuation cover is not the same as standard travel insurance: it pays for transport to an adequate hospital or home, not only treatment on site. Ordinary policies can leave you stranded after “medically necessary” care in a facility you would never choose for surgery.

Pack a small “incident kit”: spare glasses or contacts, a week of critical meds, and a laminated card with blood type and allergies in English plus the local language if you can.

## During an incident: what embassies can and cannot do

When something goes wrong, call local emergency services first if life or limb is at risk. Then contact your embassy or consulate for Americans (or your country’s mission). Consular officers can help you replace a passport, contact family, provide lists of local lawyers and doctors, and visit you in detention in many cases. They can also coordinate information during wider crises.

They **cannot** override local law. Abroad, you are subject to the host country’s legal system. An embassy cannot “get you out” of arrest, force a hospital to discharge you, pay your bills as a matter of right, or act as your private investigator. Expect information, referrals, and advocacy within narrow rules — not extraterritorial rescue.

Practical sequence for common incidents:

1. Stabilise safety (leave the scene, call **112/111/999** as relevant).
2. Notify your insurer’s 24/7 line before non-emergency procedures when your policy requires pre-authorisation.
3. Email or call the embassy with location, passport number, and a concise fact summary.
4. Loop in a trusted contact at home who can receive updates if your phone dies.

Keep communications factual. Panic essays slow help.

## Special cases: disaster, arrest, lost documents

**Natural disaster or conflict.** If you registered in STEP (or your national equivalent), watch official channels for assembly points and evacuation options. Do not rely on social media rumours for flight lists. Move toward designated safe areas only when authorities or your mission instruct it.

**Arrest or detention.** Ask to contact your consulate. Do not sign documents you do not understand. Request a translator. Consular staff can check on your welfare and help you find counsel; they will not act as your defence lawyer.

**Lost or stolen passport.** File a local police report when required for replacement, then start an emergency passport appointment with your mission. Your photocopy and digital scan shorten that process dramatically. Freeze cards through your bank app immediately; theft often pairs with card fraud within hours.

**Medical crisis far from quality care.** This is where evacuation insurance earns its premium. Standard travel cover may pay clinic bills; it may not move you to a trauma centre you trust. Know which product you bought before the stretcher arrives.

Build the stack once: registration, copies, local numbers, medical plus evacuation cover, and a home contact who knows your itinerary. Emergencies punish improvisation; they reward boring preparation.
