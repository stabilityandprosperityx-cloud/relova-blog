Expat banking is rarely one app that does everything, and the people who get burned are usually the ones who assume it is. Most experienced expats end up running a small stack: **Wise** for multi-currency transfers and holding balances in several currencies at once, a **local bank** for rent and payroll, and — for anything large, like a property deposit — a specialist FX broker rather than either of the above. The first thing to understand about any of these products is whether it's a bank with deposit insurance or an e-money institution (EMI) that isn't, because that distinction determines what happens to your money if the provider fails.

**Wise** holds balances in over 40 currencies and can give you local account details — a UK sort code, a EU IBAN, a US ACH routing number — in more than eight of them, which is genuinely useful for receiving salary or client payments like a local. Its currency conversion fees typically run **0.33–0.67%** off the mid-market rate with no markup layered on top and no monthly account fee, which makes it one of the cheaper ways to move money internationally. The important caveat: Wise is an EMI, not a bank. Customer funds are legally safeguarded in ring-fenced accounts, but they are **not** covered by a national deposit-insurance scheme the way a bank account is, so treat a large Wise balance more like cash-in-transit than a savings account.

**Revolut** is more complicated because its protections depend on which product tier and which legal entity holds your money. On the free tier, fee-free currency conversion is capped at roughly **£1,000 a month**; beyond that you're typically looking at around **1% extra**, plus a further roughly 1% weekend markup on free-tier conversions. Where it matters more: in the UK and parts of the EU, Revolut now operates through licensed banking entities. UK deposits became eligible for **FSCS protection up to £120,000** following its move to a full UK banking licence around late 2025, while EU balances held via its Lithuanian banking entity are covered up to **€100,000** under that country's deposit guarantee scheme. Not every Revolut product or region carries this protection, so check which entity actually holds your specific balance.

**N26** is a German neobank offering a genuine **German IBAN**, which solves a real problem: many European landlords and employers simply refuse to accept a foreign IBAN for rent or payroll, and N26's German details sidestep that friction anywhere in the eurozone. It comes with a hard limitation for British expats specifically — N26 withdrew from the UK market after Brexit and is not available to UK residents.

The stack that works for most people combines Wise for international transfers and holding multi-currency balances with a local bank account in the host country for domestic rails — rent, salary deposits, and utility direct debits. Local banks often carry a noticeably worse FX markup, commonly **1.5–3%**, so use them for domestic transactions rather than converting savings through them.

For anything larger — a property purchase, an inheritance transfer, or any one-off remittance above roughly **$10,000** — dedicated FX brokers such as OFX, TorFX, or Currencies Direct frequently beat both retail banks and consumer fintech apps on the exchange rate, since their spreads are built for large one-off tickets rather than everyday small transfers. US citizens carry an extra compliance layer regardless of which of these providers they use: once the aggregate value of foreign financial accounts exceeds **$10,000** at any point in the year, **FBAR** reporting to the US Treasury becomes mandatory, and platforms like Wise and Revolut report account information under **FATCA**. This isn't optional paperwork — treat any foreign-held balance as visible to the IRS by default and plan filings accordingly rather than assuming digital money is invisible.
