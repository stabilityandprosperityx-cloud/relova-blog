Divorcing across borders is a jurisdiction problem before it is an emotional one. Where you file, and whether that court has authority over your children and your assets, usually matters more than which country "feels" fair, and getting the order of operations wrong can cost months and thousands of dollars in duplicate filings.

## Which country's courts actually have jurisdiction

Inside the EU, jurisdiction for divorce is no longer governed by the old Brussels IIa regulation — it was replaced by Brussels IIb (Council Regulation (EU) 2019/1111), which has applied since 1 August 2022. Brussels IIb still uses habitual residence, not nationality or where you married, as the primary test. Courts in a member state can typically hear a divorce if both spouses are habitually resident there, if the couple last lived there together and one spouse still does, or if the filing spouse has lived there for at least a year (six months if that spouse is a national of that state or, for UK/Irish/Danish nationals in the applicable member state, "domiciled" there under that state's law). These grounds are exclusive — a state cannot invent a different basis once one of the listed grounds applies to another member state. Outside the EU, jurisdiction rules vary by country and often turn on residence duration (commonly 6–12 months) or, for some US states, as little as 90 days for one spouse.

## Custody and child relocation: the Hague framework

If children are involved and one parent moves them across a border without the other's consent, the 1980 Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction governs the return process, provided both countries are signatories — the treaty currently has just over 100 contracting states, and coverage is not universal (large parts of the Gulf, much of East Asia, and several African states are not parties). A Hague return petition asks the court in the country the child was taken *to* to send the child back to their prior habitual residence so custody can be decided there — it does not decide custody itself. These cases move faster than ordinary custody litigation (the Convention calls for a six-week target, though it is frequently missed), but they still typically require a local family lawyer, sometimes a guardian ad litem, and proof of the child's habitual residence before the move.

## Splitting assets when they sit in different countries

Cross-border asset division adds a layer most single-country divorces never face: a court can only directly order the division of property inside its own jurisdiction, and enforcing a judgment against a foreign bank account, pension, or property title usually requires a separate recognition proceeding in that country. Real estate is generally governed by the law of the country where it sits (lex rei sitae), regardless of where the divorce is filed, which is why a UK court's ruling on a French apartment often needs a French notaire to actually execute the transfer. Pensions are their own headache — a US 401(k) or UK pension typically needs a specific domestic order (a QDRO in the US, a pension sharing order in the UK) that a foreign divorce decree alone won't trigger. Combined legal and appraisal costs for a contested cross-border divorce with property in two or more countries commonly run from the low five figures into six figures in USD terms, and appeals or enforcement actions add to that.

## What happens to your visa status

If your right to live in your host country is tied to your spouse's visa — a dependent or spousal visa — divorce (or even filing) can trigger a status review. Grace periods to find another legal basis to stay (a work permit, a different family visa, or departure) vary widely by country and are often measured in weeks rather than months; the UK, for example, gives some spousal visa holders a limited period to switch categories, while other countries offer none and expect departure once the underlying relationship ends. Because immigration rules run on separate deadlines from family court timelines, it's worth involving an immigration adviser the same week you involve a divorce lawyer, not after the decree is final.

## Practical sequencing

Before filing anywhere, gather proof of habitual residence (utility bills, tax returns, school enrollment, lease dates) for both spouses, since jurisdiction disputes are won or lost on this evidence. Get a written custody and travel agreement in place before any international trip once separation is underway — a Hague case is far more expensive than an ounce of prevention. And loop in a cross-border tax adviser before assets move, since a transfer that looks like a simple division can trigger capital gains or gift-tax consequences in either country.
