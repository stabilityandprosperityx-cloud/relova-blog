Expat health coverage splits into three categories people routinely confuse, and mixing them up is the single most common insurance mistake movers make. International Private Medical Insurance (IPMI) is comprehensive, ongoing coverage designed for residents — it follows you between countries, covers routine and chronic care, and is what you want for anything longer than a few months abroad. Travel insurance, by contrast, is built for short trips: it covers emergencies and trip disruption but typically excludes routine care, pre-existing conditions outright, and often caps coverage duration at 30–90 days. Local statutory/national health systems are a third path available once you hold qualifying residency in many countries — often cheaper than private IPMI but with longer wait times for non-emergency specialists and, in some countries, a requirement to also carry private top-up cover.

## What the major IPMI providers actually cost

Pricing depends heavily on age, deductible, and geographic coverage area (worldwide-including-US plans cost meaningfully more than worldwide-excluding-US). As a rough guide for a healthy adult in their 30s choosing a mid-tier plan: Cigna Global runs around $150–400 a month, Allianz Care (formerly Allianz Worldwide Care) about $200–500, and Bupa Global $200–600 for its more premium tiers. IMG's Global Medical plans are typically positioned in a similar $150–450 band depending on deductible. These figures rise substantially for applicants over 50 and for plans that include maternity or US treatment. SafetyWing's Remote Health, aimed at nomads rather than long-term residents, is priced far lower — around $56 for a 4-week US-inclusive period for someone in their 20s-30s — but it caps coverage duration, has lower per-incident limits, and is not a substitute for comprehensive IPMI once you're settled somewhere for a year or more.

## Pre-existing conditions and the fine print that actually matters

Most IPMI insurers either exclude pre-existing conditions permanently or apply a moratorium — commonly six to twelve months of continuous coverage before a condition becomes eligible for claims, sometimes longer for chronic issues like diabetes or heart disease. Maternity is usually a separate rider with its own waiting period, often ten to twelve months from policy start, so it needs to be added well before conception if you're planning a family abroad. Emergency medical evacuation is worth checking closely if you're moving somewhere remote or mountainous — look for at least $500,000 in evacuation/repatriation benefit, since a single medevac flight can easily cost six figures. Dental and vision are almost always separate riders, typically adding $30–60 a month, and mental health coverage has improved industry-wide but still often caps the number of covered therapy sessions per year — read that clause specifically if ongoing therapy matters to you.

## When to switch from nomad-style cover to a residency-based plan

The right insurance product changes as a move shifts from short-term to settled. Someone bouncing between three countries in six months is generally better served by a SafetyWing- or Genki-style nomad plan (or robust travel insurance) that doesn't require a fixed home base, because comprehensive IPMI plans are priced and structured around a defined country of residence. Once residency is established for a year or more — a visa in hand, a lease signed, a tax residency likely forming — comprehensive IPMI becomes the better value, both because of the broader coverage and because many countries' residency or visa renewal processes explicitly require proof of adequate health insurance meeting specific minimum coverage amounts. Switching too late is the common mistake: people keep paying for thin nomad-style coverage well into a settled life abroad because switching plans feels like an administrative hassle, and in doing so carry real gaps in coverage for chronic care, specialist referrals, or anything requiring ongoing management.

## Comparing quotes without getting misled by headline prices

The advertised monthly price for any of these providers is a starting point, not a quote — actual premiums move substantially based on age band (a 55-year-old often pays two to three times what a 30-year-old pays for an identical plan), deductible/excess level chosen, geographic coverage area (worldwide-including-US costs noticeably more than worldwide-excluding-US), and whether maternity, dental, or mental health riders are added. The only reliable way to compare providers is running the same set of assumptions — same age, same deductible, same coverage area, same add-ons — through each insurer's actual quote tool or a broker who can do it in parallel, rather than comparing headline "starting from" prices that rarely reflect what any specific person will actually pay.
