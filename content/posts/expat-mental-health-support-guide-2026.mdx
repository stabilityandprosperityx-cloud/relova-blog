Finding a therapist abroad is mostly a licensing and logistics problem, not a search problem — the harder part is figuring out which of the therapists you can find are actually allowed to treat you from where you are sitting, and which insurance, if any, will pay for it.

## Teletherapy has jurisdiction limits most platforms don't advertise

Licensed therapists and psychiatrists are generally regulated to practice within the country, and often the specific state or province, where they hold their license. Large platforms like BetterHelp connect you with clinicians licensed in your *home* country — useful if you are a US citizen abroad wanting continuity with US-licensed care, but it means the therapist may not be legally able to treat you if you have moved permanently and become a tax and legal resident somewhere else, depending on that jurisdiction's telehealth rules. BetterHelp currently prices in the **$60–100 a week** range (roughly $240–400 a month) depending on plan tier and local demand for available therapist slots. Before committing, ask directly whether the platform can match you with someone licensed in your country of residence, not just your passport country — the answer varies by platform and changes as telehealth regulation catches up with remote work.

## Germany's public system requires a referral queue, not a walk-in

If you are enrolled in Germany's statutory health insurance (Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung, GKV), publicly-funded psychotherapy is available but routed through your **Hausarzt** (GP) or directly through a *Psychotherapeutische Sprechstunde* (a mandatory initial consultation hour every licensed psychotherapist must offer). Wait times for an ongoing slot with a public-system therapist are commonly measured in months, not weeks, in larger cities — Berlin and Munich are particularly stretched. Two workarounds exist: private-pay sessions with reimbursement claimed back from your Krankenkasse under specific "Kostenerstattung" rules if you can show no public slot was available within a reasonable time, or simply private-pay therapy outside the statutory system, which is faster but not automatically reimbursed. Book the Sprechstunde early and in parallel with any other plan — it is the one step that does not shortcut, regardless of how you eventually pay.

## Portugal's private-pay market is genuinely accessible

Portugal has no equivalent public queue problem for expats, mostly because most expats are paying privately anyway. Private-practice sessions with a licensed psicólogo or psiquiatra commonly run **€60–80** in Lisbon and Porto's expat-facing directories, with English-language practitioners at the higher end of that band given lower supply relative to demand. The Portuguese SNS (public health service) technically covers mental health, but access for non-citizen residents without an established médico de família (family doctor) assignment is slow enough that most expats default to private care regardless of insurance status.

## Employer EAPs are underused and time-limited

If you are employed by a company with an Employee Assistance Program, it is worth checking before paying anything out of pocket: EAPs commonly offer a fixed block, often **six to eight free sessions**, with a counselor, sometimes delivered by phone or video regardless of your location. The catch is the cap — EAP sessions are meant as a bridge to ongoing care, not a substitute for it, and providers vary widely in whether they can refer you onward to someone licensed in your actual country of residence once the free block runs out.

## Crisis resources are local, not portable

For anything urgent, do not rely on your home country's crisis line working from abroad — most, including the US 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, are only reliably reachable by phone number routing from inside that country. Before you need it, look up and save the emergency mental health number for your country of residence specifically (many EU countries route through the general 112 emergency line, with dedicated crisis lines layered on top by country), and confirm whether your private health insurance policy includes any 24/7 multilingual crisis support line as a policy benefit — many international plans do, and it is worth knowing the number before, not during, a crisis.
