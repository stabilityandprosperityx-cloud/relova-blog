The yield headline is never the whole story. Tbilisi routinely posts **gross rental yields of 6–9%** according to Global Property Guide's tracked data, which sounds like an easy trade against a 4–5% Western European alternative — until you model what happens to that return when the Georgian lari moves against the currency you actually spend in. Property investing as an expat is a currency bet wearing a real-estate costume more often than buyers admit, and 2026's regulatory environment in the most popular short-term-rental markets has made the "just buy an apartment and Airbnb it" plan considerably riskier than it was three years ago.

## Barcelona's phaseout is not a rumor anymore

Spain's Constitutional Court upheld Catalonia's legal framework for capping tourist-apartment licenses in **March 2025**, clearing the way for Barcelona to follow through on a policy Mayor Jaume Collboni first announced in June 2024: the city will not renew any of its roughly **10,101 licensed short-term rental apartments**, with every existing license expiring by **November 2028**. That is a hard, dated deadline, not a political mood. If you are underwriting a Barcelona purchase on projected Airbnb income, you are underwriting a business that legally ends in under three years unless the policy is reversed — model the property as a long-term rental from day one and treat any short-term income as a bonus you'll eventually lose, not the base case.

## Lisbon's Alojamento Local freeze, and the new EU layer on top

Lisbon has repeatedly extended its suspension on new **Alojamento Local (AL)** short-term rental licenses in the highest-pressure zones of the city, meaning an unlicensed apartment purchased today generally cannot be legally converted into a short-let without an existing license attached to the unit — a real premium buyers now pay for "AL included" listings. On top of the municipal freeze, an EU-wide short-term rental data regulation is now pushing platforms including Airbnb and Booking.com to delist units that lack verified registration numbers, which is tightening enforcement against the grey-market listings that used to pad projected yields. Fines for unlicensed AL operation in Portugal can run into the tens of thousands of euros, so "I'll just list it anyway" is not a real strategy in 2026.

## Tbilisi: the yield is real, the currency risk is too

Georgia's rental-yield numbers remain genuinely attractive, and its residency-by-investment threshold — a **$100,000 real estate purchase** — is one of the more accessible property-linked residence routes in the region. But most rental income and eventual resale value are denominated in Georgian lari (GEL), while most foreign buyers carry mortgage or opportunity-cost liabilities in USD or EUR. Georgia does not peg the lari the way Bosnia pegs the mark; it floats, and it has moved meaningfully against the dollar over multi-year periods. A 7% gross yield that loses 4–5% a year to currency depreciation against your home currency is a very different investment than the same 7% in a pegged or matched-currency market. Run the return in both currencies before you wire a deposit.

## Dubai: tax-free headline, real service-charge drag

Dubai's **5–8% gross yields** come with no income tax and no capital gains tax for individuals, which is the pitch every off-plan sales agent leads with. What gets buried lower in the brochure is the **service charge** — typically **AED 10–25 per square foot annually** depending on the building and amenities — plus a mandatory Dubai Land Department transfer fee of **4%** at purchase. On a modest one-bedroom, service charges alone can consume a meaningful slice of gross rental income before you've paid a property manager, and Dubai's rental market has historically been cyclical with off-plan oversupply periodically pressuring achieved rents below projected ones. Treat published yield figures as the ceiling, not the number you'll actually bank.

## The management fee that quietly resets your math

Whichever market you choose, short-term-rental property management typically runs **10–25% of gross rental revenue** once you include listing management, cleaning, guest communication, and dynamic pricing — before platform fees. That is a materially different economics than a long-term tenant on a 12-month lease managed by a local letting agent for a flat month's-rent-equivalent fee once a year. Run your yield calculation net of realistic management costs for the strategy you actually intend to run, not the gross number every listing site advertises, and build a vacancy assumption into any short-term-rental model given how quickly license and platform rules have moved in the last two years.
