The single biggest planning error in a relocation isn't a missing document — it's misjudging which tasks have long, fixed lead times that can't be compressed under pressure. A visa officer doesn't process faster because your flight is booked; a shipping container doesn't cross an ocean quicker because you procrastinated. Work backward from moving day and anchor each task to its real minimum lead time, not the optimistic one in a sales deck.

## 12 months out: passport, tax residency, and the decisions that touch everything downstream

Check your passport's expiry date against the destination's entry rule — most countries require **six months of validity beyond your intended stay**, and a passport renewal itself can take weeks to months depending on your home country's backlog. This is also the moment to get a tax-residency memo from an accountant familiar with both your current and destination country: exit-tax triggers, split-year treatment, and whether unvested equity or bonuses should be timed before or after your departure date are decisions that are cheap to make now and expensive to unwind later. If you're leaving a country with exit tax on unrealized gains (common for long-term residents of several EU states and the US for green-card holders), this single conversation can be worth more than every other item on this list combined.

## 9 months out: scouting trips with a scorecard, not a vibe check

If you haven't lived in the destination city, book a scouting trip now — not to fall in love with a neighborhood, but to test commute times at actual rush hour, visit the specific international schools you're considering (not just their websites), and price real rental listings rather than aggregator averages. Bring a written scorecard: healthcare access, school application deadlines, commute, and grocery/utility costs side by side across your top two or three neighborhood candidates. A trip built around Instagram-worthy cafés tells you nothing about whether the 7am train to the office actually runs on schedule.

## 6 months out: the hard filing deadlines converge

This is where visa applications, international school deposits, and freight bookings all land at once, and it's why six months — not three — is the realistic minimum for most relocations. Visa processing itself commonly runs **1–6 months** depending on the route and country, so filing later than this window risks arriving without status. Ocean freight transit is typically **4–8 weeks** door-to-door once your shipment is actually booked and collected, and international school applications for the following academic year often close **6–12 months** ahead — miss that window and you're looking at a waitlist or a compromise school for year one. Apostilling and certified-translating documents (birth certificates, marriage certificates, diplomas) generally takes **2–6 weeks** per document depending on the issuing state or country, and some destinations require documents apostilled within a certain window before use — don't apostille too early only to have the certification expire before you file.

## 3 months out: the administrative dry-run

Confirm your visa decision has actually been issued (not just "in process") before you give notice on a lease or resign from a job tied to your current status. Start the health-insurance transition — international coverage that begins the day you land, not the day your old employer coverage ends, since gaps of even a week create real exposure. If you're shipping a car, this is typically the last reasonable window to start registration and import paperwork given customs processing times in most destinations.

## 1 month out: shipping, SIMs, and the boring stuff that blocks day one

Final freight pickup, a destination-country SIM or eSIM ready before landing, and copies (physical and scanned) of every document you'll need in the first week — lease, visa approval, insurance card, school enrollment confirmation — organized in one folder, not scattered across email threads you won't have reliable access to on arrival.

The pattern across all five checkpoints: the tasks that feel administrative (passports, apostilles, school deadlines) are the ones with fixed external timelines you cannot negotiate, while the tasks that feel exciting (scouting, house-hunting) are the ones with real flexibility. Sequence accordingly.
