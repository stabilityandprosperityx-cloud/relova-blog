Every legitimate expat tax strategy starts from the same uncomfortable fact: you cannot optimize a residency you haven't actually established, and the rules used to decide where you're a resident don't care what your passport says.

## The FEIE, and why it isn't automatic

For US citizens abroad, the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion** lets you exclude **$130,000 of foreign earned income for 2025, rising to $132,900 for 2026** (inflation-adjusted annually under IRS guidance), from US taxable income — but only if you pass either the **physical presence test** (330 full days in a foreign country in any 12-month period, with US transit days counted against you) or the **bona fide residence test** (residing abroad for a full calendar year, evidenced by a lease, local bank accounts, and genuine ties, not just a passport stamp). The FEIE only covers *earned* income — wages and self-employment income — not passive income like dividends, rental income, or capital gains, which is where the **Foreign Tax Credit** typically does more work instead. Most expats benefit from combining both rather than treating them as either/or, and the right split depends heavily on whether your host country's tax rate is higher or lower than the US bracket you'd otherwise fall into.

## Totalization agreements stop the double social-security bill

The US has **totalization agreements** with around 30 countries that prevent you from paying into two social security systems simultaneously for the same work — without one, a US citizen working abroad can end up owing US self-employment tax on top of the host country's mandatory social contributions, a double hit that's entirely avoidable if the agreement exists and you file the right certificate of coverage. Check whether your specific destination has one before assuming either country's system "just applies" — coverage details vary agreement to agreement, and self-employed and employed status are sometimes treated differently under the same treaty.

## Treaty tie-breaker rules decide dual-residency disputes

When two countries both claim you as a tax resident under their own domestic rules — a common outcome for anyone who splits time or moves mid-year — a bilateral tax treaty based on the **OECD Model Convention** typically resolves it through a fixed sequence: first, where you have a **permanent home available**; if both or neither, where your **center of vital interests** lies (personal and economic ties, weighted together); if that's unclear, your **habitual abode**; and failing that, nationality, with mutual agreement between tax authorities as the final fallback. This sequence matters enormously in year-of-move situations, and it's worth walking through deliberately rather than assuming your intended "primary" country wins by default — the test is fact-based, not intent-based.

## The UK's non-dom regime is gone — know what replaced it

The UK abolished its centuries-old **non-domiciled (non-dom) remittance-basis regime** for new foreign income and gains from **6 April 2025**, replacing it with the **Foreign Income and Gains (FIG) regime**: a **four-year exemption** from UK tax on foreign income and gains, available to individuals who were not UK tax resident in any of the prior 10 tax years. That's a materially shorter runway than the old regime offered long-stay non-doms, and it changes the math for anyone who was planning a UK relocation around indefinite non-dom status — the FIG window is a firm four years, after which worldwide income and gains come fully into the UK tax net.

## Portugal's NHR is closed — IFICI is the current route

Portugal's popular **Non-Habitual Resident (NHR)** regime closed to new general applicants, with transitional arrangements running through 2025 for those who had already established ties. Its replacement, the **IFICI regime** (often called NHR 2.0), targets a narrower band of high-value-added professions and scientific research roles, offering a **20% flat rate** on qualifying Portuguese-source income and exemptions on most foreign-source income for those who qualify — but eligibility is occupation-specific rather than open to any relocating remote worker, a meaningfully different design than the old NHR's broader reach. If your Portugal plan was built around the old regime's rules, revisit the numbers against IFICI's actual eligibility criteria before assuming the tax benefit still applies to your situation.

## Structure matters more than the country you pick

The single biggest lever most expats underuse isn't a country choice at all — it's whether income flows through a personally held contract, a foreign company, or a locally registered entity, each of which can trigger different withholding, corporate tax, and permanent-establishment consequences in both the host and home jurisdiction. This is squarely accountant territory, not a DIY spreadsheet exercise, and the cost of a proper cross-border tax consultation before you move is almost always smaller than the cost of unwinding a structure that triggered double taxation or an unplanned permanent-establishment finding a year in.
