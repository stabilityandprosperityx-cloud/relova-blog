There is one non-negotiable rule in finding long-term housing abroad: never sign a lease or wire a deposit before an in-person viewing, or at minimum a live video call with the landlord or agent walking the property in real time. Everything else in this guide is detail; that rule is the whole point.

The method that consistently works is booking **2–4 weeks** of temporary housing — a short-let Airbnb, a serviced apartment, or a guesthouse — before committing to anything long-term. That window buys you the ability to see flats in actual daylight, meet agents in person, walk neighborhoods at different times of day, and compare districts against your commute and lifestyle rather than against a map. Listing photos should be treated as marketing, not documentation, until you've verified them yourself: they're frequently outdated, cropped to hide flaws, or in the worst cases simply stolen from another listing entirely. Whatever you eventually sign, insist on a written contract — even for something as informal as a shared room in someone's apartment — and pay only through a traceable bank transfer, never unreceipted cash, so you have a paper trail if a dispute arises later.

The fraud patterns worth memorizing are consistent across countries and rarely change. Anyone who asks for payment via wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or gift cards before you've seen the property in person or on a live call is running a scam, no exceptions. The classic line — "I'm currently abroad, I'll mail you the keys once you pay" — is essentially always fraudulent; legitimate landlords do not operate this way. A listing priced well below the going rate for a comparable property in a desirable area is a lure, not a bargain. Video tours that are clearly pre-recorded rather than live, with no ability to ask the person to pan the camera or answer a question in real time, are a warning sign. And watch for the softer tells: template-sounding emails, inconsistent spelling or grammar that doesn't match the claimed nationality of the landlord, and manufactured urgency ("another tenant is ready to pay today"). If a listing's photos feel too polished or oddly generic, a reverse image search takes thirty seconds and regularly turns up the same stolen photos circulating across multiple fake listings.

Several countries have quirks specific enough to catch newcomers off guard. **South Korea's jeonse** system lets tenants pay a lump-sum deposit — sometimes up to the full value of the property — instead of monthly rent, refunded at the end of the lease; it's an attractive-sounding arrangement, but jeonse fraud has become a serious national problem, with reported losses exceeding roughly **$1 billion a year**, so this is not a system to enter without a local lawyer verifying the landlord's actual ownership and any existing liens on the property. In **Japan**, **reikin** ("key money") — a non-refundable payment to the landlord, historically one to two months' rent — is still charged by many landlords, though a growing number of agencies now offer reikin-free listings if you specifically search for them. In **Germany**, some landlords illegally attempt to extract additional payments after the deposit and lease are already signed; this is a clear violation of German tenancy law and worth pushing back on rather than assuming it's normal practice. In **Spain**, deposits for primary residences are generally capped near **one month's rent** by law, and since 2023 agency fees for primary residential lettings typically fall on the landlord rather than the tenant — though this protection doesn't extend to short-term or seasonal lets, which operate under different rules.

A few platforms build in specific renter protections worth knowing about: **HousingAnywhere** offers a roughly 48-hour post-move-in protection window during which you can flag a property that doesn't match its listing, and **Flatio** focuses on medium-term rentals without requiring the large upfront deposits typical of long-term leases. Budget beyond the headline rent figure too — a typical total outlay is a deposit of **1–3 months' rent** plus an agent fee often around **one additional month**, on top of the first month's rent itself, which means your actual move-in cash requirement can be three to five times the monthly rent.
