Freelancing abroad is really two separate jobs running in parallel: keeping clients happy, and keeping your entities, invoices, and tax residency defensible when two different countries could plausibly claim the same income. The legal-wrapper question — should I set up a company, and where — comes up early for almost every location-independent freelancer, and the honest answer is that no wrapper by itself fixes your immigration status or your tax residency. It just gives you a cleaner way to invoice clients and separate business from personal finances. The three options below cover the vast majority of setups freelancers actually use, each solving a slightly different problem depending on where your clients, your banking relationships, and your own long-term plans sit.

**Estonian e-Residency** is the most talked-about option for digital freelancers, largely because of Estonia's genuinely streamlined digital government infrastructure. Setup typically costs around **€100–120** for the e-Residency card itself, with ongoing company maintenance (accounting, registered agent services, annual filings) commonly running near **€300 a year**. What e-Residency does not do, despite how it's sometimes marketed, is grant you any right to actually live in Estonia or the EU — it's a digital identity and company-formation tool, not an immigration status. You can run an Estonian OÜ company entirely from Bali or Mexico City; the company being Estonian has no bearing on where you're allowed to physically reside.

A **UK limited company** is the cheapest formal option on this list — online registration costs as little as **£12** through Companies House — and it's attractive for freelancers with UK clients or a UK banking relationship. Like e-Residency, forming a UK Ltd grants zero UK residence rights; it's purely a corporate structure, and non-resident directors running a UK company from abroad still need to sort out their personal tax residency separately, in whichever country they're actually living.

A **US LLC** is the most flexible in terms of where you incorporate — costs vary by state, commonly cited in the **$50–500** range depending on filing fees and whether you use a registered agent service, with Wyoming and Delaware frequently recommended in freelancer playbooks for their low fees and privacy-friendly rules. The recurring mistake with US LLCs is assuming the structure itself "solves" global tax exposure — it doesn't. A single-member LLC is typically a pass-through entity for US tax purposes, meaning the income flows through to the owner's personal tax return regardless of where the LLC is registered, and non-US owners still face their own home-country or country-of-residence tax obligations on top of that.

Whichever wrapper you pick, you'll need a way to actually move money between currencies and clients without losing a chunk of it to fees. **Wise Business** has become a default choice for freelancers invoicing across currencies, since it lets you hold and invoice in multiple currencies with the same low, transparent FX fees as Wise's personal product, avoiding the poor exchange rates that traditional business banking often applies to incoming foreign payments.

The tax residency question underneath all of this comes back to the **183-day rule**, and it's worth repeating that this is a heuristic, not a universal law — most countries use some version of a roughly six-month presence test as one factor in determining tax residency, but treaty tie-breaker rules (permanent home, center of vital interests, habitual abode) can override a simple day count, and some countries apply stricter or different tests entirely. The practical takeaway for any freelancer picking a legal wrapper is to treat the company structure and the personal tax residency question as two separate decisions — get the company set up for invoicing convenience, and get proper advice on personal tax residency in whichever country you're actually spending most of your time, rather than assuming one decision handles both.
