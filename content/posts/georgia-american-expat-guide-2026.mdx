[Georgia](/blog/georgia-digital-nomad-tax-visa-deep-dive-2026)'s visa entry is the easy part for Americans — a US passport gets you up to 365 days visa-free, longer than almost any other country offers, which is exactly why [Georgia](/blog/georgia-russian-citizen-2026-update) shows up on so many American nomad shortlists. The part that gets glossed over is what stays true regardless of how long you live there: US citizens owe federal tax on worldwide income no matter where they reside or how long they've been gone, and Georgia's local tax simplicity doesn't touch that obligation at all.

## No tax treaty means no treaty relief

This is the detail most general "moving to Georgia" content skips entirely, and it matters specifically for Americans: the United States and Georgia do not currently have a bilateral income tax treaty in force. Without a treaty, you can't rely on treaty tie-breaker residency rules or treaty-specific relief provisions — double taxation exposure instead runs entirely through the US's own domestic mechanisms (the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, which shelters roughly the first $130,000 of foreign-earned income for the 2026 tax year, and the Foreign Tax Credit under Form 1116 for tax actually paid to Georgia) plus whatever unilateral relief Georgia's own tax code offers foreign-source income under its territorial system. For W-2-style remote employees, FEIE alone often gets effective US tax close to zero on earned income under the threshold; for anyone with Georgian-source business income, US investment income, or income above the FEIE cap, the Foreign Tax Credit calculation gets more complicated without a treaty to fall back on — this is genuinely a case where paying a US-Georgia-experienced CPA for one planning session pays for itself.

## FATCA and FBAR don't pause because you moved

Two filing obligations continue exactly as before, and neither cares that you're now a Georgian tax resident: FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) triggers once the aggregate balance across all your foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year, and FATCA's Form 8938 has its own separate, higher thresholds that kick in for foreign-resident Americans (commonly cited around $200,000 for single filers at year-end, higher at any point during the year — confirm the exact current figures with a CPA, as they differ from the FBAR threshold and from domestic-resident FATCA thresholds). Missing either isn't a minor paperwork slip; penalties for willful FBAR non-compliance can run into tens of thousands of dollars.

## Banking friction is real and worth planning around

FATCA's reporting burden on foreign financial institutions has a well-documented side effect: many banks outside the US, including several in Georgia and the wider region, either restrict account opening for US citizens or decline US-citizen customers outright, because the compliance overhead of reporting to the IRS isn't worth it for a small number of accounts. TBC Bank and Bank of Georgia both generally still serve American clients, but expect more documentation requests than a non-US applicant would face, and expect some smaller institutions to simply say no. Keeping a US-based account (Charles Schwab's checking account is popular among expats specifically because it rebates ATM fees worldwide) alongside a local Georgian account for day-to-day lari spending is the standard workaround. Registering with the US Embassy Tbilisi's STEP program is worth doing given the region's periodic instability, and Tbilisi's American community — estimated in the low thousands and growing alongside the city's tech and remote-work scene — is active enough to make the FATCA/banking learning curve easier through word of mouth.

## State-tax residency is a separate question from federal

Federal tax obligations aren't the only thing that follows Americans abroad — state tax residency can too, depending on which state was home before the move. States like California and New York are notoriously aggressive about continuing to treat departed residents as tax residents unless specific steps (severing driver's license, voter registration, and property ties, and in some cases establishing residency in a no-income-tax state before leaving the US) are taken to formally break residency. This is a separate legal question from federal FEIE eligibility and easy to overlook while focused on the more visible Georgia-specific and IRS-specific rules — anyone moving from a high-tax state should address state residency deliberately rather than assuming a move abroad automatically ends state tax obligations.

## Retirement accounts and investment friction

Americans abroad routinely run into a less-discussed friction point: many US brokerages restrict or close accounts for customers who update their address to a foreign country, and separately, many foreign banks and investment platforms won't accept US citizens as customers at all because of the compliance burden PFIC (Passive Foreign Investment Company) reporting and FATCA place on both the institution and the investor. This leaves a real gap for Americans who want to keep investing while abroad — Charles Schwab International and Interactive Brokers are among the few providers that reliably keep serving US citizens living overseas, and it's worth confirming a brokerage's policy on expat customers before a move rather than discovering a forced account closure after the fact. Non-US mutual funds and ETFs, in particular, are generally something to actively avoid as a US citizen given the punitive PFIC tax treatment the IRS applies to them.
