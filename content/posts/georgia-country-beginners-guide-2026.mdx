If you've heard "Georgia" mentioned in expat and digital nomad circles and are now wondering whether that's the US state or somewhere else entirely, it's the country — a small nation in the South Caucasus, bordered by Russia to the north, Turkey and Armenia to the south, Azerbaijan to the east, and the Black Sea to the west. It sits at the geographic crossroads of Europe and Asia, which shows up in everything from its cuisine to its architecture to the fact that it's been fought over, absorbed, and reasserted its independence more times than most countries manage in a millennium. This is meant as the true starting point: a first-principles primer for someone who knows almost nothing about the place, before you get into visa mechanics or tax registration, which are covered in more depth in our dedicated [Georgia digital nomad tax and visa deep dive](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/georgia-digital-nomad-tax-visa-deep-dive-2026).

The country is genuinely old and genuinely modern at the same time, and that contrast is probably the single most disorienting thing for first-time visitors. Georgian winemaking traditions trace back roughly **eight thousand years**, using a qvevri (clay vessel) method now recognized by UNESCO — this is widely considered one of the oldest continuous winemaking cultures on earth. At the same time, Tbilisi apartments commonly have fiber internet running **50–200 Mbps**, and the capital has positioned itself deliberately as a low-friction base for remote workers and small businesses, with a startup and coworking scene that would be unremarkable in Lisbon but is genuinely notable for a city of Tbilisi's size and regional context.

The currency is the **Georgian lari (GEL)**, and the language is **Georgian**, which uses its own unique alphabet (Mkhedruli) unrelated to Cyrillic or Latin scripts — it's a Kartvelian language, not a Slavic one, a distinction that surprises people who assume any country near Russia must speak a Russian-adjacent language. Learning at least the alphabet before arrival genuinely helps with reading signage and menus, since transliteration isn't always consistent. English proficiency is reasonably strong among under-40s in Tbilisi, especially in hospitality, tech, and university settings, but it fades quickly once you leave the capital or step outside tourist-facing businesses, so don't assume English will carry you everywhere in the country.

On safety, Georgia consistently ranks well on global peace and safety indices, generally landing in or near the top thirty countries worldwide on measures like the Global Peace Index — comparable to many Western European countries and notably safer-feeling than its geopolitical neighborhood might suggest to someone unfamiliar with the region. Violent crime against foreigners is rare, though the usual advice about petty theft awareness in crowded tourist areas still applies.

Climate is worth planning around rather than assuming. Tbilisi summers are genuinely hot, commonly hitting **35–38°C (95–100°F)** in July and August, and most older apartment buildings were not built with air conditioning as standard, so check for AC specifically when apartment-hunting if you're arriving in summer. Winters are mild by comparison but can be damp and grey, and the country's mountainous regions (Svaneti, Kazbegi) offer a completely different, much colder climate if you venture beyond the capital.

Healthcare splits sharply between private and public: Georgia's private healthcare sector is strong, modern, and affordable by Western standards, while public healthcare infrastructure is comparatively under-resourced and inconsistent in quality outside major cities. Most expats and long-term visitors default to private insurance or paying out of pocket for private clinics rather than relying on the public system.

On cost of living, a realistic monthly budget in Tbilisi runs from around **$1,000** for a tight, locally-styled lifestyle up to **$1,500** for a comfortable one, and **$2,000** or more for a genuinely plush setup with a nicer apartment, regular dining out, and a car — figures that make Georgia one of the more affordable bases in the wider Europe-adjacent region even at the upper end.
