This is a tax-mechanics piece, not a general moving guide — if you're looking for visas and cost of living, see [How to Move to Georgia (the Country)](/blog/how-to-move-to-georgia-country). What follows is specifically how [Georgia](/blog/retire-to-georgia-country-guide-2026)'s tax code treats a remote worker or freelancer once they're actually inside the system, because the country's reputation as a "1% tax haven" is accurate but only if you register correctly and stay inside the rules.

## Territorial taxation is the foundation everything else sits on

[Georgia](/blog/georgia-eu-citizen-guide-2026) taxes residents on Georgian-source income and, separately, on foreign-source income only in specific circumstances — it does not run a full worldwide-taxation model the way the US or most of Western Europe does. In practice this means a tax resident whose income comes from foreign clients, via the Individual Entrepreneur (IE) structure described below, can often structure that income so it's taxed lightly or not captured at the higher personal rates that would apply to Georgian-source employment income. This territorial principle, not any single visa, is the actual mechanism behind Georgia's low-tax reputation for remote workers.

## Individual Entrepreneur status is the workhorse structure

Almost every freelancer or remote worker case study you'll read about "Georgia's 1% tax" is really describing Individual Entrepreneur (IE) registration combined with Small Business Status. Registration is handled through the Revenue Service (part of the Ministry of Finance) and is genuinely fast by international standards — typically completed within a single business day once you have a personal ID number and the paperwork in order, with a nominal registration fee in the tens of GEL. IE status alone doesn't set your tax rate; Small Business Status is the separate designation that unlocks the preferential rate.

## Small Business Status: the 1% rate, and its real ceiling

Once registered as an IE, you can apply separately for Small Business Status. Qualifying turnover is taxed at 1% up to an annual threshold of 500,000 GEL (roughly $180,000 depending on the exchange rate) — a figure worth reconfirming against the Revenue Service's current published number before you rely on it, since currency conversions shift and the GEL figure itself has been the subject of periodic government review. Turnover above that threshold in the same calendar year is taxed at a higher marginal rate (commonly cited as 3% on the excess) rather than losing the preferential rate retroactively. Exceed the threshold two years running, however, and Small Business Status is revoked from the following January, pushing you back onto standard personal income tax rates — a meaningfully worse outcome, so consistent high earners should model this before assuming the 1% rate is permanent.

## The 183-day rule decides who Georgia even considers a tax resident

None of the above matters unless you're a Georgian tax resident in the first place. The standard test is physical presence: spend 183 days or more in Georgia within any 12-month period and you're treated as a tax resident for that period. This is separate from immigration status — you can be in Georgia legally under the visa-free entry rule without crossing the 183-day tax-residency threshold, in which case you're not filing as a Georgian tax resident at all. People who split time between Georgia and elsewhere need to track this day count carefully, because crossing it retroactively changes your filing obligations for the whole period.

## VAT registration is the threshold people forget about

Small Business Status covers income tax, but it doesn't exempt you from VAT once turnover crosses a separate registration trigger, commonly cited around 100,000 GEL in a 12-month period. Crossing that line means registering for VAT and charging it on applicable invoices, which is a different compliance track from the income-tax side and catches freelancers who assumed the 1% regime covered everything.

## The High Net Worth Individual route is a different door entirely

Separate from IE/Small Business Status, Georgia also offers a High Net Worth Individual (HNWI) tax residency route for people who don't want to rely on the 183-day physical-presence test. It's aimed at individuals who meet asset or income thresholds set by the Revenue Service and typically requires either a documented asset base or verified annual income above a set bar, plus some minimum physical presence in Georgia (far lower than 183 days). This route suits higher earners who want certainty about tax residency without committing to more than half the year physically in the country — but the qualifying thresholds and required documentation should be confirmed directly with a Georgian tax advisor, since HNWI criteria are reviewed periodically and are less publicly standardized than the IE/Small Business numbers.

## Banking and compliance realities worth knowing before you register

Opening a business account tied to IE status is generally straightforward with Georgia's major banks (TBC, Bank of Georgia), but banks have tightened compliance checks in recent years around source-of-funds documentation for foreign clients, so expect to provide invoices and client contracts rather than a purely self-declared turnover figure. Georgia also maintains double-tax treaties with a substantial number of countries, which matters if your home country would otherwise also claim taxing rights over the same income — treaty relief isn't automatic and generally needs to be claimed, not assumed.
