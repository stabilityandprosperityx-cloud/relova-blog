[Georgia](/blog/georgia-american-expat-guide-2026) has remained one of the few countries offering Russian citizens a genuinely simple entry route since 2022: as of 2026, Russian passport holders can still enter and remain visa-free for up to 365 days per calendar year, a policy [Georgia](/blog/georgia-digital-nomad-tax-visa-deep-dive-2026) has kept in place even as many other countries tightened or eliminated visa-free access for Russian nationals. This makes Georgia one of the more consistently accessible destinations for Russians relative to most of Europe, where Schengen visa issuance to Russian citizens has become substantially harder since 2022. Confirm current entry requirements directly with the Georgian Ministry of Foreign Affairs or your nearest consulate before traveling, since sanctions-adjacent policy in the region has shifted more than once and a general guide can go stale faster than the usual pace of visa-rule changes.

## Banking is the real friction point, not the visa

The practical difficulty for Russian residents in Georgia is almost entirely on the banking side rather than immigration. Mir, Russia's domestic card payment system built after Visa and Mastercard withdrew from Russia in 2022, does not function for payments outside Russia in any meaningful way due to the international sanctions architecture around it — a Mir card is effectively useless for daily spending in Georgia. This pushes Russian residents toward opening a local Georgian bank account instead. TBC Bank has generally been more accessible for Russian citizens opening accounts and for third-country Visa/Mastercard transactions than some competitors, though requirements and friction can shift with each sanctions update. Bank of Georgia has applied progressively heavier know-your-customer (KYC) documentation requirements for Russian applicants, reflecting the compliance pressure Georgian banks face from their own correspondent banking relationships with Western institutions. None of this is Georgia-specific hostility — it's the downstream effect of SWIFT-related sanctions on Russian banks generally, which makes cross-border payment and account-opening friction for Russian citizens a near-universal experience internationally, not something unique to Georgia.

## Residence alternatives beyond the visa-free year

For anyone planning to stay past a single visa-free year, or who wants a documented status rather than repeated border runs, Individual Entrepreneur (IE) registration remains a viable path with thorough documentation and generally benefits from working with a local accountant familiar with the current requirements for Russian applicants specifically. Real estate ownership is available to Russian citizens for residential apartments, which can also support a residence permit application, but agricultural land purchases remain restricted to foreign nationals broadly (not a Russia-specific rule) under Georgian law. Community estimates for Russian residents in Georgia peaked in the tens of thousands in the years following 2022, concentrated heavily in Tbilisi and Batumi, and the community's visible growth has at times been a subject of public discussion and protest within Georgia — a social dynamic worth being aware of, reported here simply as context rather than commentary. Armenia is commonly considered as a hedge or alternative base, offering up to 180 visa-free days for Russian citizens along with its own IT-sector tax incentives.

## Sending and receiving money is the other bottleneck

Beyond opening a local account, moving money into and out of Georgia remains genuinely harder for Russian citizens than for most other nationalities, because the underlying international payment rails (SWIFT among them) have restricted numerous Russian banks since 2022. In practice, this pushes many Russian residents in Georgia toward earning and holding funds in cash, cryptocurrency, or through third-country intermediary accounts (a relative or business partner in a non-sanctioned jurisdiction, for instance) rather than relying on a direct transfer from a Russian bank account. International freelance platforms and payment processors have also tightened compliance checks for accounts linked to Russian citizenship or Russian banking relationships, adding friction even for legitimate freelance income that has nothing to do with sanctioned entities. None of this is unique to Georgia — it reflects the broader state of Russia's international banking access — but it shapes daily financial life for Russian residents there more than the visa question does.

## What to verify before committing to a move

Given how much of this has shifted since 2022 and continues to be revisited, anyone seriously planning a move should treat this article as a starting orientation rather than a final answer: confirm current visa-free entry terms directly with the Georgian consulate or Ministry of Foreign Affairs, confirm current account-opening requirements directly with TBC Bank or Bank of Georgia (policies at the branch level can differ from published policy), and confirm current IE registration requirements with a Tbilisi-based accountant who has recent experience with Russian-citizen clients specifically. Georgian-language and English-language expat forums focused on the Russian community in Tbilisi are also a reasonably current, if informal, source for what's actually working on the ground month to month, since official policy and practical bank-branch behavior don't always move in lockstep.
