The first fork in the road for any [freelancer](/blog/nomad-visa-europe-comparison) in Germany is a distinction that has real financial consequences: Freiberufler (liberal professions — this covers most software developers, designers, writers, doctors, lawyers, and consultants) versus Gewerbetreibende (commercial trade, covering most other self-employed activity, including e-commerce and many consulting setups that involve reselling or intermediary work). The Finanzamt decides which box you fall into based on your answers on the Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung, filed online via ELSTER within a month of starting work. The distinction matters because Gewerbetreibende owe Gewerbesteuer (trade tax) on top of income tax, at a rate that varies by municipality but commonly lands between roughly 7% and 17% of trade profit; Freiberufler are exempt from it entirely. Borderline cases (a developer who also resells software licenses, for instance) are worth a tax advisor's input before registering, since reclassification later is more painful than getting it right up front.

## The freelance residence permit

Non-EU citizens need the Aufenthaltserlaubnis für selbständige Tätigkeit (self-employment residence permit) before they can legally freelance in Germany. The application requires demonstrating an economic interest or regional need for your services, a viable business plan, and evidence of financial means to support yourself — the local Ausländerbehörde has real discretion here, and processing times and documentation expectations vary noticeably between cities (Berlin's is famously slower and more bureaucratic than many mid-sized cities). Freiberufler in in-demand fields like software development generally have an easier time clearing this bar than commercial-trade applicants.

## Taxes, VAT, and the Künstlersozialkasse

Kleinunternehmer status exempts [freelancer](/blog/move-to-hamburg-guide-2026)s from charging VAT once annual revenue stays under €22,000 in the prior year (and a forecast under €50,000 for the current year) — useful for people just starting out, though it also means you can't reclaim VAT on business expenses. Income tax is progressive from roughly 14% to 45%, with a Solidaritätszuschlag surcharge (5.5% of the income tax owed, though most lower and middle earners are now exempt from it) layered on top. Freelancers pay quarterly advance income tax payments (Vorauszahlungen) based on estimated annual profit, reconciled at year-end filing. One easily missed cost: freelance artists, writers, journalists, and certain creative/media professionals can join the Künstlersozialkasse (KSK), which pays roughly half their statutory health, pension, and long-term care insurance contributions the way an employer normally would — it's a meaningful subsidy that many eligible freelancers never apply for simply because they don't know it exists.

## Health insurance is not optional

Proof of health insurance is required for the residence permit and for daily life generally. Voluntary public insurance (gesetzliche Krankenversicherung) through providers like TK or AOK runs close to €400 a month for many self-employed people once income-based contributions are calculated, though the exact figure depends on declared income. Private insurance can be cheaper for younger, healthier freelancers but gets progressively more expensive with age and is harder to switch back out of later — worth thinking through as a long-term decision, not just a first-year cost comparison.

## Bookkeeping and finding an accountant

Most freelancers with straightforward Category B or Freiberufler income can manage bookkeeping themselves in the first year or two using simplified income-expense accounting (Einnahmenüberschussrechnung, EÜR), filed alongside the annual tax return. Once revenue grows or the business gets more complex — hiring subcontractors, cross-border invoicing, VAT registration — most freelancers bring in a Steuerberater (tax advisor), and DATEV-compatible bookkeeping software becomes genuinely useful for handing off clean records rather than a shoebox of receipts. A German-speaking tax advisor familiar with foreign freelancers is worth the premium over a generalist, since the interaction between residence-permit requirements and tax filings is a detail generalist accountants sometimes miss.

## Common early mistakes worth avoiding

Underestimating quarterly prepayments is the most frequent first-year surprise: the Finanzamt sets Vorauszahlungen based on an initial income estimate, and if actual income comes in higher, the difference becomes due at year-end filing rather than being spread out — building a buffer rather than spending every euro of invoiced income is the standard advice from German tax advisors for exactly this reason. Missing the ELSTER registration deadline or filing the wrong activity classification on the Fragebogen zur steuerlichen Erfassung can also trigger a reclassification review later, which is more disruptive than simply asking a tax advisor to review the form before submission. Finally, freelancers renewing a residence permit should keep clean, exportable records of invoices and bank statements from day one — the Ausländerbehörde's renewal review generally wants to see sustained, plausible income, and scrambling to reconstruct a year of financial history at renewal time is avoidable with basic ongoing discipline.
