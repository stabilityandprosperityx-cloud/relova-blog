Grocery spending is one of the few recurring costs an expat can genuinely control, and the gap between countries — and between shopping habits within the same country — is bigger than most people expect before they arrive.

## Where you land sets the baseline

[Georgia](/blog/tbilisi-budget-guide-2026) is one of the cheapest food markets available to Western expats, with a single person eating well on local produce, bread, dairy, and meat commonly spending USD 150-200 a month — local bazaars (like Tbilisi's Deserters' Bazaar) undercut supermarket prices substantially if you're willing to shop there instead of at Carrefour or Goodwill supermarkets. Thailand runs similarly low, often USD 100-150 a month for a single person, particularly if street food and local wet markets make up a meaningful share of the diet rather than imported-goods supermarket shopping. Portugal sits at a moderate EU benchmark, typically EUR 200-300 a month, reflecting a functioning EU grocery market with real competition between chains like Pingo Doce, Continente, and Lidl. Switzerland is the outlier at the top: a comparable basket runs CHF 600-800 a month, among the highest grocery costs in Europe, driven by high labor costs, import duties on many categories, and a retail market dominated by two major chains (Migros and Coop) with limited discount competition.

## The store-type decision matters more than the country

Within any given country, the single biggest lever is which type of store you actually shop at. Discount chains (Lidl and Aldi across much of Europe, Mercadona in Spain) routinely run 20-40% cheaper than premium or convenience-format supermarkets for a comparable basket, and local wet markets or bazaars in Southeast Asia, Georgia, and much of Southern Europe undercut supermarkets further still on produce, though at some cost in convenience and consistency. Expats who default to the supermarket nearest their apartment, without comparing it to a discount chain a few minutes further away, routinely leave real money on the table every month.

## Cooking at home is still the biggest lever of all

Across every country in this comparison, the gap between cooking at home and eating out remains the largest controllable factor in a food budget — commonly estimated at around a 40% saving versus a habit of regular restaurant meals or delivery, even accounting for the time cost of shopping and cooking. That gap tends to widen, not narrow, in expensive markets like Switzerland or Singapore, where restaurant meals carry a bigger premium over home cooking than in cheaper destinations — meaning the incentive to cook at home actually gets stronger, not weaker, the more expensive your destination is.

## Batch cooking and seasonal produce compound the savings

Beyond the store-type decision, aligning your meal planning with what's actually in season locally tends to cut costs further, since imported off-season produce carries a real premium almost everywhere, while local seasonal produce is often the cheapest item on the shelf. Batch cooking — preparing several days of meals at once — adds a further layer of savings on top of the basic "cook at home" gap, since it reduces per-meal preparation time and lets you buy staple ingredients (rice, pulses, proteins) in larger, cheaper quantities rather than small, expensive top-up trips.

## Currency and import-good traps

One thing that catches new arrivals off guard everywhere: imported or "home country" branded goods (specific cereals, cheeses, sauces) can cost multiples of the local equivalent, and building a shopping list around familiar home-country brands rather than adapting to local staples is one of the quietest ways expat grocery budgets balloon in the first few months. Budgeting a temporary premium for the adjustment period, while deliberately shifting toward local staples, is usually the fastest way to bring monthly food costs back in line with the ranges above.
