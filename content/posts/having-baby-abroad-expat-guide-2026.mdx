Having a baby while living abroad involves two separate questions that get conflated constantly: what nationality does the child acquire, and what does the birth itself actually cost and involve logistically. They deserve separate answers.

## Jus soli is rarer than people assume

Unconditional birthright [citizenship](/blog/irish-citizenship-by-descent-guide-2026) — where simply being born on a country's soil grants [citizenship](/blog/eu-citizenship-guide-2026) regardless of the parents' immigration status — is the rule in the United States and Canada, and across most of the Americas including Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina. It is the exception, not the norm, almost everywhere else. Most of Europe, including Germany, France, and the UK, uses a modified system where a child born on national soil generally needs at least one parent who is a citizen or long-term legal resident to acquire [citizenship](/blog/french-citizenship-naturalization-guide-2026) automatically — pure jus sanguinis ([citizenship](/blog/german-citizenship-guide-2026) by descent from a parent) governs Germany's default rule for foreign parents, meaning a baby born in Berlin to two foreign nationals typically does not become German at birth. If citizenship planning is part of your reason for giving birth in a specific country, verify the exact rule for that country's current law — "birth tourism" citizenship strategies only work in the true jus soli countries, and even the US has periodically seen political proposals to narrow this, though none has changed the underlying constitutional rule (14th Amendment) as of 2026.

## The cost gap is enormous, and insurance status changes everything

Giving birth in a US hospital without insurance commonly runs USD 10,000-30,000 for an uncomplicated delivery, escalating well beyond that with a C-section or NICU stay — a figure that regularly shocks expats and medical tourists who assume US healthcare pricing resembles their home country's. By contrast, private hospital maternity packages in Thailand, a long-standing medical tourism destination, commonly run USD 2,000-5,000 including neonatal care add-ons, at facilities that routinely serve international patients with English-speaking staff. Countries with strong public healthcare systems present a third model entirely: in Germany, statutory health insurance (Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung) covers maternity care fully for insured members, with essentially no out-of-pocket delivery cost — the practical planning question there isn't cost, it's confirming your Krankenkasse enrollment and coverage status well before the due date.

## Parental leave varies from generous to nonexistent

Sweden offers one of the most generous parental leave systems in the world: 480 days of paid parental leave per child, shared between parents, with a portion reserved specifically for each parent on a "use it or lose it" basis to encourage shared caregiving. That's a policy headline worth verifying against your specific employer, though — the statutory floor and what a specific employer tops up (or doesn't) can differ, and eligibility often depends on prior Swedish social insurance enrollment, not just residency. Expats moving to Sweden shortly before a birth should confirm their own eligibility rather than assuming the full 480-day benefit applies immediately.

## Register the birth with your embassy inside the window

Whatever citizenship your child acquires by birth in-country, registering the birth with your own embassy or consulate (for Consular Report of Birth Abroad, in the US system, or the equivalent elsewhere) is what actually gets that additional citizenship formally recognized and a passport issued. Most consulates specify a recommended or required registration window — often encouraging registration within the child's first year, sometimes with more flexible windows for older children but added documentation burden. Missing the window doesn't usually forfeit citizenship claimed by descent, but it does turn a routine paperwork appointment into a longer proof-of-relationship process later, so book the consular appointment as early as your local birth certificate and hospital paperwork allow.

## Prenatal care access shapes the whole experience

Insurance and residency status determine how smoothly prenatal care unfolds well before delivery day. In countries with public systems tied to legal residency — Germany and much of the EU among them — enrolling in the national or statutory insurance system early in pregnancy, not after, ensures continuity of prenatal appointments and avoids gaps that private travel insurance typically excludes for pregnancy-related care altogether. In countries relying more heavily on private hospital care, like Thailand or the US, confirm what a maternity package actually includes (routine prenatal visits, ultrasounds, delivery, and the newborn's initial checkups) before assuming a quoted delivery price covers the full pregnancy, since many private packages price prenatal visits separately from the delivery itself.
