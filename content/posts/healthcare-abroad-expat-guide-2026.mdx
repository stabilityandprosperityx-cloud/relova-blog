## Public, Private, or International: The Core Decision

Healthcare is one of the few relocation decisions where getting it wrong can be genuinely dangerous rather than just expensive, so it's worth treating as a first-order planning item rather than something to sort out after you land. The core decision most expats face is a three-way split: rely on a destination country's public system (if you're eligible), buy comprehensive private local insurance, or carry an international health insurance policy that follows you across borders.

## SafetyWing: The Entry-Level Nomad Option

**SafetyWing's** Nomad Insurance is the most commonly cited entry-level option for younger, healthier travelers, with starter pricing often discussed around **$56 a month** for applicants under 40 — a genuinely low price point that reflects both its risk pool and its coverage limits, which lean toward emergency and urgent care rather than comprehensive ongoing treatment or elective procedures. It's a reasonable stopgap for someone testing out nomad life, but it's worth reading the actual policy limits rather than assuming "nomad insurance" means "full health insurance."

## Cigna Global and Allianz Care: Comprehensive Coverage

**Cigna Global** sits at a different tier entirely, with plans often modeled in the **$150–400 a month** range depending on age, coverage area, and whether you include outpatient or maternity add-ons. This is closer to genuine comprehensive international coverage — the kind that includes routine care, specialist referrals, and broader hospital networks, not just emergency stabilization. **Allianz Care** runs in a similar band, often modeled around **$200–500 a month**, and both Cigna and Allianz are frequently used by expats who want a single policy that works consistently regardless of which country they're currently in, which matters a lot for people who split time across multiple countries in a year.

## How the Destination Country Shapes Your Choice

Where you land in the public-versus-private decision often depends more on the destination than on your own preference. **Germany**, for example, runs a statutory health insurance system that most residents — including many long-term expats on qualifying visas — are legally required to join, with contributions commonly around **14.6% of salary**, split between employee and employer, covering a genuinely comprehensive standard of care. This isn't optional the way private international insurance is for a digital nomad; it's baked into German residency requirements for most work-based visas. Contrast that with somewhere like **Thailand**, where private consultations with a doctor commonly cost as little as **$30–80** out of pocket — low enough that many expats simply pay cash for routine care rather than carrying a formal policy at all, reserving insurance for catastrophic or hospitalization scenarios instead.

## The Budgeting Mistake: Treating Healthcare as One Line Item

The budgeting mistake to avoid is treating "healthcare cost" as a single line item when it's really three separate questions: what does routine care cost in this country if you pay out of pocket, what does a comprehensive insurance policy cost if you want one, and what's actually covered by any public system you might be eligible to join based on your visa category. These three numbers can differ by an order of magnitude, and conflating them leads to either wildly overbudgeting (buying premium international insurance in a country where local care is cheap and good) or dangerously underbudgeting (assuming you'll just pay cash somewhere that a serious illness or accident could produce a bill running into tens of thousands of dollars).

## Building Lean, Realistic, and Stress-Case Budget Scenarios

A sensible approach is to build three budget scenarios rather than one: a lean scenario assuming minimal coverage and mostly out-of-pocket routine care, a realistic scenario with a mid-tier policy matched to your actual risk profile (age, existing conditions, family size), and a stress-case scenario that assumes a serious medical event requiring hospitalization or evacuation. Layer a **15–25% contingency** on top of whichever scenario you plan around, since healthcare costs are one of the categories most prone to surprise even with a policy in place — deductibles, exclusions, and pre-authorization requirements all have a way of showing up at the worst possible moment.

## What to Verify Before You Actually Need the Policy

Whatever you choose, verify the policy explicitly names your destination country as covered territory, check whether it includes medical evacuation (a genuinely important feature in countries with limited specialist care), and confirm pre-existing condition exclusions before you need to rely on the policy rather than after.
