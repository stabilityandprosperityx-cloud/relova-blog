The single most common misunderstanding about an International Driving Permit (IDP) is that it's a license in its own right. It isn't — it's a standardized, multilingual translation of your existing home license, issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention on Road Traffic (or the older 1926/1968 Vienna framework, depending on the issuing and destination country), and it's only valid alongside your original license, never instead of it. Roughly 150 countries recognize some version of these conventions, though which one a given country honors — and for how long — varies enough that checking the destination-specific rule before you travel is worth the five minutes.

## Getting one is cheap and fast if you go through the right issuer

In the United States, AAA and the American Automobile Touring Alliance are the only two organizations authorized by the State Department to issue IDPs, and AAA's fee has stayed remarkably stable at around USD 20 for the permit itself, with total out-of-pocket (once you add two passport-style photos, typically USD 10-17 depending on where you get them taken) landing closer to USD 30-40. You do not need to be a AAA member, and most branches issue it same-day or by mail within about a week. In the UK, the Post Office is the designated issuer, and the fee is a modest GBP 5.50 — a genuinely different tier of hassle compared to some countries where the process requires an in-person visit to a government transport authority. Whichever issuer you use, the permit is valid for exactly one year from your requested start date and cannot be issued more than six months ahead of that date, and — critically — it automatically becomes invalid the moment your underlying home license expires, since it's a translation of that document, not an independent one.

## Where you actually need one

Japan is one of the strictest and most commonly cited cases: rental agencies and, in practice, police during any traffic stop expect to see a valid IDP alongside your home license, and driving without one when required can void your rental insurance entirely. The UAE similarly expects an IDP for most car rentals, though nationals of some countries (including much of the EU) can convert directly to a UAE license without one under bilateral arrangements — worth checking with the specific emirate's traffic authority rather than assuming. Inside the EU and wider EEA, the picture flips: an EU-issued driving license is generally valid to drive across the whole bloc without any IDP at all, since EU licenses are mutually recognized under EU law — the IDP need mostly disappears once you're moving license-to-license within the EU/EEA. Germany illustrates the exception that trips people up: a non-EU license, even alongside an IDP, is often only valid for the first six months of residence, after which Germany requires converting to a German license (Führerschein) — a process whose difficulty varies sharply depending on whether your home country has a reciprocal agreement with Germany.

## The practical takeaway

Get the IDP before you leave home — it cannot be issued once you're already abroad in most cases — and treat it as a companion document, not a substitute: pack the physical original of your home license every time, since a photocopy or a photo on your phone doesn't satisfy the requirement in any country that checks.
