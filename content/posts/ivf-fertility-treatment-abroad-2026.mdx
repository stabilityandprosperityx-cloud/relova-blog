An American IVF cycle without insurance coverage runs **USD 15,000–30,000** before medication, and most US employer plans still exclude fertility treatment entirely or cap it at a lifetime dollar figure that one cycle can exhaust. That gap, not wanderlust, is what sends people to Prague, Athens, and Barcelona. It is worth being precise about what "IVF abroad" actually buys, because sticker prices between clinics quoting the same country can differ by more than the price of the flight.

## Where the real savings sit

Own-egg IVF in the Czech Republic runs roughly **EUR 2,100–3,500** per cycle, and Greece sits close behind at **EUR 2,500–3,500** — both a fraction of the US figure even after flights and two weeks of short-let accommodation. Spain, despite being the most established fertility-tourism market in Europe with dense ESHRE-accredited lab networks, is pricier at **EUR 3,600–6,700**, reflecting a country that built its industry on donor-egg volume rather than budget positioning. North Cyprus advertises **EUR 2,500–3,000**, often bundled with donor-egg packages that would cost far more in the UK, where donor-egg anonymity rules and shorter donor waitlists make it a common referral point for British patients. None of these headline numbers include stimulation medication, which can add **EUR 1,000–2,500** depending on protocol and your ovarian reserve, or a frozen embryo transfer if the fresh cycle does not result in a viable pregnancy — budget for at least one FET when you model total spend, not just the number the clinic leads with in its marketing email.

## Donor programs and legal variation

Donor-egg cycles cost meaningfully more everywhere — typically **EUR 4,000–8,500** in the countries above — and the legal framework around donor anonymity, compensation caps, and maximum donor age varies by country in ways that matter more than price. Spain and Greece permit anonymous donation with regulated compensation; Czechia is similar. This is the detail that decides waitlists: anonymous, well-compensated donor pools in Spain tend to move faster than countries where compensation caps are lower and fewer women donate. If a specific phenotype match matters to you, ask the clinic for their actual current waitlist in weeks, not a marketing claim about "extensive donor banks."

## Surrogacy is a different legal category entirely

If your path involves gestational surrogacy rather than IVF with your own or donor eggs, do not treat any pricing you see for post-Soviet or Central Asian destinations as current without direct legal counsel in both that jurisdiction and your home state or country — surrogacy legality, parentage recognition, and exit-visa rules for the resulting child have shifted unpredictably across several countries that marketed themselves as surrogacy hubs in past years, and a legacy price quote is not a legal guarantee that the arrangement will result in a passport you can bring the child home on.

## Building the actual budget

Beyond the clinic invoice, plan for round-trip flights timed around your cycle (sometimes two trips — one for baseline testing, one for the transfer), 10–14 days of accommodation near the clinic, local bloodwork and monitoring scans if your home clinic is coordinating remotely, and a contingency fund for a second cycle, since live-birth rates per cycle for most age groups remain well under 50%. Ask every clinic for its live-birth-per-embryo-transfer rate, not its generic "success rate," and ask whether that figure is age-stratified — a 38-year-old's realistic odds and a 29-year-old's are not the same number, and a clinic that quotes one blended figure to everyone is not giving you data you can plan around.

## Insurance, travel timing, and paperwork

Most travel insurance excludes fertility treatment as a pre-existing elective procedure, so budget separately for medical evacuation cover for the treatment window itself, and confirm with your home country's tax authority whether cross-border medical expenses of this kind are deductible — several countries allow it, but the documentation requirements (itemized foreign invoices, proof of medical necessity) are stricter than for domestic care. Book flights only after your baseline hormone panel clears, since stimulation timing is not flexible once it starts, and a missed monitoring appointment abroad can cost you the cycle.
