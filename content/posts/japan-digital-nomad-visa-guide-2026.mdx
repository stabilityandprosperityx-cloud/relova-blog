Japan's digital nomad visa is easy to misread as a residency track because of how it gets talked about online, but it is formally a Designated Activities status, created under Immigration Notice No. 53 and launched in March 2024. It is a short-stay remote-work permission, not a step toward residency, and understanding that distinction up front will save you from planning a life in Japan around a visa that was never designed to support one.

## Who qualifies, and the income and nationality limits

Applicants need to document roughly ¥10,000,000 in annual income — somewhere around $67,000–70,000 depending on the exchange rate at the time you apply — paid entirely by employers or clients located outside Japan. This is a strict rule, not a guideline: you may not work for Japanese companies or invoice Japanese clients while on this status, which rules it out for anyone hoping to pick up local freelance work on the side to supplement their income while in the country. Eligibility is also nationality-limited to roughly 49 to 50 countries, specifically those that both enjoy visa-free entry to Japan and have a tax treaty in force with Japan — the list includes the United States, Canada, the UK, Australia, Singapore, and South Korea, among others, but plenty of countries are notably absent. Mandatory private health insurance is also required, with coverage of at least ¥10,000,000 — standard domestic Japanese health insurance is not part of this status since holders are not enrolled in the national health insurance system the way long-term residents are.

## Six months, no renewal, and a mandatory cool-down

The single most important limitation to internalize before you plan around this visa: permission runs for up to six months and cannot be renewed or extended. Once your six months are up, you must spend at least six months physically outside Japan before you are eligible to apply again. In practical terms, this makes the visa suitable for a genuine workation — an extended stay, not a foothold toward a longer-term life in Japan. If your actual goal is eventual residency, this status will not get you there on its own; it exists in a separate lane from Japan's work visas, spouse visas, or highly skilled professional points-based system, and switching between them is not straightforward. ## Life without a Residence Card

The detail that surprises the most people in practice is that this status does not come with a Zairyu Card (Residence Card), the identity document that unlocks most of daily administrative life in Japan. Without one, opening a standard bank account, signing a conventional two-year apartment lease, or getting a normal mobile phone contract is frequently blocked outright by banks, landlords, and carriers who are set up to require it. The practical workaround most nomads use is monthly or serviced apartments, which are priced and structured for exactly this kind of short, undocumented stay, combined with international banking or a travel-friendly account rather than attempting to open a local one. Budget for this friction in both time and cost before you arrive — it is a meaningfully different logistics setup than a standard long-term move to Japan, and trying to force local, permanent-resident-style arrangements onto a six-month permit tends to waste time you do not have.

## Fees, processing, family, and tax

Visa fees have historically been modest — ¥3,000 for single entry and ¥6,000 for multiple entry — but from July 2026, updated fee schedules are commonly cited putting these at roughly ¥15,000 for single entry and ¥30,000 for multiple entry, with a transit fee around ¥3,500; confirm the live fee at application time since fee schedules can be adjusted. Processing timelines range widely, anywhere from five business days on the fast end to two months on the slow end, with most applicants seeing something closer to one to three months in practice. Spouses and children can accompany the primary applicant under a separate sub-status created under Notice No. 54, but each dependent must independently meet their own eligibility requirements rather than simply being attached to the primary applicant's file. On tax: stays under 183 days combined with entirely foreign-source income are typically outside the scope of Japanese income tax, but this determination depends on your specific facts and treaty position, so confirm with a tax adviser familiar with both your home country and Japan rather than assuming the general rule applies cleanly to your situation.
