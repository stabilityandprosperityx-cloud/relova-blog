Montenegro's Adriatic coast splits into two genuinely different personalities once you get past the postcard photos, and the choice between them says more about your lifestyle preferences than your budget. [Kotor's](/blog/move-to-kotor-montenegro-guide-2026) UNESCO-listed bay draws sailors, remote workers who want quiet, and a noticeably calmer winter community, with one-bedroom rents typically running €500–900. Budva, a beach town of roughly twenty thousand residents just down the coast, parties considerably harder — its nightlife scene is the biggest draw for a younger crowd — with rents actually a touch lower at €400–800, offset by more summer noise and a livelier, less residential feel through the high season.

## Vibe first, then budget

The practical way to choose between these two towns is to be honest about what kind of evenings you want. Kotor's old town is a warren of stone alleys wrapped around a fjord-like bay, and it empties out noticeably once the cruise ships leave for the day — appealing if you want a slower pace and don't mind a smaller social circle outside peak season. Budva is the opposite: a genuine party-beach-town atmosphere that runs late into the night for much of the summer, with a much larger transient population of tourists and seasonal workers cycling through. Neither is objectively better; they simply suit different people, and visiting both in the shoulder season (rather than deep summer, when both towns are at their busiest and least representative) is the best way to get an honest read on daily life.

Tivat and its Porto Montenegro marina development sit between the two geographically and add a third, more polished option — one-bedroom rents here run higher, typically €700–1,200, reflecting the yacht-set, higher-income crowd the development was built to attract. It is worth a look if budget allows and you want marina-adjacent amenities and a slightly more international, less backpacker-oriented feel than either Kotor or Budva.

## The visa and tax picture

Montenegro's [digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-montenegro-guide-2026) framing commonly cites a minimum monthly income requirement around €800, though this figure appears in policy discussions rather than as a single, universally confirmed number, so it is worth verifying the current threshold directly against the Ministry of Interior's published requirements before you plan around it — Montenegro's immigration rules have been evolving as the country continues its EU accession process. Personal income tax in Montenegro is genuinely competitive by European standards, running roughly nine to fifteen percent depending on which income bracket applies, which is part of why the country has attracted a growing remote-work community even without an especially generous nomad visa income floor.

One planning detail that is easy to overlook: Montenegro is not in the Schengen Area, despite sitting geographically among several Schengen countries and having its currency pegged to the euro (it uses the euro unilaterally without formal Eurozone membership). That means your time in Montenegro does not count against a Schengen 90/180-day allowance, which can actually be useful for people splitting time across the region — but it also means Montenegro has its own separate visa-free allowance and entry rules to track, distinct from whatever Schengen calendar you are running elsewhere. EU accession talks continue to advance, but Montenegro operates under its own immigration framework for now, and that is unlikely to change quickly.

## What it actually costs to live there

All-in, a realistic nomad monthly budget across Kotor, Budva, or Tivat lands somewhere around €1,000–1,800, including a car — genuinely useful along this stretch of coast, since public transport between towns is limited and a car opens up day trips to smaller villages, the Lovćen and Durmitor national parks, and neighboring Croatia and Albania without much added driving time. Groceries and everyday goods run noticeably cheaper than in most of Western Europe, but imported goods and anything oriented toward tourists in Kotor's old town or Budva's beachfront carries a real markup, particularly in peak summer months when prices across the whole coast climb with the seasonal population. Winter, by contrast, is markedly quieter and cheaper in both towns, and is worth considering if your priority is cost of living over social scene.
