Lanzarote is a Spanish island in every legal sense — same visa system, same residency rules as the mainland — but its Canary Islands tax status makes the cost-of-living math genuinely different, and that gap is worth understanding before you pick a neighborhood.

## IGIC replaces [Spain](/blog/gran-canaria-expat-long-term-guide-2026)'s mainland VAT entirely

The Canary Islands sit outside the EU VAT area and run their own indirect tax, **IGIC**, at a general rate of **7%**, versus mainland [Spain](/blog/lisbon-vs-barcelona-expat-guide-2026)'s standard 21% IVA. That difference shows up everywhere — groceries, restaurant bills, car purchases, most retail — and it is the single biggest structural reason a long-term Lanzarote budget runs lighter than an equivalent mainland Spanish city despite similar rents. It is not a personal tax break tied to your residency status; it applies to everyone shopping on the island, resident or not, and it is worth explicitly asking whether a listed price already includes IGIC before comparing it to a mainland figure that includes 21% IVA.

## The non-lucrative visa income floor for 2026

Non-EU citizens relocating to Lanzarote without local employment typically use Spain's **non-lucrative visa (NLV)**, and the income requirement is pegged to Spain's IPREM index. For 2026, IPREM is frozen at **€600 a month** (€7,200 annually), and the NLV threshold sits at **400% of annual IPREM** — **€28,800 a year, roughly €2,400 a month** — for the main applicant, plus an additional **€7,200 a year** per dependent. A couple relocating together should budget for the combined figure, not just the primary applicant's number, since the dependent addition applies per person, not per household. Confirm the current-year IPREM figure directly with the consulate before filing, since it is set annually and this guide's number reflects where it stood as of 2026.

## Puerto del Carmen versus Arrecife versus Costa Teguise

**Puerto del Carmen** carries the island's deepest expat infrastructure — English-speaking clinics, a long-established British and Scandinavian resident community, and the most reliable stock of long-term (rather than holiday-let) rentals — with one-beds typically running **€600–900** a month. **Arrecife**, the working capital, is the value play: **€550–800** for a comparable one-bed, with the trade-off being a more local, less English-oriented daily life and a longer drive to the best beaches. **Costa Teguise** sits in between on price (**€600–950**) but offers the most consistently walkable beach-town layout of the three, which matters if you are not planning to keep a car. All three markets get materially tighter and pricier from roughly June through September as holiday-let demand crowds out long-term supply — signing a 12-month lease outside that window gets meaningfully better terms.

## Budgeting beyond rent

A realistic all-in monthly budget for a single remote worker, rent included, lands around **€1,500–2,300**, with the wide range driven mostly by car ownership (near-essential outside Puerto del Carmen and Arrecife's walkable cores) and private health insurance, which most non-EU residents need regardless of visa route since the NLV does not grant automatic access to Spain's public health system. Building in Spain's own residency-renewal cadence matters too: the NLV renews after one year, then two more years, before permanent residency becomes available at the five-year mark — treat year one as a trial with real paperwork attached, not a settled decision.
