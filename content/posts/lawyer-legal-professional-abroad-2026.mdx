A law degree does not travel the way an engineering degree does. Every jurisdiction gatekeeps who can stand up in its courts, and most gatekeep who can even call themselves a "lawyer" in local advertising. Working abroad as legal counsel in 2026 comes down to three real lanes — in-house counsel who never needs local court rights, full local requalification, or an advisory role that deliberately stays out of the courtroom — and mixing them up is the single most common mistake foreign-qualified lawyers make before they move.

## Dubai: tax-free pay, no local bar required

Dubai's in-house legal market is built for exactly this. Multinationals, DIFC-based funds, and free-zone companies routinely advertise in-house counsel packages in the **$100,000–200,000 tax-free** range in recruiter decks, and the UAE genuinely has no personal income tax on salaried pay. What that figure hides is the bonus structure: a large slice is often deferred or subject to clawback clauses tied to retention, so read the offer letter's bonus mechanics before you anchor a budget to the headline number. Critically, none of this requires UAE bar admission — in-house counsel and DIFC-registered practitioners advising on non-UAE law do not need to requalify locally. Appearing before onshore UAE courts is a different, much narrower lane reserved for UAE-licensed advocates, almost always Emirati nationals or long-established local firms.

## Singapore's Foreign Practitioner Examination is the real bottleneck

Singapore looks similarly open until you try to litigate. Full admission to practice Singapore law across all areas runs through the Legal Profession Act's local pathways, and the route open to foreign-qualified lawyers — the **Foreign Practitioner Examination (FPE)**, administered by the Singapore Institute of Legal Education — is a genuinely hard exam covering evidence, criminal procedure, civil procedure, and professional conduct, sat only in Singapore. As of 2026 the FPE is under a comprehensive review following its 2025 sitting, with the scope of the next exam not yet published — treat any FPE prep timeline you're quoted as provisional until SILE confirms it. Most multinationals sidestep the whole question by hiring foreign lawyers registered to advise on foreign or international law and policy work, which needs no FPE and no local call to the bar, just registration under the Legal Profession Act's foreign lawyer provisions.

## The UK replaced QLTS with the SQE

If England and Wales is the target, the old Qualified Lawyers Transfer Scheme is gone. Since September 2021, foreign-qualified lawyers requalify as solicitors through the **Solicitors Qualifying Examination (SQE)** — SQE1 (legal knowledge, two exams) and SQE2 (practical skills), plus two years of qualifying work experience, which can often be satisfied by prior practice in your home jurisdiction rather than starting a training contract from zero. It is cheaper and faster for most foreign lawyers than the old QLTS route was, but SQE1's pass rates run meaningfully below what candidates expect coming from a jurisdiction with a different exam culture — budget for a proper prep course, not a weekend refresher.

## When an LLM actually pays for itself

An LLM pivot makes sense in one specific case: your target jurisdiction requires a local degree-equivalent credential before you can sit its bar or requalification exam at all, not just as a résumé polish. Programs in the US, UK, and Singapore commonly run **$20,000–60,000** in tuition alone, before a year of reduced or zero income while you study. If your actual goal is an in-house Dubai role or a foreign-registered advisory seat in Singapore, an LLM is usually the expensive way to solve a problem neither market requires you to solve — verify with the specific employer or bar body what credential they actually gate on before committing to the degree.
