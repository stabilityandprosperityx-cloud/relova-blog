Moving to Lisbon does not pause your US tax obligations — America taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence, and the paperwork stack that follows is the part that catches newcomers who assumed "I pay Portuguese tax now" was the whole story. Here is the mechanical reality specific to being American in Portugal, not the general expat-life pitch.

## The exclusion that does not erase the filing requirement

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion for the 2026 tax year is **$132,900** per qualifying person (the IRS adjusts it annually for inflation, so re-check the figure each filing season rather than reusing last year's number). It excludes earned income — salary, self-employment income from active work — up to that ceiling if you meet either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test (330 full days outside the US in a 12-month period). It does **not** exclude passive income: dividends, capital gains, rental income, or pension distributions still flow onto your US return, and Portuguese tax paid on those categories is credited via the Foreign Tax Credit rather than excluded. You still file a US return every year regardless of whether the FEIE zeroes out your US liability — nonfiling is a paperwork failure the IRS can penalize even when no tax was actually owed.

## FBAR: the form most people forget until it's late

If the combined balance across all your foreign financial accounts — Portuguese checking, savings, a Millennium BCP account, Revolut, even a joint account you barely use — exceeded **$10,000 at any single point during the calendar year**, you must file FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR) by April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15. This is an aggregate test across all accounts, not a per-account threshold, and it is a Treasury filing separate from your IRS return with its own penalty regime. People who would never have $10,000 in one account routinely cross the line once a Lisbon rental deposit, a Wise transfer buffer, and a local checking account are added together.

## FATCA and why some Portuguese banks are slow to say yes

The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) requires foreign banks to report American accountholders to the IRS, and the compliance burden this creates is exactly why some Portuguese banks are cautious about opening accounts for US citizens, or ask for extra documentation (a US taxpayer ID, a W-9, sometimes a higher minimum balance). In practice, Americans in Lisbon commonly run a two-bank setup: a Portuguese bank like Millennium BCP or Novo Banco for local salary, rent payments, and Multibanco access, paired with Wise or a similar multi-currency account for cross-border transfers, since Wise's FATCA compliance framework is generally more Americanized and the onboarding friction is lower.

## What professional filing actually costs

Because the interaction between FEIE, the Foreign Tax Credit, the US-Portugal tax treaty, and Portugal's own residency-based taxation is genuinely easy to get wrong, most Americans in Lisbon use a specialist expat preparer rather than a general accountant on either side of the Atlantic. Firms built specifically around American-abroad filing typically price a standard return in the **$400–800** range per season, more if you have Portuguese self-employment income, a company structure, or PFIC-flagged foreign mutual funds and ETFs — a category that catches out Americans who keep investing through a Portuguese or European brokerage without realizing US tax law treats most non-US pooled funds as punitively taxed PFICs. Buying only US-domiciled ETFs through an international broker is the standard workaround, and it is worth confirming with a preparer before you make your first investment, not after.
