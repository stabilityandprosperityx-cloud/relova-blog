**Mallorca as a long-term base** works differently than Mallorca as a two-week holiday, and the gap between those two experiences is where most new arrivals get their budget wrong.

## The island empties out, and your costs should too

Mallorca's population effectively doubles in July and August, and rental listings, restaurant prices, and even supermarket parking reflect that surge for roughly four months a year. A one-bedroom in Palma's Santa Catalina or El Terreno neighborhoods commonly runs EUR 900–1,400 a month on a standard 11-month or annual contract, but short lets during peak season can price the same unit at double that, which is why long-term residents almost universally sign winter or annual leases rather than chasing month-to-month flexibility. Puerto Pollença and the northern coast run slightly cheaper at EUR 800–1,300 for a comparable one-bedroom, while Sóller, tucked in its own valley with a direct train line to Palma, is the best value of the three at EUR 700–1,200 — though its microclimate means more rain in winter than the rest of the island. Outside the three summer months, restaurants, gyms, and service businesses in tourist-facing towns often reduce hours or close entirely, so anyone planning to live there year-round should scout a target neighborhood in January or February, not August, to see what actually stays open.

## Two visa routes lead to the same island, with different income math

[Spain](/blog/nomad-visa-europe-comparison)'s Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard route for people living off savings, remote non-Spanish income, or a pension, and requires proof of at least 400% of Spain's IPREM index — EUR 28,800 a year for a single applicant in 2026, plus roughly EUR 7,200 for each additional dependent. Spain's separate [Digital Nomad Visa](/blog/move-to-barcelona-nomad-guide-2026), introduced under the 2023 Startups Law, is aimed specifically at people working remotely for a non-Spanish employer or client base and carries its own income threshold tied to the national minimum wage (SMI) rather than IPREM — generally a higher bar than the NLV, and it requires evidence of an existing remote-work contract or client relationship, something the NLV does not ask for. Neither visa grants the right to work for a Spanish employer, and both require private health insurance with no co-pays before the consulate will process the application. Applications go through the Spanish consulate in your home jurisdiction, and processing commonly takes 8–12 weeks, so it is worth applying well before any lease deadline.

## Property versus renting is a real fork, not a formality

Mallorca has a large and well-established foreign resident population — Germans alone are commonly estimated at over 45,000 residents on the island, concentrated heavily around Palma, the southwest coast, and the port towns — which means the property market is unusually developed for non-resident buyers, with English- and German-speaking notaries, gestores, and mortgage brokers readily available. Buying carries transfer tax (ITP) of roughly 8–11% depending on price band, plus notary and registration fees on top, so budget an additional 10–13% over the purchase price for a resale property. Renting long-term instead avoids that upfront cost but means competing with the annual contract market that tightens every spring as landlords weigh a guaranteed 11-month tenant against the far larger returns of summer short-lets — securing a lease by February or March, before that seasonal calculation kicks in, meaningfully improves your odds and your price.

## Budget for a full year, not a good month

A realistic all-in monthly budget for a single remote worker or retiree living comfortably — rent, private health insurance, utilities, groceries, and a car or scooter — lands around EUR 2,000–3,000 depending on neighborhood and lifestyle, with Palma running toward the top of that range and inland or northern towns toward the bottom. Car ownership is close to a necessity outside central Palma, since bus service to smaller towns thins out sharply in winter when tourist-route frequency drops.
