Marketing is one of the harder professions to relocate on a pure sponsorship model, because it rarely appears on shortage-occupation lists the way engineering or healthcare does. That doesn't make it impossible — it means the realistic paths split into three lanes: employer sponsorship into a role that already pays above a country's salary threshold, a specialist visa for people with in-demand digital or performance-marketing skills, or a freelance/consulting setup that sidesteps sponsorship entirely by working with clients rather than an employer.

## The UK: sponsorship works, but the salary bar matters more than the job title

The UK's Skilled Worker visa doesn't ask whether "marketing" is shortage-listed; it asks whether the specific role clears the going-rate threshold for its occupation code (marketing and PR professionals typically sit in SOC code 2472/3543-range roles) and the general salary floor, which has moved up several times in recent years — check the current figure on GOV.UK before assuming an offer qualifies, since employers routinely under-price marketing roles relative to the threshold. Senior marketing, brand, and growth roles in London commonly land in the GBP 45,000–75,000 range, with performance-marketing and CRM specialists at agencies or scale-ups often clearing the upper end faster than in-house generalist roles. Sponsorship is legal and common, but competitive: agencies without a sponsor licence can't sponsor at all, so it's worth confirming licence status before pursuing a specific employer.

## The Netherlands: the 30% ruling changes the math for skilled hires

Amsterdam is one of Europe's more marketing-friendly hubs specifically because of the Highly Skilled Migrant (HSM) route, paired with the 30% ruling — a tax facility that lets qualifying employees receive up to 30% of salary tax-free for a period, though the ruling has been scaled back in recent years and is now capped and time-limited, so treat any specific percentage as something to confirm with the employer's HR/tax team rather than assume it applies in full. HSM sponsorship requires the employer to be a recognized sponsor and the salary to clear an annual threshold that's indexed each year. Marketing salaries in Amsterdam at HSM-eligible employers typically run EUR 50,000–75,000, concentrated in tech, e-commerce, and consumer brands with EU headquarters there.

## Singapore: the Employment Pass salary bar is the real gatekeeper

Singapore doesn't sponsor by job title either — the Employment Pass (EP) requires a minimum fixed monthly salary that rises with age and is reviewed periodically by the Ministry of Manpower, plus a COMPASS points-based assessment covering salary, qualifications, diversity, and local hiring support. Marketing roles at regional HQs (many consumer, fintech, and tech firms run APAC marketing out of Singapore) commonly pay SGD 70,000–110,000 annually for mid-to-senior positions, comfortably above the EP floor, but junior roles can sit close to the threshold and are riskier bets for sponsorship.

## Dubai and the UAE: employer-route is standard, and it's usually free to the employee

The UAE runs almost entirely on employer-sponsored work permits tied to a company's trade licence, with the Golden Visa as a longer-term option for higher earners or company owners rather than the typical entry route. Marketing salaries for employer-sponsored roles commonly land AED 15,000–30,000 per month depending on seniority and industry (real estate, hospitality, and fintech tend to pay toward the top of that band). There's no personal income tax, which meaningfully changes the net-pay comparison against London or Amsterdam even when gross figures look similar.

## Freelance and remote: the fallback that doesn't require a sponsor at all

A growing share of marketing professionals sidestep sponsorship entirely by working as freelancers or contractors for agencies and brands that don't need to employ them locally. Freelance digital marketing (paid media management, content strategy, email/CRM consulting, SEO) commonly nets USD 3,000–8,000 per month for experienced operators with an existing client base, and this income is portable across most digital nomad visa programs (Portugal, Spain, Croatia, and others) that require proof of remote income rather than a local job offer. The trade-off is inconsistent income and the added complexity of registering as self-employed and handling your own tax filings in whichever country you're tax-resident in.
