[Medellín](/blog/move-to-colombia-medellin) rewards people who get the boring logistics done in the first week and punishes people who let SIM cards, banking, and visa paperwork drag into month two. The good news is that almost none of it is expensive — the [Colombia](/blog/move-to-medellin-guide-2026)n government fee side of a nomad move here is genuinely cheap by global standards, which is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly.

## The Visa V income line moves with the minimum wage, so recheck it

[Colombia](/blog/medellin-vs-bogota-vs-cartagena-guide-2026)'s **Visa V (Nómadas Digitales)** requires proof of income at roughly **three times the national minimum monthly wage**, which for 2026 puts the bar around **COP 5,252,715** — approximately **€1,300 (roughly USD 1,300–1,400)** a month — shown through the last three months of bank statements matching your employment or client contracts. Because Colombia's minimum wage is renegotiated and typically raised every January, this threshold shifts annually; confirm the current figure with the consulate or Cancillería before submitting rather than trusting last year's number. The visa itself is valid for up to **two years** of continuous legal stay, a real upgrade over the old 180-day tourist-stamp shuffle most nomads used before the DNV existed.

## Government fees are almost an afterthought

Total official cost for the visa is close to **€150** — a non-refundable **€44** study fee plus a **€106** issuance fee charged only on approval — and the follow-on Cédula de Extranjería (foreign ID card), which you need for almost everything else on this list, runs roughly **€70** more. All-in government spend for a single applicant lands around **€220–270**, which is genuinely low next to comparable digital nomad visas in Europe.

## SIM and banking take an afternoon, not a week

A prepaid **Claro** SIM with a data plan is available same-day at any Claro store or kiosk for around **COP 25,000** for the SIM itself, plus whatever data package you choose on top — Movistar and Tigo are the other two national carriers if Claro coverage is weak in your specific building. Banking is the trickier piece: full Colombian bank accounts (Bancolombia, Davivienda) generally want a Cédula de Extranjería in hand first, so most nomads bridge the gap with **Nequi**, a mobile-first digital wallet that accepts a passport and is usable within the country almost immediately, then upgrade to a full bank account once the ID card comes through.

## El Poblado buys convenience, Laureles buys value

**El Poblado** is the default landing zone for a reason — coworking density, English-speaking clinics, and the highest concentration of short-term furnished rentals — and a one-bedroom there commonly runs **USD 600–1,000** a month. **Laureles**, a walkable, tree-lined neighborhood just west across the river, offers a comparable standard of living with more local character and less nightlife noise for **USD 500–800**, and it is the neighborhood most returning nomads say they wish they had picked first.

## Budget for the full picture, not just rent

A realistic all-in monthly burn — rent, food, transport on the Metro or in-app taxis, coworking, and a private health insurance policy (the visa does not require Colombia's public EPS system, but most nomads still need proof of coverage) — lands around **USD 1,200–2,000** depending on neighborhood and lifestyle. Build that number before committing to a lease; El Poblado's café-and-coworking convenience adds up faster than the headline rent figure suggests.
