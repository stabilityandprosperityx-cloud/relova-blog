**Medellin vs Bogota vs Cartagena** is, more than anything else, a climate decision before it is a cost decision. The three cities sit at wildly different elevations, and elevation in Colombia dictates weather far more than latitude does. Medellín's "eternal spring" reputation comes from sitting roughly **1,500–2,300 meters** up in the Aburrá Valley, which keeps temperatures in a narrow **18–28°C** band all year with no real winter or summer to speak of — a big part of why it has built the deepest expat and digital-nomad infrastructure of the three. Bogotá, by contrast, sits at **2,600 meters**, high enough that afternoon rain and highs of **7–18°C** are the norm rather than the exception; it is genuinely cold by tropical-country standards, and it is the single biggest reason first-time arrivals who pictured "tropical Colombia" end up disoriented or unhappy within the first month, even though the capital wins decisively on business opportunities, museums, and the quality of formal Spanish instruction available. Cartagena flips the script entirely: it's sea level, Caribbean, and hot — **28–35°C** with humidity high enough that air conditioning stops being optional and becomes a line item in every housing search. ## Climate as the deciding factor

If you have never lived somewhere hot and humid year-round, Cartagena is worth testing with a short stay before committing to a lease. The heat does not let up at night the way it does in many temperate cities, and daily life — walking to the market, waiting for a bus, sitting on a patio in the evening — is shaped by managing sun and humidity rather than avoiding cold. Most people who relocate to Cartagena full-time either adapt within a few months or decide it works better as a **2–3 day** trip layered onto a Medellín or Bogotá base than as a primary home. Medellín's mild, unchanging range is the easiest of the three to acclimate to for people coming from four-season countries — there is no real adjustment period because the weather barely varies week to week. Bogotá rewards people who actually like cooler, overcast climates or who are moving primarily for career, graduate study, or a serious immersion in Spanish rather than for lifestyle and beaches; treating it as a tropical posting is the most common planning mistake newcomers make.

## What each city actually costs

Medellín is generally the cheapest of the three for a comparable standard of living, typically running **20–30% below Cartagena** on a like-for-like basis, and a one-bedroom in Laureles can come in **30–40% cheaper** than an equivalent unit in Getsemaní, Cartagena's most popular expat-adjacent neighborhood. Electricity is one of the more surprising line items to get wrong: without air conditioning, a typical Medellín apartment runs **COP 80,000–150,000 a month**, while a comparable unit in Cartagena that needs A/C running most of the day can hit **COP 200,000–500,000 or more** — a gap that catches people who budget rent carefully but forget utilities scale with climate. El Poblado, Medellín's most internationalized district, has seen rents climb hard since 2020, with furnished two-bedroom apartments now commonly **$1,200–2,200 a month**, putting it in the same range as a mid-tier U.S. city rather than the "cheap Latin America" reputation the country still carries in some circles; Laureles remains noticeably cheaper while still offering solid infrastructure and walkability. Bogotá, meanwhile, typically runs **10–25% more expensive** than Medellín for an equivalent lifestyle, driven mostly by housing in the northern, expat-favored neighborhoods.

## Safety, healthcare, and staying connected

Medellín's transformation on safety is one of the most cited statistics in any conversation about the city, and for good reason: the homicide rate fell from **381 per 100,000 people in 1991**, at the height of the cartel era, to roughly **13 per 100,000 today** — a genuinely historic turnaround that underpins the city's entire modern reputation. Bogotá's safety picture is much more neighborhood-dependent than Medellín's citywide narrative; areas like Chapinero Alto, Usaquén, and Zona Rosa are considered solidly safe by expat standards, while parts of the city center and the south require more day-to-day awareness. Cartagena's risk profile skews toward petty crime rather than violence — pickpocketing and scams targeting tourists in the walled city and Getsemaní are more common concerns than the headline safety issues that shaped Medellín's past. On healthcare, Medellín has become something of a medical-tourism hub in its own right, anchored by internationally recognized hospitals like Pablo Tobón Uribe and Clínica Las Américas, and it's common for serious Cartagena cases to be referred to Medellín or Bogotá rather than treated locally. Internet infrastructure follows a similar pattern: fiber connectivity in the expat-heavy districts of Bogotá and Medellín is broadly comparable and reliable enough for remote work, while Cartagena is usable for most daily needs but noticeably a step behind the other two.
