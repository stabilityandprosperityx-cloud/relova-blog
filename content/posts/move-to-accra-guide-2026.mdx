Ghana's Immigration Service still runs on the same core mechanism it did before the 2019 Year of Return momentum: a **work and residence permit** tied to an employer-secured **immigrant quota**, administered by the **Ghana Immigration Service (GIS)** and the Ministry of the Interior, without which a foreign professional generally cannot both work and hold residence legally beyond a short visitor stay. Fees are published and periodically revised by GIS — the practical move for anyone planning a hire is to pull the current fee schedule directly from GIS's own published notices rather than trust a secondhand figure, since Ghana has revised its immigration fee structure more than once in recent years and older blog posts routinely quote stale numbers. The African diaspora angle matters here in a way most destination countries don't offer: Ghana's Right of Abode and various diaspora-focused residency provisions, extensions of the Year of Return momentum, give people of African descent a meaningfully smoother path than the standard employer-sponsored quota route.

## The tax bracket that changes your calculus

Ghana runs a **progressive resident income tax schedule** that climbs from **0% on the first roughly 5,880 cedis** of annual income through intermediate bands of 5%, 10%, 17.5%, and 25%, up to a top marginal rate of **30–35%** on higher income bands. Non-residents, by contrast, pay a **flat 25%** on Ghana-sourced income only — no progressive relief, but also no exposure to worldwide income. Residency status is determined in part by the **183-day presence rule**, and the twist worth knowing is that becoming a Ghanaian tax resident pulls your *worldwide* income into scope, which can genuinely increase your total tax burden versus staying a non-resident paying the flat 25% on local-source income alone — the kind of counterintuitive result that makes a local tax consultation worth the fee before you cross the residency threshold.

## Neighborhood rents, real numbers

**East Legon**, [Accra](/blog/cape-town-vs-nairobi-vs-accra-expat-2026)'s most diaspora- and professional-heavy district with international schools and a dense restaurant scene, runs **roughly $400–700 a month** for a one-bedroom. **Labone**, more central and slightly older in building stock but closer to Osu's commercial strip, sits at **$350–600**. **Osu** itself, the historic mixed-use commercial heart of expat Accra, comes in at **$300–550** — cheapest of the three, but also the least residential in character, with more street noise and commercial traffic. All three neighborhoods sit well above Accra's citywide median rent, reflecting the premium foreign tenants and returning diaspora consistently pay for reliable power backup, water pressure, and 24-hour security that aren't universal outside these pockets.

## Power, water, and the infrastructure gap that isn't optional

The detail that catches newcomers regardless of budget: intermittent power ("dumsor," the local shorthand for load-shedding) and water pressure issues are real enough that most mid-to-upper-tier rentals in East Legon and Airport Residential market backup generators and water storage tanks as standard features, not luxuries — factor a generator-equipped building into your search from day one rather than discovering the gap during your first outage.

## Banking and the practical first month

Opening a Ghanaian bank account as a new resident typically requires a work or residence permit already in hand, which creates a chicken-and-egg gap most newcomers bridge with mobile money services (MTN Mobile Money and its competitors are near-universal in Accra for everyday payments, from taxis to market stalls) during the first few weeks. Budget realistically for that transition period — a first-month float covering rent deposit, a local SIM, and cash-based daily spending until formal banking is sorted is worth planning for explicitly rather than assuming a foreign card will smooth every transaction.

Accra in 2026 is a **GIS-quota-permit, 25%-flat-or-progressive-tax, $300–700-for-a-one-bed** market, with diaspora-specific residency pathways that meaningfully shorten the process for applicants of African descent.
