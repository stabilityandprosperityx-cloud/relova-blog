That single rule changes the calculus for Americans considering Saranda, Ksamil, or Vlora versus nearby Croatia or Montenegro, where the 90-day clock starts ticking the moment you land. ## The 365-day entry window and what comes after

For most Western passport holders, including Americans, arriving in Albania means an automatic **365-day** stay with no visa application, no proof-of-funds letter, and no interview — you simply enter and the clock starts. That is long enough for most people to properly test the Riviera before deciding whether to commit further, which is part of why Saranda and the surrounding coast have become such a popular "trial year" destination. Once that year is up, anyone who wants to stay legally has to move into Albania's formal residence system, which runs through the **e-Albania** government portal. In practice, e-Albania has a reputation among expats for crashing, timing out, and generally being unreliable at the exact moments people need it most, which is why a large share of foreigners hire a local lawyer or fixer to handle the residence-permit filing rather than attempt it solo. Budgeting for that help is worth doing early: a realistic all-in cost for a residence file — document translations, official filing fees, and legal guidance — tends to land around **$1,700 to $2,150**, and that figure is entirely separate from your monthly cost of living and from any one-time furniture or apartment setup spend.

## Saranda, Ksamil, and the Vlora story

Saranda is the Riviera's best-known base, and its sea-view rentals reflect steady demand: expect roughly **€300 to €500 a month** in the low season, running from autumn through spring. Summer changes everything. Once **July and August** arrive, the same units can command close to double their off-season price as owners shift toward short-term tourist rentals, which makes off-season lease-signing the smarter move for anyone planning to stay year-round. Ksamil, the small beach town just south of Saranda famous for its turquoise coves, follows an almost identical seasonal pattern — cheap and quiet outside summer, expensive and crowded during it. Vlora, further north along the coast, is the market's current growth story: the opening of Vlora International Airport has pulled real estate demand and investor attention toward the city, and while prices have moved up accordingly, they still often sit below what you'd pay for an equivalent unit in Saranda or Ksamil at peak season, making it worth watching for anyone not fixed on the more established towns.

## Budgeting, self-employment, and healthcare on the coast

A comfortable solo budget on the Riviera typically runs **€800 to €1,200 a month**, and a couple can expect somewhere in the **€1,200 to €1,800** range for a comparable standard of living — figures that make Albania's coast one of the more affordable Mediterranean-adjacent options in Europe even after the recent uptick in coastal property interest. Anyone planning to work as self-employed or run a small business locally needs to budget differently than someone simply living off outside income: Albania's self-employed residents are required to make **quarterly** social security and health contributions, which is a materially different cash-flow pattern than the steady monthly burn a non-working resident faces. On healthcare, most expats skip the public system for routine care and use private clinics instead, where cash-pay visits remain generally affordable by Western European or American standards. One practical budgeting note worth flagging separately: keep one-time setup costs — furniture, kitchen basics, appliances — off your recurring monthly spreadsheet, since folding them into your "typical month" number is a common way new arrivals underfund their first quarter on the ground.
