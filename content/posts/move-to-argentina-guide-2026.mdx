Argentina remains one of the more accessible South American moves for people with passive income or remote work, though the country's persistent currency volatility makes it a very different kind of planning exercise than a move to, say, Portugal or Colombia. Most relocation attempts fail before the paperwork is even submitted — usually from picking the wrong visa category, showing weak income evidence, or getting the sequencing between visa, housing, and banking backwards. The most common lawful entry route for people without an Argentine employer is the **rentista visa**, built for people living on passive income such as rental income, dividends, or a pension, and planning for it typically starts around **$2,500 a month** in demonstrable passive income, though the exact figure and required documentation should always be confirmed with the nearest Argentine consulate since requirements shift. Most nationalities, including U.S. citizens, can also enter as tourists for an initial **90 days**, with extension options available from within the country — a useful runway for house-hunting or testing a city before committing to a formal visa application.

## Building the paperwork trail correctly

The single biggest mistake people make with Argentine visa applications is treating documents as a checklist to gather quickly rather than a matrix that needs to line up on dates. Build a document tracker with issue dates, apostille status, and expiry windows for every piece of paper in your file — birth certificates, income statements, bank letters, criminal record checks — because Argentine consulates and immigration offices are notably strict about documents that have expired or gone stale between issuance and filing. Losing a month to a document that needed re-issuing is one of the most avoidable and most common delays reported by people going through the rentista process, and it compounds badly if your target moving date is tied to a lease or a shipping container.

## What things actually cost, and why the peso complicates budgeting

Generic country-average cost-of-living figures are close to useless in Argentina because of how volatile the currency has been. The more reliable approach is city-first and category-first: price out your rent tier, your transport style, your food pattern, your insurance level, and your coworking usage individually rather than trusting a single blended number. In Buenos Aires, a one-bedroom apartment paid in U.S. dollars commonly runs **$400 to $800 a month** in many expat reports — a figure that looks remarkably cheap by North American or Western European standards, but one that depends heavily on which exchange mechanism you're using to convert your money. Argentina's blue-market and official FX dynamics can materially change your real purchasing power month to month, so anyone planning a long-term budget should build three separate models — lean, realistic, and stress-case — and carry a **15–25%** contingency on top of whatever number looks comfortable on paper. This is genuinely one of the more distinctive planning challenges of moving to Argentina compared with most other Latin American destinations, where currency stability is less of a variable.

## Healthcare, banking, and the residency ladder

Foreign residents in Argentina typically rely on private health insurance rather than the public system for anything beyond emergencies, and setting up a local bank account is easier once you have a DNI (national ID number) than it is on a tourist visa alone — plan for a transition period where you're operating partly on foreign cards and partly on emerging local banking access. On the residency side, temporary residency converting to permanent residency is commonly discussed around a **two-year** pathway for people who maintain their visa category and stay in good standing, though this varies by category and is worth confirming directly with Argentina's immigration authority (Dirección Nacional de Migraciones) rather than assuming a fixed timeline.
