[Baku](/blog/tbilisi-vs-yerevan-vs-baku-guide-2026) sells itself on a combination most Caucasus cities can't match: oil-boom infrastructure, Caspian Sea views, and rent levels that undercut most of Europe by a wide margin — but the immigration paperwork rewards planning ahead more than the marketing suggests.

## Getting in: e-visa first, residence permit for anything longer

Most visitors enter through Azerbaijan's ASAN e-Visa system, a straightforward online application that's typically processed within a few business days and costs around USD 20-30 depending on nationality and service tier — this covers short tourist or business stays but not long-term residence or the right to work. For anything longer, foreign nationals need a temporary residence permit, applied for through Azerbaijan's State Migration Service (often via the ASAN Xidmet one-stop government service centers, which handle a wide range of civil paperwork efficiently by regional standards). A separate work permit is required if you intend to work for a local employer; the exact income and sponsorship requirements are employer-driven rather than a fixed published threshold, so confirm specifics with the sponsoring company's HR or an immigration lawyer before relying on generic guides.

## Rent and a realistic monthly budget

Baku's housing costs remain low relative to the quality of the city's newer development: a one-bedroom apartment in a decent central or semi-central neighborhood commonly runs AZN 400-700 a month (roughly USD 235-410 at typical exchange rates). A realistic single-person monthly budget covering rent, food, and transport lands around USD 700-1,300, which is genuinely competitive with much cheaper-sounding destinations once you account for Baku's modern metro system, walkable waterfront boulevard, and reasonably reliable utilities.

## Taxes on foreign-source income

Azerbaijan's tax treatment of foreign-source income for residents varies by individual circumstances — some remote workers and freelancers structure their situation so that foreign-earned income isn't taxed locally, but this depends heavily on residency status, the specifics of how income is paid, and any applicable double-taxation treaty between Azerbaijan and the home country. This is not a blanket "zero percent" rule and shouldn't be treated as one; a local accountant or tax adviser familiar with foreign-resident cases is worth the consultation fee before assuming any particular tax outcome.

## The city itself

Baku is Azerbaijan's capital and by far its largest city, with a metropolitan population commonly cited around 2.3 million — a genuine regional capital with international flight connectivity, a growing fintech and startup scene tied to the country's oil-wealth diversification push, and an old-town UNESCO World Heritage core (Icherisheher) alongside the flame-shaped modern towers that dominate its skyline.

## Day-to-day logistics and connectivity

Baku's metro system is genuinely useful for a city its size, with two operating lines covering much of the central and inland districts, and taxi and ride-hailing apps are widely used and inexpensive relative to Western European capitals. Internet infrastructure in central Baku is solid, with fiber connections common in newer apartment buildings, though speeds and reliability can drop noticeably outside the city center — worth testing before committing to a longer lease if remote work depends on it. English is spoken reasonably well in business, hospitality, and among younger residents in central Baku, but Azerbaijani (and Russian, which retains a strong regional presence from the Soviet era) remains the practical default for government offices, older residents, and many day-to-day transactions outside the main expat-facing establishments.

## Healthcare and insurance

Baku has a growing private healthcare sector with several modern clinics and hospitals catering to international patients and expat communities, generally offering a higher standard of care and more English-language service than the public system. Most long-term foreign residents carry private international health insurance, both because it's often a practical necessity for quality care and because some residence permit categories expect proof of coverage as part of the application file. It's worth comparing a few private insurers with specific Azerbaijan or CIS-region coverage before committing, since plan terms and network hospitals vary meaningfully.
