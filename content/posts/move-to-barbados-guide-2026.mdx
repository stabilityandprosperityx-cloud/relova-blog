Barbados was one of the earlier Caribbean remote-work programs and it still runs on the same basic shape it launched with: the **Welcome Stamp** asks applicants to show **at least USD 50,000 in annual income** (or demonstrated financial self-sufficiency for a family), pay a **USD 2,000 fee for an individual or USD 3,000 for a family application** covering spouse and dependents, and grants a **12-month visa** that's renewable if you still qualify when it expires. Processing is genuinely fast by immigration standards — approval commonly lands within **a few working days** of a complete application, not the months-long wait typical of a traditional work visa. What the Welcome Stamp does *not* let you do is work for a Barbadian employer; it's strictly for remote income earned outside the island, verified through employment or business documentation, plus a private health insurance policy that covers your full stay — travel insurance alone doesn't satisfy the requirement.

## Why "not a tax resident" matters more than the headline number

The detail worth underlining: Welcome Stamp holders are explicitly **not considered tax residents of Barbados**, which means income earned outside the country isn't subject to local income tax during your stay on the stamp. That's a meaningfully different structure from, say, Portugal's NHR-era regimes, which taxed you but at a reduced rate — Barbados simply keeps remote workers off its tax rolls entirely as long as the income stays foreign-sourced. You'll still owe whatever your home country requires; the Welcome Stamp doesn't erase a US citizen's IRS obligations, for instance, it just removes a second layer of Barbadian tax on top.

## Holetown rents and the real island budget

A one-bedroom in Holetown — the west-coast strip that's the closest thing Barbados has to an expat and tourism hub, with beach clubs, the Chattel House shopping village, and a concentration of remote workers — commonly runs **USD 1,500–2,500 a month**, sitting at the premium end of the island's rental market precisely because it's the most walkable, beach-adjacent option. A realistic all-in monthly budget for someone actually living comfortably, not backpacking, lands around **USD 3,000–5,000**, and that range reflects Barbados' genuine downside: nearly everything, from groceries to fuel to a rental car, is imported and priced accordingly for an island with limited domestic production. Bridgetown and the south coast run somewhat cheaper than Holetown without losing much in the way of English-language administration, reliable internet, or the general ease of a country where bureaucracy runs in English and the legal system inherits directly from British common law.

## The practical trade-off nobody markets

What the glossy Welcome Stamp campaign undersells is that Barbados is genuinely small — roughly 21 miles by 14 miles — and that intimacy cuts both ways: low crime and a tight expat community on one hand, limited variety in housing stock, healthcare specialists, and consumer goods on the other. Budget a return flight home into your annual planning; Barbados is a hub for the Eastern Caribbean but not itself a major air-travel crossroads, and flights off-island can be pricier than the distance suggests.

## Hurricane season and the insurance line item

Barbados sits within the Atlantic hurricane belt, with the official season running **June through November**, and while the island's location toward the southeastern edge of the Caribbean arc gives it a somewhat better track record than islands further north and west, it is not exempt — property insurance premiums on the island reflect that exposure, and renters should confirm what a landlord's policy actually covers before assuming personal belongings are protected. Building this into the annual budget, rather than treating it as an afterthought, is standard practice among longer-term island residents.

Barbados in 2026 is a **$50,000-income, $2,000-fee, not-a-tax-resident, $1,500–2,500-for-Holetown** proposition — fast to get, genuinely tax-favorable, and priced like the import-dependent island it is.
