Moving to Bogotá in 2026 starts with a piece of peso math that changed the whole visa landscape: from **1 January 2026**, Colombia's national minimum wage (the **SMMLV**) jumped roughly **23%** to **COP 1,750,905 a month**, and because so many of Colombia's visa income thresholds are pegged directly to SMMLV as a multiple, every one of those thresholds moved with it. Anyone reusing a 2025 planning checklist or budget spreadsheet is working with numbers that are now meaningfully out of date — the digital nomad and pensionado income floors sit at **3× SMMLV**, while the rentista floor, for passive income, is a much steeper **10× SMMLV**.

## What the 2026 wage hike actually changed

Doing the multiplication carefully matters here: **3× SMMLV** works out to roughly **COP 5,252,715 a month**, while **10× SMMLV** comes to about **COP 17,509,050 a month** — both meaningfully higher than the 2025 equivalents, since the underlying wage base itself rose about 23%. On the process side, Colombian visas are typically issued or renewed in **two-year** blocks, and there's no hard cap on how many times you can renew as long as you continue meeting the requirements. For people planning toward permanent settlement, Resident (**R**) visa status generally becomes available after **2 to 5 years** on an M-category visa, with the exact timeline depending on which subcategory you originally entered on. ## Sorting out Digital Nomad, Pensionado, and Rentista

The single most common mistake in Bogotá visa applications is confusing these three categories, and the distinctions genuinely matter to which paperwork you need to assemble. The **Digital Nomad visa (V Nómadas Digitales)** requires income at **3× SMMLV** — roughly **COP 5,252,715 a month**, or about **$1,375 to $1,400** — sourced from remote work for a foreign employer or foreign clients. **Pensionado (M-11)** status sits at that same **3×** cash floor, but the income has to come specifically from a **lifetime pension** — social security, or a public or private pension — and critically, rental income or dividend income does not qualify under this category even though the dollar amount looks similar. **Rentista** status requires the much higher **10× SMMLV**, around **COP 17,509,050 a month** or roughly **$4,585**, but in exchange accepts passive income streams like rental property income and investment dividends that Pensionado status explicitly excludes. Mixing these categories up — assuming pension-style income thresholds apply to rental income, for instance — is the classic error that gets Bogotá applications rejected or sent back for additional documentation.

## Budgeting and timelines once you're on the ground

A comfortable solo lifestyle in Bogotá in 2026 typically runs **$2,200 to $3,200 a month**, while a leaner budget can work at **$1,200 to $1,700**, depending heavily on neighborhood choice and lifestyle expectations. Housing in a well-regarded, safe area like **Chicó** commonly runs **$800 to $1,600 a month** (roughly **COP 3 to 5 million**) for a one-bedroom, putting it toward the higher end of Bogotá's neighborhood spectrum but reflecting the security and amenities that draw expats there specifically. On timing, visa decisions after fee payment often come back within roughly **30 days**, though the full process — including obtaining your **cédula de extranjería**, the physical foreign ID card required for daily life — typically takes **4 to 10 weeks** end to end, so plan your move-in date with that runway in mind rather than assuming everything clears in a month. Beyond Chicó, neighborhoods like **Usaquén** and **Chapinero Alto** are worth serious consideration for their own reasons: Usaquén offers a village-like feel within the city, with a well-known Sunday flea market and a lower-key, more residential vibe than Chicó's polish, while Chapinero Alto sits closer to the city's nightlife and restaurant scene at a somewhat lower price point. Bogotá's altitude — roughly 2,600 meters above sea level — is worth taking seriously in the first weeks regardless of which neighborhood you choose; many newcomers underestimate how much it affects energy levels, sleep, and exercise capacity until they're a few days in, and pacing your first week accordingly makes the adjustment considerably smoother.
