Bordeaux is the city expats pick when they want France without [Paris](/blog/paris-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026)'s rent or [Nice](/blog/move-to-south-france-guide-2026)'s tourist crush — a UNESCO-listed 18th-century center, a fully renovated tram-and-pedestrian riverfront, and, since the LGV Sud Europe Atlantique high-speed line opened, a Paris commute short enough to make hybrid work realistic.

## Getting there and getting around

The TGV between Bordeaux and Paris Gare Montparnasse takes as little as 2 hours 4 minutes on the fastest direct services, which has quietly turned Bordeaux into a bedroom-community option for people who need to be in Paris a day or two a week rather than daily. Within the city, the tram network (lines A, B, C, and D) plus an extensive and genuinely usable bike-lane network — the metro area has built out roughly 900 km of dedicated cycling infrastructure — mean many residents skip car ownership entirely, unusual for a French city outside Paris. The Atlantic coast (Arcachon, the Dune du Pilat) is about 45 minutes by car or a direct regional train, making weekend beach trips a realistic habit rather than an occasional splurge.

## Neighborhoods and what they cost

**Chartrons**, the old wine-merchant quarter along the river north of the center, is Bordeaux's most sought-after expat-friendly district: restored stone buildings, a strong café and gallery scene, and a one-bedroom typically running EUR 800–1,200/month. **Saint-Michel**, around the Gothic basilica south of the center, is grittier and more mixed — a large North African and student population, more street markets, noticeably cheaper at roughly EUR 700–1,100 for a one-bedroom — and is gentrifying fast enough that early movers are getting meaningfully better value than latecomers will in two or three years. Families more often land in **Caudéran** or **Le Bouscat**, quieter residential areas to the west with easier parking and access to bilingual and international schooling options, at rents generally comparable to or slightly below Chartrons.

## Visa and residency basics

EU/EEA and Swiss citizens can move and work in Bordeaux with no visa required beyond registration formalities. Non-EU citizens generally need either a long-stay visa tied to a work contract (via the *autorisation de travail* process, employer-sponsored) or, for remote workers and retirees living on non-French income, the **visa de long séjour valant titre de séjour (VLS-TS)** "visiteur" category, which requires proof of sufficient means — commonly benchmarked against the French minimum wage (SMIC), currently a little over EUR 1,800/month gross — and proof of private health insurance for the first year before you're eligible to join the French social security system.

## Work, wine, and the local economy

Bordeaux's economy runs on more than wine tourism, though the wine trade — négociants, château management, export logistics — remains a genuine local employer and a common entry point for expats with hospitality or sales backgrounds. Aerospace and defense (Dassault Aviation has a significant regional presence), digital and tech (a growing cluster around the Bordeaux Technowest and Darwin Ecosystem hubs), and a large university and research base round out the job market. Freelancers and the self-employed generally register under France's **micro-entrepreneur** regime, which simplifies tax and social-charge reporting for smaller incomes and is often the fastest way for a newly arrived remote worker or consultant to get legally set up without waiting on a full company registration. Once you're settled, France's residency permit renewal cycle (typically annual for the first few years, then multi-year) means budgeting an afternoon at the prefecture roughly once a year — appointments book out weeks in advance in Bordeaux specifically, so set a calendar reminder well before your card's expiry rather than scrambling at the deadline.

Bordeaux's wine-region setting also means a longer-term option worth knowing about: several nearby appellations (Saint-Émilion, Médoc, Pessac-Léognan) support a small but real expat community working in wine production, hospitality, and export, distinct from the city-based tech and aerospace crowd, and often reachable via a short regional train or a car from the city center rather than requiring a move to the countryside itself.
