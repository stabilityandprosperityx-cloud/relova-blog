**Bulgaria** is the cheapest EU country most digital nomads never seriously consider, largely because it has no single dedicated "digital nomad visa" brand — the actual route runs through Bulgaria's long-stay Type D visa, and understanding that distinction changes how you plan the paperwork.

## There is no purpose-built nomad visa, but there is a working route

Unlike Spain, Portugal, or Greece, Bulgaria does not have a visa category formally named for remote workers. Long-stay applicants instead apply for a Type D national visa on financial self-sufficiency or freelance/remote-work grounds, then convert it to a residence permit after arrival. Consulates generally expect documented income in the range of EUR 25,000–30,000 a year for a solo applicant — a figure often summarized as roughly 50 times Bulgaria's monthly minimum wage — though there is no single legally fixed threshold the way Spain's IPREM-linked NLV has one, so the practical bar varies somewhat by consulate and case officer. The Type D visa itself typically costs EUR 100–120, with the follow-on residence permit application running an additional EUR 150–300, and processing commonly takes 30–45 days from a complete file.

## The 10% flat tax is real, but tax residency triggers fast

Bulgaria's flat 10% personal income tax rate — one of the lowest in the EU — applies once you become a Bulgarian tax resident, which happens automatically after 183 days of physical presence in a calendar year, or sooner if Bulgaria becomes your "center of vital interests" (where your home, family, or primary economic activity sits). That 183-day trigger matters for freelancers moving mid-year: spend most of the year in Bulgaria and the flat rate applies to worldwide income, not just Bulgarian-sourced income, so it is worth checking your home country's tax treaty position before assuming the 10% rate is a clean win.

## Sofia and Plovdiv price very differently for the same lifestyle

Sofia one-bedroom apartments in central, expat-friendly neighborhoods commonly run EUR 400–650 a month, while Plovdiv — Bulgaria's second city and a genuinely walkable, lower-cost alternative with a growing remote-work community — runs EUR 300–500 for a comparable unit. Both cities have functional, low-cost public transport, and neither requires a car for day-to-day life in the central districts. Utilities, groceries, and eating out remain noticeably below Western European prices even accounting for 2025–2026 inflation, which is the core of Bulgaria's appeal relative to Portugal or Spain at similar visa complexity.

## Schengen access arrived in stages, not all at once

Bulgaria's Schengen integration happened in phases rather than a single cutover: air and sea border checks were lifted in March 2024, and land border checks with neighboring Schengen states followed in January 2025. Practically, this means Bulgaria now functions inside the Schengen travel area for most purposes, though it is worth double-checking current land-border procedures if you are driving in or out, since phased rollouts of this kind occasionally carry residual friction at specific crossings.

## Naturalization is a long game, not a fast track

Bulgarian citizenship by naturalization generally requires several years of continuous legal residence — commonly cited around five years for standard cases, longer for some categories — and involves language and integration requirements on top of the residence clock. Anyone moving to Bulgaria specifically with EU citizenship as the end goal should treat this as a multi-year plan from day one, not something to figure out once residency paperwork is already sorted.

## Renewal, not the first year, is where most applicants stumble

The initial Type D visa and residence permit only cover the first stretch of stay; Bulgaria requires periodic renewal of the residence permit, and each renewal generally re-checks the same self-sufficiency or income documentation required at the original application, not just a formality confirming you're still in the country. Freelancers whose income fluctuates year to year should keep the same standard of documentation — invoices, bank statements, a clear paper trail tying income to the applicant — current and ready well before each renewal window, rather than assembling it retroactively once a renewal notice arrives. Missing a renewal deadline, rather than any dramatic disqualifying event, is the most common reason long-stay applicants in Bulgaria lose their legal status.
