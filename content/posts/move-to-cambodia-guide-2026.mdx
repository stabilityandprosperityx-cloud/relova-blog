Cambodia has no digital-nomad visa, and the country's most common expat mistake is treating the **Ordinary (E-class) visa** as if it were one. It isn't a remote-work permit — it's a long-stay entry document that, once extended, still leaves anyone actually working locally needing a separate **work permit**, and the two paperwork tracks run on entirely different clocks.

## Two documents, two deadlines

The E-class visa itself costs **$35** on entry, whether via e-visa, visa on arrival, or embassy application. Once in-country, an **Ordinary (EB) extension** runs roughly **$285–350 for a 12-month term** through a local visa agent, with shorter increments priced proportionally (a 1-month extension around $50–60, 3 months around $80–100). Separately — and this is the part people skip — anyone working in Cambodia, including remote workers billing foreign clients from a Phnom Penh coworking desk if they're doing so under a locally registered structure, is required to hold a **work permit** issued through the **FWCMS** system, applied for within **90 days of arrival** for new hires and renewed annually by **31 March**, with government fees around **$130** on top of a sole-proprietorship registration (roughly $100–150). An EB extension alone does not authorize work; conflating the two is the single most common compliance gap among long-stay foreigners here.

## Tax residency arrives quietly at day 183

Cambodia treats you as a **tax resident** — taxed on worldwide income — once you're domiciled in the country, have your principal abode there, or are physically present for **more than 182 days** in a calendar year. Resident salary income is taxed on a progressive scale in Cambodian riel, from **0% up to KHR 1.5 million a month** through **20% above KHR 12.5 million**; non-residents instead pay a **flat 20%**, but only on Cambodian-sourced income. If your income is foreign-sourced and you're structuring around non-residency, the 182-day line is the one to actually track — not your visa expiry date, which is a completely separate calculation.

## What Phnom Penh actually costs

Current cost-of-living data puts a **one-bedroom in central Phnom Penh at around $630 a month** (ranging roughly $400–1,190 depending on building and neighborhood), and about **$361** outside the centre. Phnom Penh's fiber connectivity is genuinely solid in the popular expat pockets — BKK1, Tonle Bassac, and along Norodom Boulevard — which is why it, rather than Siem Reap or Sihanoukville, has become the default base for anyone running Zoom-heavy remote work. **Kampot**, a smaller riverside town popular with a slower-paced expat crowd, trades meaningfully lower rent for a longer trip back to Phnom Penh whenever an embassy appointment or document run is unavoidable.

## Healthcare: buy the evacuation cover

Private hospitals like **Royal Phnom Penh Hospital** and the Sen Sok International University Hospital network anchor decent day-to-day private care in the capital, but Cambodia's healthcare system outside a handful of Phnom Penh facilities is thin, and serious trauma or complex specialist cases are still routinely referred to Bangkok. If your plans include diving, surfing, or motorbiking around the islands and provinces on weekends — a common pattern here — carry an international policy that explicitly includes medical evacuation, not just inpatient cover; it is the gap that actually matters if something goes wrong outside the capital.

## The riel and the dollar coexist, unevenly

The Cambodian riel is the official currency, but the **US dollar circulates freely and is the de facto currency for larger transactions**, rent, and most expat-facing pricing — riel is mostly used for small change and local-market purchases under a dollar. That dual system is convenient for budgeting in dollars, but it also means you're exposed to riel-denominated local prices drifting relative to the dollar over time without it showing up cleanly in your own spending, since most of what you actually pay for as an expat is already dollar-priced.
