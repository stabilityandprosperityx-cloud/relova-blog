Moving to Canada for most skilled workers is fundamentally a points game, run through the **Express Entry** system, which pools candidates under programs like the Federal Skilled Worker stream and ranks them by a **Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS)** score that competes against periodic invitation draws. Planners have recently discussed invitation bands in the **470 to 490+** range, but treating any specific number as a guarantee is a genuine mistake — cutoffs shift from draw to draw and vary by program and occupation, so checking the latest **IRCC** draw results for your specific category before building a strategy around a static number is essential. This guide also covers the cost reality: **Toronto** one-bedroom rents commonly run **$2,200 to $2,800 a month** in current planning estimates, and healthcare access has its own timing quirk, including Ontario's roughly **three-month OHIP waiting period** after eligibility kicks in. ## Express Entry, Provincial Nominee Programs, and the founder route

Beyond Express Entry's federal points competition, **Provincial Nominee Programs (PNPs)** open a genuinely parallel door: individual provinces nominate candidates against their own criteria, often tailored to regional labor shortages, and a provincial nomination adds a substantial CRS boost that can make an otherwise uncompetitive federal application successful. For founders and entrepreneurs, the **Start-up Visa** program requires securing a **letter of support from a designated organization** — a qualifying venture capital fund, angel investor group, or business incubator — along with meeting other qualifying business elements, and the process typically involves meaningful legal and incorporation lead time that's worth budgeting for seriously rather than treating as an afterthought. ## What Canadian cities actually cost

Housing costs vary substantially across Canada's major cities. **Toronto** one-bedroom rents commonly land around **$2,200 to $2,800 a month** in current expat planning estimates, while **Vancouver** often runs similar or higher depending on neighborhood — both cities rank among the most expensive in North America for housing. **Montreal** and **Calgary** offer genuinely different tradeoffs: Montreal tends to be more affordable on rent but requires functional French for full social and professional integration, while Calgary often undercuts both Toronto and Vancouver on housing costs while offering a different climate and economic base centered more on energy and finance than tech. These figures should be treated as orientation rather than fixed contracts, since rental listings move on a monthly basis in all four cities.

## Healthcare timing and the coverage gap

Ontario's public healthcare system, **OHIP**, typically includes a roughly **three-month waiting period** after you become eligible for coverage — a gap that catches a lot of newcomers off guard if they assume healthcare access starts the moment they land. Most experienced planners buy **private bridge insurance** specifically to cover that window rather than going without coverage during the wait. It's also worth carrying both digital and paper copies of immunization records and prescription documentation through the transition, since accessing your previous country's medical records gets harder once you're settled and dealing with a new system.

## Banking, credit, and the practical mechanics of settling in

Canadian banking rewards patience in a way that surprises a lot of newcomers: credit history doesn't transfer internationally, so even someone with an excellent credit score back home effectively starts from zero in Canada's system. ## Winter, housing search, and a realistic landing timeline

Winter in most of Canada isn't a lifestyle detail to romanticize — it's real infrastructure planning: winter tires, a commute mode that works in snow and ice, and genuinely warm clothing rated for the actual temperatures your city sees, not just what looks stylish. On housing, the search in major Canadian cities competes directly with both newcomers and a large student population, so the practical sequence most people use is booking temporary housing first, then hunting for a long-term lease once you have local ID and, ideally, some banking history in hand.
