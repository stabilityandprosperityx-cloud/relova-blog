Cape Verde is one of the few Atlantic archipelagos actively courting remote workers, but its ten islands are not interchangeable — Praia, Sal, and Mindelo run on different economies, different price levels, and different languages of daily life.

## The remote work residency route

Cape Verde markets a remote-worker residency track aimed at people earning steady income from outside the country, commonly cited around EUR 1,500 in monthly income as the benchmark applicants are expected to show, though exact documentation requirements (bank statements, employment or freelance contracts, proof of remote-work arrangement) and any minimum-stay conditions should be confirmed directly with the Cape Verdean consulate handling your passport before you commit to dates or a lease, since published income figures for this program vary by source and consulate practice can differ. Applicants typically also need proof of accommodation and health insurance valid in Cape Verde. Once granted, the permit is generally renewable, but renewal terms (six months vs. a full year) are worth pinning down at application time rather than assuming.

## Money: what rent and daily costs look like

Praia, the capital on Santiago island, is the administrative and economic center, with one-bedroom rents commonly running CVE 30,000-50,000 a month (roughly USD 270-450 at typical exchange rates, since the Cabo Verdean escudo is pegged to the euro). São Vicente's Mindelo — the cultural and music capital, famous for its Carnival and colonial-era architecture — runs somewhat cheaper, with comparable one-bedrooms often CVE 25,000-45,000. Sal, the main tourist and resort island with the country's busiest international airport, operates on a different pricing logic entirely: it's dollarized-in-spirit even though the currency is still CVE, with tourist-oriented one-bedroom rentals often quoted directly in euros at EUR 400-700, reflecting resort-economy demand rather than local wages.

## Language, connectivity, and getting around

Portuguese is the official language and the one that unlocks local (non-tourist) pricing at markets, municipal offices, and informal rentals — Cape Verdean Creole (Kriolu) is what's actually spoken day to day, but Portuguese gets you through bureaucracy. English has a real foothold in Mindelo's music and expat-adjacent circles thanks to the island's tourism and diaspora ties, but don't expect it at a municipality desk in Praia. Inter-island travel relies on domestic flights (the ferry network is limited and weather-dependent), so budget both time and money for island-hopping if your remote-work base isn't your only stop. Internet infrastructure is reasonably solid on Sal and Santiago but weaker on the smaller islands, worth testing before signing a longer lease if connectivity is non-negotiable for your work.

## Healthcare, banking, and practical setup

Cape Verde's public healthcare system is limited outside Praia and Mindelo, and specialist care for anything serious commonly means a medical evacuation to Lisbon or Dakar — private international health insurance with evacuation coverage is treated as a near-essential by most long-term expats rather than an optional extra. Banking as a new arrival can be slow to set up; expect to need proof of address (sometimes a notarized rental contract), your residency documentation, and patience, since account-opening timelines here run slower than in mainland Portugal despite the historical ties between the two countries. The euro-pegged escudo (fixed at 110.265 CVE per EUR) means currency risk against the euro is minimal, which is a genuine advantage for anyone earning or budgeting in euros, though it also means the currency moves fully with the euro against other currencies like the US dollar or British pound.

## Weather and the best time to arrive

Cape Verde's climate varies meaningfully by island — Sal and Boa Vista are drier and more desert-like, ideal for windsurfing and consistent sun, while Santiago and the inland parts of São Vicente see more of the brief rainy season that typically runs from August through October. Most new arrivals time their move for the dry season (roughly November through July) to make the early months of apartment-hunting, paperwork, and general settling-in easier, though this isn't a hard requirement given the archipelago's generally mild year-round temperatures compared to mainland West Africa.
