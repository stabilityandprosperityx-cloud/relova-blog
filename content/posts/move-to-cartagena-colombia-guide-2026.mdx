**[Move to Cartagena](/blog/medellin-vs-bogota-vs-cartagena-guide-2026)**, and you are trading Colombia's cool Andean cities for a walled Caribbean port town that runs on tourism, heat, and colonial architecture. If you have read the [Medellín vs Bogotá vs Cartagena comparison](/blog/medellin-vs-bogota-vs-cartagena-guide-2026), this is the deeper, standalone look at what daily life in Cartagena specifically involves once the comparison stops and the actual move begins.

## The neighborhoods that define the city

Cartagena's identity splits cleanly along a few districts. The historic Walled City (Centro) is the postcard version — narrow colonial streets, balconies dripping with bougainvillea, restaurants aimed squarely at cruise-ship tourists — and it is beautiful but expensive and loud, with reggaeton and street vendors running late into the night. Getsemaní, just outside the old walls, is where most long-term expats actually settle: a formerly rough barrio that gentrified hard over the last decade into a mix of boutique hotels, street art, hostels, and increasingly, long-term rentals. A one-bedroom in Getsemaní commonly runs COP 1.8 million to 3.0 million a month. Bocagrande, the high-rise beachfront peninsula that looks more like Miami than colonial Colombia, is the other main expat cluster — quieter at night, more family-oriented, and priced a notch higher at roughly COP 2.0 million to 4.0 million for a comparable one-bedroom. Manga and the newer areas across the bay offer a cheaper, more local alternative if you are willing to trade walkability for savings.

## Heat, humidity, and the climate reality

This is the detail that separates Cartagena from Medellín and Bogotá more than any visa rule: the city sits near sea level on the Caribbean coast and holds a steady average temperature around 32°C (90°F) year-round, with humidity that rarely drops below uncomfortable. There is no "eternal spring" here — Medellín's famous claim to fame — just consistent tropical heat, afternoon downpours especially from August through November, and air conditioning that becomes a non-negotiable utility rather than a luxury. Factor higher electricity bills into your budget accordingly, and expect your pace of life, and probably your wardrobe, to adjust within the first few weeks.

## Visa routes and the practical paperwork

Cartagena sits under the same national immigration framework as the rest of Colombia. The Digital Nomad Visa (Visa V, Tipo Nomada) uses a national income baseline commonly cited around USD 3,000 a month, verified through recent bank statements or freelance contracts, and grants an initial stay that is renewable while you maintain the income proof. Retirees and remote workers with passive income sometimes route through the pensioner or investor visa categories instead, which carry different thresholds. Whichever category applies, confirm the current figure directly with Colombia's Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Cancillería) or a licensed immigration attorney before filing, since income floors and required documentation have moved more than once in recent years.

## Money, tourism swings, and the rental market

Cartagena's economy runs on tourism in a way Medellín's does not, and that shapes the rental market directly: short-term vacation rental demand from cruise passengers and holiday travelers pushes landlords toward Airbnb-style listings over stable long-term leases, which can make finding a proper year-long contract harder and pricier than the headline numbers suggest, especially in the Walled City and Bocagrande during December-to-March high season. A realistic nomad or remote-worker budget, covering a one-bedroom outside peak tourist blocks, groceries, transport, and health insurance, lands around USD 1,500 to 2,500 a month for a comfortable single-person setup — noticeably higher than Medellín for equivalent square footage, largely because of that tourism premium. Building a 15 to 25 percent cash buffer into your first few months is worth it here specifically because of how seasonal pricing swings can be.
