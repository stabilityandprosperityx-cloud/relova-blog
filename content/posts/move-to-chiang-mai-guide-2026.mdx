**[Move to Chiang Mai](/blog/bangkok-vs-chiang-mai-expat-guide-2026)** and you join a nomad scene that has run continuously since the early 2010s coworking boom — cheap living, a genuine community, and a visa landscape that has gotten both more formal and more confusing as Thailand rolls out newer long-term options alongside older workarounds.

## Sorting the visa options

Thailand does not hand remote workers a single obvious route, so most newcomers land on one of two paths. The Long-Term Resident ([LTR](/blog/move-to-thailand-guide)) visa, aimed at wealthy professionals, remote workers, and retirees, is commonly discussed with an annual income threshold around USD 80,000, though lower tiers exist for specific categories like retirees with lower income paired with larger fixed deposits — read the actual Board of Investment criteria rather than a summary, because eligibility categories differ meaningfully. For people who don't clear that bar, the Multiple-Entry Tourist Visa (METV) is the practical fallback many nomads still use: a roughly six-month window built from stacked 60-day entries, with a modest visa fee commonly cited around 2,000 THB per entry, that lets you stay long-term without formal work authorization, provided you are not earning from Thai clients. Thailand's visa rules have shifted more than once in the last few years, so confirm current terms with a Thai consulate or immigration lawyer before booking anything non-refundable.

## Nimman versus Old City rent

Chiang Mai's two main expat clusters have distinct personalities. Nimmanhaemin (Nimman) is the polished, café-dense district next to Chiang Mai University — coworking spaces, boutique coffee shops, and a younger, more international crowd — and one-bedroom rent there commonly runs THB 8,000 to 15,000 a month. The Old City, inside the moat walls, trades some of that polish for atmosphere and history: temples on every block, a slower pace, and slightly cheaper one-bedroom rents typically between THB 6,000 and 10,000. Both areas put you within a short scooter ride of everything that matters, and neither requires a car. Utilities, a scooter rental, health insurance, and food easily add another THB 10,000 to 20,000 a month depending on how much you cook versus eat out.

## The smoke season nobody puts on a postcard

The detail that catches newcomers who plan their move purely around rent and visas is air quality. From roughly February through April, agricultural burning across northern Thailand and neighboring countries pushes the city into a genuine smoke season, with the Air Quality Index regularly climbing above 200 — unhealthy by any international standard — and peaking hardest in March. Locals and long-timers often leave the city entirely for those weeks, heading south to the islands or abroad, and a serious number of nomads plan their Chiang Mai stints to avoid March altogether. If you are moving for the first time, do not schedule your arrival for peak burning season expecting the mountain views and clear skies you saw in photos; check current AQI data before committing to a lease during those months.

## Settling in day to day

Once you are past visa logistics and smoke-season timing, Chiang Mai is one of the easier cities on this list to actually live in. SIM cards and fiber internet are cheap and fast, coworking spaces are plentiful and social, and the cost of eating well — whether street food or restaurant meals — stays low by almost any international comparison. Healthcare is a genuine strength: private hospitals like Bangkok Hospital Chiang Mai serve the expat community with English-speaking staff at a fraction of US or European prices, which is why many retirees and long-stay nomads carry only modest local insurance rather than expensive international plans. Build in a 15 to 25 percent cash buffer for your first few months regardless — visa runs, deposit costs, and the inevitable furniture and appliance purchases add up faster than the headline rent figures suggest.
