**How to move to Costa Rica in 2026** starts with picking the right legal category, because the country runs three distinct residency-by-income routes rather than one universal visa, and most relocation failures trace back to filing under the wrong one or underestimating the document sequencing between immigration, banking, and housing.

## Matching your income to a visa category

Rentista status is built for people living off savings or guaranteed passive income rather than a pension: applicants commonly need to show either a stable income around USD 2,500 a month for at least two years, or an equivalent lump-sum deposit — often cited near USD 60,000 — placed in a Costa Rican bank, guaranteeing that monthly draw. Pensionado status, the more established route, is aimed squarely at retirees with a verifiable pension and commonly references a monthly pension income around USD 1,000, a noticeably lower bar than Rentista because it assumes a permanent, government-verified income stream rather than a depleting deposit. The newer Digital Nomad Visa, introduced to capture remote workers who neither retire nor invest, typically asks for around USD 3,000 a month in foreign-sourced income and runs on a one-year, renewable logic rather than converting toward permanent residency the way Pensionado and Rentista eventually can. All three figures move with inflation and policy updates, so confirm the live thresholds on Costa Rica's official immigration portal (Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería) before you build a budget or book an appointment around them.

## Budgeting realistically by city, not by country average

Generic "Costa Rica costs $X/month" figures are close to useless because San José, the Central Valley towns, and the beach towns on either coast run on entirely different price scales. In the capital, a one-bedroom apartment commonly rents for USD 600 to 1,000 a month, with the Central Valley's smaller expat towns like Atenas or Grecia often coming in cheaper, and beach destinations like Tamarindo or Santa Teresa running noticeably higher due to tourism demand. Build your budget city-first rather than country-first: settle on your rent tier, then layer in a car or reliable transport plan (public transport thins out fast outside the Central Valley), a food pattern that reflects whether you're shopping at local ferias or importing habits, private health insurance or CAJA enrollment, and coworking costs if you need them. Modeling three scenarios — lean, realistic, and stress-case — with a 15 to 25 percent contingency on top catches the deposit and setup costs that first-timers routinely miss.

## The territorial tax draw, and its limits

Costa Rica taxes residents on a territorial basis, meaning foreign-sourced income is generally not taxed by the Costa Rican government, which is a genuine and frequently cited draw for retirees and remote workers alike. That said, "territorial tax framing" is not a blank check: it does not exempt you from your home country's tax obligations if you remain a tax resident or citizen there (a real issue for Americans, given US citizenship-based taxation), and it does not cover locally sourced income if you take local work or run a Costa Rica-based business. Treat the territorial system as a real advantage worth planning around, but get a cross-border tax consultation before assuming it simplifies your full tax picture.

## Healthcare, banking, and getting through the first 90 days

Costa Rica's CAJA public health system is available to legal residents through mandatory contributions once residency is approved, and it is widely regarded as strong for a middle-income country, though many expats layer private insurance or direct-pay private clinics on top for shorter wait times. Banking requires your residency paperwork (or at minimum a DIMEX card application in process) before most banks will open a full account, so plan an interim cash-and-transfer strategy for your first weeks. A practical 90-day sequence looks like: lock your visa category and gather notarized, apostilled documents before departure; secure short-term housing on arrival rather than signing a long lease sight unseen; open a bank account and complete CAJA enrollment as soon as your paperwork allows; then move to a permanent rental once you have a feel for which region actually suits your lifestyle.
