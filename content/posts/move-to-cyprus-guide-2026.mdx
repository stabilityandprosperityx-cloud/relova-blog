**How to move to Cyprus in 2026** usually comes down to which of a handful of residency and tax structures actually fits your income type, because Cyprus markets itself heavily on its tax regime and that reputation attracts a wider mix of retirees, remote workers, and company owners than most Mediterranean destinations.

## Matching a residency route to your situation

Cyprus offers several distinct paths depending on how you earn. Category F residency is aimed at people living on passive income — pensions, investments, rental income — and commonly references an annual threshold around €9,568 for a single applicant, with additional amounts required per dependent; it is a comparatively low bar by EU standards but does not grant the right to work locally. The Digital Nomad Visa, by contrast, targets remote employees and freelancers working for companies outside Cyprus, with planning figures often starting around €3,500 a month in verifiable income — a notably higher bar than Category F, reflecting its different applicant profile. Company owners and investors sometimes route through business-registration-linked permits instead. Because all of these thresholds are periodically revised, verify the current figures and required documentation on Cyprus's official immigration portal before filing.

## The non-dom tax regime that draws people here

Cyprus's non-domiciled tax status is the headline draw for a specific type of relocator: those with significant dividend or interest income. Under the regime, qualifying non-domiciled tax residents can be exempt from tax on dividends and interest for up to 17 years, a structure that has made Cyprus a serious contender for retirees living off investment portfolios and for business owners paying themselves through dividends rather than salary. This exemption sits alongside a corporate tax rate commonly cited at 12.5 percent, one of the lower headline rates in the EU, which is why Cyprus also attracts holding companies and small international businesses. None of this is automatic — non-dom status has its own application and eligibility criteria, and how it interacts with your home country's tax treaty and citizenship-based obligations (a real issue for Americans in particular) requires a cross-border tax consultation rather than a blog-post assumption.

## Limassol versus Nicosia on cost

The two cities pull very different crowds. Limassol, the coastal business and finance hub, has become something of an international enclave — a large Russian, Israeli, and broader European business community has pushed rents up accordingly, with one-bedroom apartments commonly running €900 to 1,400 a month. Nicosia, the inland capital, is calmer, more bureaucratic in character since it hosts the government ministries, and noticeably cheaper, with comparable one-bedrooms typically €700 to 1,100. Limassol suits people prioritizing a beach-city, business-networking lifestyle; Nicosia suits those who want a lower cost base and don't need coastal proximity day to day. Both cities are compact enough that a car is useful but not always essential within the center.

## Healthcare, banking, and getting set up

Cyprus runs GESY, a national health insurance scheme that legal residents contribute to and can access once registered, and it has matured considerably since its 2019 rollout, though many expats still carry supplemental private insurance for faster specialist access or English-language continuity of care. Banking setup requires your residency documentation and proof of address, and Cypriot banks — shaped by the country's history as a financial center — are generally comfortable processing foreign-resident accounts, though compliance and source-of-funds checks can be thorough given the jurisdiction's past experience with financial scrutiny. Build a realistic 90-day plan: settle your residency category first, since it determines which tax and banking doors open, then handle housing and account setup once that paperwork is confirmed rather than in parallel.
