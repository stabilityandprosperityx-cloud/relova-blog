**How to move to Czech Republic in 2026** most often runs through one of two main legal categories: employment sponsorship or self-employment via a trade license, and picking correctly between them from the start saves months of rework once you understand how the system actually processes each one.

## Employee Card versus trade license

The Employee Card is the standard route for people with a job offer from a Czech-registered employer; it combines work and residence permission into a single document and is commonly issued for up to two years, renewable as long as the underlying employment continues. It requires the employer to demonstrate the role could not easily be filled locally, so the process leans on your employer's HR and legal team as much as your own paperwork. The alternative, popular with freelancers and small business owners, is registering as a self-employed tradesperson (živnostenský list). Registration itself is inexpensive — commonly quoted around CZK 1,000 — and comparatively fast, but it shifts the burden of health insurance, social contributions, and tax filing entirely onto you rather than an employer, and it requires a residence permit application filed separately from the trade registration itself. Confirm current document lists and processing times with the Ministry of the Interior or a Czech immigration consultant, since both routes involve appointment backlogs that vary significantly by region and season.

## Prague versus Brno on rent and pace

Prague dominates the country's job market, international schools, and English-language services, and its rents reflect that: a one-bedroom apartment commonly runs CZK 22,000 to 32,000 a month depending on district, with the historic center and popular expat neighborhoods like Vinohrady sitting toward the top of that range. Brno, the country's second city and a genuine university and tech hub in its own right, offers a real alternative at roughly CZK 16,000 to 22,000 for comparable space, with a noticeably smaller but still functional international community and shorter commute times across the city. Neither city requires a car for daily life — both run efficient tram and metro networks — but Prague's public transport is more extensive and better suited to a car-free lifestyle long-term.

## Tax, healthcare, and the practicalities

The Czech Republic runs a comparatively simple flat income tax structure, with a headline rate commonly cited around 15 percent for standard income bands (a higher rate applies above a set annual threshold), which is straightforward to plan around compared to countries with steep progressive brackets. Public health insurance is mandatory for residents and, once you're enrolled through employment or self-employment contributions, gives access to a healthcare system generally well regarded by regional standards, though English-language service quality varies by clinic and city — Prague has more English-speaking private practices than smaller towns. Address registration and residence permit collection typically happen in person, and appointment availability at the Ministry of the Interior's foreign police offices is one of the more common bottlenecks newcomers hit, so book early rather than assuming a walk-in slot will be available.

## The long game toward permanent residency

For anyone planning to stay beyond a few years, permanent residency is typically targeted around the five-year mark of continuous legal residence, after which the requirements to renew work and residence permits repeatedly fall away in favor of a more stable long-term status. Getting there cleanly depends heavily on maintaining continuous, correctly documented residence from day one — gaps in permit validity, missed renewals, or switching between employment and self-employment status without proper transitions can reset or complicate that timeline, so it is worth treating your very first permit application as the foundation for a multi-year plan rather than a one-off formality.
