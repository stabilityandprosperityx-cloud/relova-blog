**[Move to Dubai as a freelancer](/blog/dubai-vs-abu-dhabi-expat-guide-2026)**, and the path runs almost entirely through freezone licensing rather than a conventional employer-sponsored visa — a structure that gives independent professionals genuine legal standing to invoice clients and hold their own residency, provided you pick the right freezone and understand where the tax line actually falls.

## How the freezone freelance visa actually works

Dubai's freezones — semi-autonomous economic areas each with their own regulator — let a solo professional register a freelance permit or license without needing a UAE-based employer to sponsor them. IFZA (International Free Zone Authority), one of the more freelancer-friendly and cost-conscious options, commonly prices its freelance packages starting around AED 12,500 a year, while DMCC (Dubai Multi Commodities Centre), a more prestigious and Dubai-mainland-adjacent freezone popular with trading and consulting businesses, starts noticeably higher at around AED 20,000 annually. The price difference reflects more than just cachet: DMCC offers a wider range of permitted activities and a downtown Dubai physical presence option, while IFZA is built for lean, remote-first solo operators who mainly need the legal wrapper and the residency visa that comes attached to it. Either license typically bundles in your residence visa application, meaning the freezone fee is doing double duty as both your business registration and your immigration sponsor.

## Where the corporate tax line actually sits

The UAE introduced federal corporate tax in 2023, and it is a genuine consideration for anyone setting up as a freelancer here, but the practical threshold matters more than the headline rate. Freelancers and small businesses with taxable revenue under AED 375,000 a year are commonly modeled as falling outside the standard corporate tax liability band, either through a small business relief provision or by simply not crossing into taxable profit territory once allowable deductions are applied. Above that threshold, standard corporate tax applies to profits, and freezone entities need to confirm they meet qualifying free zone person criteria to retain any preferential treatment — a technical area where getting UAE-specific tax advice before you scale past that revenue line is worth the cost of the consultation. Do not assume your setup is automatically tax-free simply because you are freezone-based; the exemption depends on your revenue, structure, and ongoing compliance with the qualifying conditions.

## A realistic setup timeline

Most freelancers moving through a freezone license report a two-to-four-week timeline from application to residency visa in hand, assuming documents and the mandatory medical fitness test move without delay. The sequence typically runs: choose and apply through your freezone (IFZA and DMCC both process applications largely online), receive your trade license, then proceed to the Emirates ID biometrics appointment and medical test required for residency. Delays cluster around two points — document authentication for foreign-issued paperwork (degree certificates, prior business registrations) that need attestation, and medical test scheduling during busy periods. Building in a buffer of a few extra weeks beyond the optimistic four-week estimate is realistic for a first-time applicant navigating the system without local guidance.

## Living costs and what the license doesn't cover

The freezone fee and visa cost are only the entry ticket — housing, health insurance (mandatory for residency), and daily living costs in Dubai run meaningfully higher than most of the destinations covered on this site, and a freelancer's income needs to clear both the license renewal cost and a genuinely competitive cost of living, not just the visa threshold. Health insurance is a hard requirement tied to your residency visa renewal, not an optional add-on, so budget it as a fixed annual cost from day one. Building 15 to 25 percent slack into your first-year budget accounts for setup costs that rarely show up in the headline licensing fee — bank account minimum balances, office or flexi-desk add-ons some freezones require, and the inevitable early-months learning curve on which local services and providers are worth using.
