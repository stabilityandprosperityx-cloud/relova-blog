Croatia's **digital nomad residence permit** income test moves every year, and 2026's figure is meaningfully higher than the number still circulating in older guides: the requirement is **2.5 times the previous year's average net salary**, which lands around **€3,622.50 a month** using 2025's published average of roughly €1,449. Add roughly **€145 a month** per accompanying family member (10% of the average salary figure, not 10% of the threshold). Because the multiplier resets each spring when Croatia's statistics office publishes the new average, treat any income number here as a planning estimate and re-check the current figure the month you file — this is exactly the kind of wage-indexed threshold that quietly moves while forum posts stay frozen in time.

## Split's residence permit versus [Dubrovnik](/blog/split-vs-dubrovnik-vs-zadar-guide-2026)'s tourist tide

The permit itself — application fee around **€60**, total setup costs (background check, insurance, translations) typically **€400–1,000** — works identically whether you land in Split or Dubrovnik. What differs is everything downstream. Split functions as a real, year-round Croatian city with a university, a ferry terminal to the islands, and a population that does not evaporate in October, which makes it easier to build an actual life and rent a normal apartment outside peak season. Dubrovnik, by contrast, runs on tourism in a way that shapes almost every transaction: landlords who can earn more from three months of Game-of-Thrones tourists than a full year of a resident tenant, restaurant staff cycling seasonally, and a walled Old Town that is genuinely magical in November and genuinely overwhelming in July.

## Two rent markets, one seasonal multiplier

Split one-bedrooms commonly run **€700–1,100 a month** outside the historic core, with better value the further you get from Diocletian's Palace. Dubrovnik runs higher across the board — **€900–1,500** for comparable one-bedrooms — and that gap widens sharply if you are trying to rent short-term during peak summer, when Dubrovnik landlords understandably chase tourist rates over annual leases. If your plan depends on flexibility (a few months here, a few there), Split's larger, less tourism-dependent housing stock gives you more negotiating room; Dubrovnik rewards people who can commit to a 12-month lease signed in the off-season.

## The Schengen-membership upside nobody mentions

Croatia became a **full Schengen member on 1 January 2023**, and by 2026 that is simply the operating reality rather than a recent change worth dwelling on — but it is still underweighted in most relocation content. A Croatian digital nomad permit is not just a Croatia-only stay; once you hold it, movement across the rest of the Schengen area follows Schengen rules rather than a separate bilateral arrangement. That matters concretely for anyone running client meetings out of Vienna, Milan, or Munich, or anyone who wants weekend flexibility without recalculating a 90/180 day count against a non-Schengen host country.

## Ferries, flights, and the eighteen-month ceiling

The Croatian nomad permit currently caps at **18 months with no renewal or extension in the same stint** — after it expires you must leave and wait **six months** before reapplying. That hard ceiling, more than the income threshold, is what actually shapes long-term planning: this is a visa for a defined chapter, not an open-ended settlement path, so pair it with a real exit or transition plan (another country's visa, an EU ancestry route, or simply a return home) rather than assuming renewal. Split Airport and Dubrovnik Airport both run substantial seasonal European schedules, but winter frequencies thin out fast — check actual route maps for your specific months before assuming a cheap weekend flight home is always available.
