**How to move to Estonia in 2026** trips up more people on one specific confusion than any other single issue: mixing up e-Residency, Estonia's well-marketed digital business program, with an actual right to live in the country. They are not the same thing, and understanding that distinction early saves a lot of wasted planning.

## e-Residency does not grant residence

Estonia's e-Residency program, launched in 2014, lets anyone in the world register and run an Estonian company remotely, complete with a digital ID kit that typically costs around €120 to 130. It is a genuinely useful tool for EU-facing business banking and administration, but it grants zero physical residency rights — you cannot move to Estonia on an e-Residency card alone, and conflating the two is the single most common misconception newcomers arrive with. If you actually want to live in Estonia, you need a separate residence permit or visa category entirely, most commonly the Digital Nomad Visa.

## The Digital Nomad Visa income bar

Estonia's Digital Nomad Visa, aimed at remote employees and freelancers working for companies based outside Estonia, is commonly cited with an income requirement around €4,500 a month — a notably high threshold compared to many other European nomad visa programs, reflecting Estonia's generally higher cost baseline and stricter vetting. The application fee is comparatively modest, often cited around €100, but the income proof itself (payslips or contracts covering the preceding months) is the part that trips up applicants who assume a lower bar based on other countries' programs. Because Estonia periodically updates this threshold, confirm the current figure directly with the Police and Border Guard Board (PPA) before assuming your income qualifies.

## What Tallinn rent and corporate tax actually look like

Tallinn, where the overwhelming majority of Estonia's foreign residents and digital nomads settle, has a one-bedroom rental market commonly running €800 to 1,300 a month depending on neighborhood — the Old Town and central districts sitting toward the top, with Kalamaja and slightly further-out areas offering more reasonable rates without sacrificing much walkability. On the business side, Estonia's corporate tax structure is one of its most distinctive features: retained and reinvested corporate profits are commonly taxed at 0 percent, with the standard 20 percent rate applying only once profits are distributed as dividends. That structure rewards businesses that reinvest rather than pay out immediately, and it is a genuine draw for founders running lean, growth-focused companies through their e-Residency setup, though how it interacts with your personal home-country tax residency and any dividend income you eventually draw needs its own separate check with a cross-border tax advisor.

## Settling into daily life

Estonia runs one of the most digitized public administration systems in Europe — nearly all government services, from tax filing to voting, happen online — which makes bureaucratic friction lower here than in most EU countries once your residence permit is approved, even if getting that initial approval takes patience. English is widely spoken in Tallinn's business and tech circles specifically because the country has built its economy around attracting international talent, though Estonian (and Russian, spoken by a sizable minority) remain the practical languages outside those circles. Healthcare access ties to your residence status: once your permit is active and you're contributing through employment or the relevant insurance scheme, the public system is solid by regional standards, though many nomads on shorter-term visas carry private international coverage during the gap before full registration completes.
