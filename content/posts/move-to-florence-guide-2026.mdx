Florence in 2026 is two cities layered on top of each other: a UNESCO historic center absorbing roughly **14 million visitors a year** against a resident population of around **370,000**, and a genuinely livable Italian city just outside the tourist perimeter where August still empties out and long lunches still happen. Which one you land in depends almost entirely on which neighborhood you choose and whether you time your search around the new rental rules.

## The rental crackdown just got much bigger

Florence's historic-center short-term rental ban already covered **35,593 properties**. On **26 May 2026**, Mayor Sara Funaro pushed through what she called a national first: extending restrictions to **67,780 additional properties** beyond the center, bringing the total affected stock past **100,000 residential units** across neighborhoods including San Jacopino, Statuto, Le Cure, Campo di Marte, and parts of San Frediano. Existing short-term-rental operators are protected under a **moratorium running through 31 May 2028**, after which an authorization system may begin winding down the properties currently grandfathered in. If you're weighing a Florence property purchase as an income-producing Airbnb, this policy — not the yield spreadsheet — is now the binding constraint, and it has grown considerably wider than the original historic-center-only rule most guides still describe.

## Elective residency, priced for real retirees

The **elective residence visa** — Italy's long-stay route for people with independent passive income who won't work locally — carries an official minimum around **€31,000 a year for a single applicant**, rising by roughly 20% per additional family member (so about **€37,200** for a couple). Consular officers in practice look more favorably on applicants showing **€35,000–40,000**, plus meaningful liquid savings as a buffer — figures closer to €50,000 for singles and €80,000 for couples are commonly cited as what actually clears review comfortably, even though they exceed the bare legal minimum. This is not a working visa: any local employment or Italian-sourced business activity generally falls outside its scope, and consulates do check.

## Digital nomads have a separate, higher bar

Italy's dedicated **[digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-italy-guide-2026)** sets its income threshold differently — roughly **€28,000–30,000 annually**, benchmarked at three times the healthcare-exemption threshold used elsewhere in Italian immigration law — and unlike elective residency, it explicitly permits remote work for a non-Italian employer or clients. The two routes get confused constantly because the numbers sit close together; the practical difference is what you're legally allowed to do once you're in the country, not just the income bar to enter.

## What a real Oltrarno rent looks like

A one-bedroom in the **Oltrarno**, Florence's left-bank artisan quarter and the neighborhood most expats actually want, typically runs **€800–1,300 a month**, while **Santa Croce** — closer to the tourist core but with genuine residential streets a few blocks off the main routes — can push **€900–1,500**. Both are meaningfully cheaper than equivalent central [Rome](/blog/move-to-rome-guide-2026) or Milan addresses, which is part of why Florence draws a disproportionate number of remote workers and retirees relative to its size. Expect a landlord to ask for a registered lease (*contratto di locazione*) for anything beyond a short-let, which is also the document your residency permit application will eventually want to see.

## Living around the crowds, not despite them

Florence's tourist density is concentrated almost entirely around the Duomo, the Uffizi, and the Ponte Vecchio corridor; a ten-minute walk into the Oltrarno's side streets or up toward San Niccolò changes the character of the city completely, even in peak June and July. August is the genuine local exodus — many family-run trattorias close for two to three weeks, which is a real planning consideration for a first month rather than trivia. If overtourism is the thing that would sour you on Florence, the rental-restriction expansion is arguably good long-term news for residents: it's a direct attempt to slow the conversion of residential housing stock into tourist accommodation, which has been squeezing the local rental market for years.
