France runs one of Europe's more structured visa systems, which cuts both ways: the categories are well-defined and the healthcare system genuinely delivers once you're in it, but each route has specific eligibility numbers that are easy to misjudge from outside the country.

## The Passeport Talent route covers more people than the name suggests

France's Passeport Talent multi-year residence permit isn't a single visa but a family of categories covering investors, company founders, qualified employees, researchers, artists, and more, each with its own qualifying bar. The investor track commonly cited requires roughly €300,000 in direct investment creating or preserving jobs (figures for this and the qualifying-employee salary thresholds are periodically revised and should be checked against France-Visas, the official government portal, before applying), while the company-creation track generally requires a viable business plan and a funding level in the tens of thousands of euros rather than hundreds of thousands — treat any specific figure as a starting reference to verify rather than a fixed rule, since thresholds for these categories have shifted in recent years. Salaried "qualified employee" applicants instead qualify based on a job offer paying at least roughly 1.5 to 2 times the French minimum wage, depending on category, rather than a lump investment.

## [Paris](/blog/paris-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026) and Lyon rents: budget for a meaningful gap between them

Paris rents for a one-bedroom apartment commonly run €1,400–2,200 depending on arrondissement, with the western and central districts (7th, 8th, 16th) toward the top of that range and eastern, more residential arrondissements toward the bottom. Lyon, France's second city and increasingly popular with people priced out of Paris, runs meaningfully cheaper — typically €900–1,400 for comparable space — while offering strong transit, a large university population, and a shorter commute culture overall. Both cities require a dossier (proof of income, often a French guarantor or garantie visale insurance, and standard ID documents) to secure a lease, and the process is noticeably more paperwork-heavy than renting in the US or UK.

## Healthcare reimbursement rates are generous but not 100%

Once enrolled in France's Assurance Maladie (Sécu) system, which long-term visa holders typically become eligible to join after a qualifying period of residence, standard reimbursement rates for many common medical services sit around 70%, with the remainder typically covered by a supplementary private insurance policy (a "mutuelle") that most residents carry alongside the public system. Employer-provided mutuelle coverage is common for salaried workers; self-employed and non-working long-stay residents need to arrange their own.

## Language requirements bite harder at the residency and citizenship stages than at entry

Most initial long-stay visas don't require proven French proficiency, but subsequent steps do: the multi-year Carte de Résident (typically available after several years of legal residence) and naturalization both generally require French language proficiency at a B1 level or higher on the CEFR scale, assessed via an approved test or diploma. This catches people who move on a purely professional visa without building language study into their first couple of years, then find the requirement is a hard gate later rather than a soft expectation.

## Naturalization: five years is the reference point, not a guarantee

Standard naturalization by residence (naturalisation par décret) generally requires around five years of continuous, regular residence in France, though this can be reduced in specific circumstances (having completed higher education in France, for instance) and can also be extended in practice by processing backlogs at the prefecture level, which have been a persistent source of delay independent of the formal eligibility timeline. Applicants should treat five years as the legal minimum baseline, not an expected total time to citizenship.

## The prefecture appointment system is the real bottleneck for most applicants

Nearly every visa renewal, Carte de Séjour issuance, and status-change step routes through a local prefecture, and appointment availability — not document eligibility — is the most commonly cited source of delay for new residents. Major prefectures serving Paris and other large cities have seen appointment backlogs stretch to several months during peak periods, which is why many long-stay visa holders now begin renewal paperwork well before their permit's expiry date rather than waiting for a reminder. France has also rolled out online appointment and renewal portals in various prefectures to ease this bottleneck, but coverage and reliability vary by département, so it's worth checking the specific local prefecture's current process rather than assuming a uniform national system.
