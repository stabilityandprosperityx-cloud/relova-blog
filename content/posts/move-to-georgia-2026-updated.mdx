Georgia's appeal as a relocation destination hasn't changed much in substance since it became a fixture on digital-nomad shortlists a few years ago: a long visa-free stay for most Western passport holders, a low cost of living relative to the EU, and a genuinely low-friction path to legally working there as a freelancer. What has changed is that the rules have been reviewed and tightened for specific nationalities, and the cost advantage has narrowed somewhat as Tbilisi has gotten more expensive. Here's the country-level picture as of 2026.

## The visa-free entry rule, and who it still covers

Georgia has long allowed citizens of a wide range of countries — including the US, UK, EU member states, and dozens of others — to enter and remain visa-free for up to 365 days, one of the most generous such allowances in the world. That headline figure is still broadly accurate for those core nationalities. What has changed is around the edges: Georgia's Ministry of Internal Affairs amended the rules in 2025 to tighten requirements for a specific list of nationalities (including several African and South/Central Asian countries), generally requiring those travelers to hold a valid Gulf-state visa or residence permit as a condition of visa-free entry. If you're not from one of the traditionally visa-free Western countries, don't assume the 365-day rule applies to you without checking Georgia's current visa policy directly — reciprocity and country-specific carve-outs shift periodically.

## What "visa-free" doesn't give you: legal work and long-term residence

Visa-free entry lets you live in Georgia and, in practice, work remotely for foreign clients or employers, but it is not a residence permit and it does not by itself authorize local employment with a Georgian company. For anyone planning to stay past the visa-free window, or who wants a more durable legal status, Georgia offers several residence permit categories: a work permit tied to local employment, a residence permit based on property ownership above a set investment threshold, a student permit, a family-reunification route, and residence tied to registering as an Individual Entrepreneur with a qualifying level of declared income or investment. Each has its own document list and processing timeline through the Public Service Hall (Georgia's one-stop government service centers), and processing generally runs from a few weeks to a few months depending on the category and how complete the application is on first submission.

## Cost of living has risen, but Georgia is still inexpensive by Western European standards

Tbilisi rents have climbed noticeably since the early 2020s, driven partly by an influx of remote workers and relocating Russians and Ukrainians after 2022, and partly by ordinary urban growth. A one-bedroom in a central, desirable neighborhood like Vera or Vake commonly runs $500–900 a month today, well above pre-2022 levels, while Batumi and smaller cities remain considerably cheaper, often $300–600 for comparable space. Groceries, transport, and eating out remain inexpensive relative to Western Europe or North America even with these increases, which is part of why the overall cost-of-living case for Georgia still holds up even as the "$600/month for everything" era has largely passed.

## Banking has gotten more procedural, not harder in principle

Opening a personal or business bank account as a foreigner (TBC Bank and Bank of Georgia are the two most commonly used by expats) is still realistic to do in a single visit for many nationalities, but compliance checks have tightened: expect banks to ask for proof of address (often a rental agreement plus a recent utility bill), a clear explanation of the source of funds, and sometimes additional documentation for larger account activity. This is a broader global trend in banking compliance rather than something unique to Georgia, but it's worth budgeting an extra day or two for paperwork rather than assuming account opening is instant.

## Why people still choose Georgia over other options

The combination that keeps Georgia competitive is speed and simplicity: a long visa-free window that removes immigration pressure in year one, a genuinely fast and low-cost path to formalizing freelance income (see our [deep dive on Georgia's tax regime](/blog/georgia-digital-nomad-tax-visa-deep-dive-2026) for the specific mechanics), a Mediterranean-adjacent climate and food culture, and a cost of living that, even after recent increases, still undercuts most of Western Europe. The trade-offs are real too — political volatility in 2024–2025 made some expats more cautious, and the language barrier outside major cities is significant — but for people who want low bureaucratic friction to get started, Georgia remains one of the more accessible options on the table.
