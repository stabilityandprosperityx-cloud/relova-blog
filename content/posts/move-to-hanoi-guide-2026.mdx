Hanoi rewards people who plan around its seasons and its paperwork in roughly equal measure. Vietnam's e-visa system remains the entry point for most nomads and remote workers: it's generally issued for stays of up to 90 days, single or multiple entry, and it can usually be arranged online before you fly rather than at the border. Longer stays typically mean pairing that with a visa run, a sponsored work permit if you land a local employer, or a business visa arranged through a registered company — Vietnam still does not offer a dedicated digital nomad visa, so most remote workers technically operate in a grey zone on tourist or e-visa status while paying local rent and living like residents. If you plan to stay past a single 90-day window, build in either an exit-and-re-entry trip (Bangkok and Kuala Lumpur are common weekend runs) or start the conversation with an immigration agent early, since renewal rules shift often enough that what worked for a friend last year may not apply this year.

Housing is where Hanoi's dual character shows up clearest. Tay Ho (West Lake) is the expat-heavy district: leafy streets, lakeside cafes, international schools nearby, and a large community of long-term residents, with one-bedroom apartments generally running USD 450 to 800 depending on building age and whether it's a serviced unit. The Old Quarter sits at the opposite end of the experience — narrow lanes, constant street noise, motorbike traffic at all hours, and genuine proximity to the city's food scene — with comparable one-bedrooms often landing USD 400 to 700. Many newcomers spend their first two weeks in a short-let in the Old Quarter to get oriented, then relocate to Tay Ho or the Ba Dinh area once they understand their daily commute and noise tolerance. Whichever district you choose, budget a deposit of one to two months plus a signing fee, and always view a unit in person before wiring money, since photos rarely capture how close the nearest karaoke bar is.

Air quality deserves its own line item in your planning, not a footnote. Hanoi's winter months — roughly November through February — regularly push the Air Quality Index into the 100 to 150 range on bad days, driven by a mix of regional agricultural burning, construction dust, and temperature inversions that trap pollutants over the city. That's the "unhealthy for sensitive groups" band on most AQI scales, and it's a real consideration if you have asthma, young children, or simply want to run outdoors. Many long-term residents keep an air purifier running at home and check an app like AirVisual before planning outdoor exercise during those months; it's a minor adjustment but one that catches newcomers off guard if nobody mentions it before they sign a year-long lease.

On the money side, a realistic monthly nomad budget in Hanoi typically lands between USD 800 and 1,400, and that figure includes rent, food, transport, and basic entertainment, but not health insurance or a coworking membership if you need a desk outside your apartment. Motorbike rental or purchase is close to mandatory for daily life outside the Old Quarter core — taxis and ride-hailing apps like Grab work fine but add up quickly if used daily. Health insurance is worth arranging before you land rather than after: private clinics serving the expat community (Family Medical Practice and Vinmec among them) are reliable but not cheap without coverage, and public facilities are a harder experience without Vietnamese language skills. Build a first-month buffer of 15 to 25 percent above your projected budget to absorb deposit costs, SIM and banking setup, and the inevitable unplanned taxi rides while you learn the city's geography.
