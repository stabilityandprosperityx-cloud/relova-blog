Hungary's "White Card" is one of the more restrictive digital nomad permits in the EU on paper — it explicitly bars local employment — but it's also one of the cheapest to apply for and among the fastest to process.

## Who qualifies, and what it costs

The White Card is aimed squarely at non-EU/EEA remote workers and freelancers earning from clients or employers based outside Hungary; you cannot use it to work for a Hungarian company or take on Hungarian clients, and doing so risks the permit being revoked. Income requirements commonly cited by immigration lawyers and consulates sit around EUR 3,000 net per month from foreign sources, though applicants should confirm the exact current figure with the Hungarian consulate handling their application, since minimums can be adjusted. The application fee itself is modest, typically in the EUR 60-100 range, and processing commonly takes two to four weeks, though timelines vary by consulate location and season. The permit is issued for one year initially, with one renewal option bringing the maximum continuous stay under this specific status to two years — after that, White Card holders need to switch to a different residence category or leave and reapply fresh later.

## Where people live, and what a [Budapest](/blog/move-to-budapest-guide-2026) lease costs

Budapest absorbs the overwhelming majority of Hungary's remote-worker population, and rent varies sharply by district: central, well-connected districts (V, VI, VII — the old Jewish Quarter and downtown) command one-bedroom rents commonly in the HUF 200,000-350,000 range, while outer districts and up-and-coming areas like District IX (Ferencváros) or District XIII run noticeably cheaper. Utilities, a monthly transit pass, and groceries add a few hundred euros on top for most single applicants budgeting realistically.

## Tax reality check

Hungary is frequently cited for a "flat 15% personal income tax," and that rate is broadly accurate for Hungarian tax residents on Hungarian-taxable income — but White Card holders whose income is foreign-sourced and who remain tax resident elsewhere may not owe Hungarian income tax on that income at all, depending on the specifics of their contract, days-present count, and any double-taxation treaty between Hungary and their home country. This is genuinely case-by-case, and it's worth getting a written opinion from a Hungarian accountant rather than assuming the flat rate automatically applies to remote foreign income.

## Healthcare and daily logistics

White Card holders are generally required to hold private health insurance valid in Hungary for the duration of their permit, since the permit itself does not grant automatic access to Hungary's public healthcare (TAJ) system the way employment-based residence does. Budapest's healthcare infrastructure is solid by regional standards, with a mix of public hospitals and a growing private clinic sector that caters specifically to expats and international patients, often with English-speaking staff. Public transport in Budapest — trams, buses, and a compact metro system including the historic Line 1 — is inexpensive and extensive enough that most single professionals don't need a car, which helps offset some of the cost of private insurance and other visa-related expenses.

## What the White Card doesn't cover

Because the permit explicitly excludes local employment, White Card holders who later want to take a Hungarian job, start a Hungarian-registered business, or otherwise shift their income source need to apply for a different residence category entirely — the White Card isn't a stepping stone to Hungarian work authorization by itself. Family members can generally be included as dependents on a related application, but the exact process and required documentation differ from the primary applicant's, so it's worth confirming dependent procedures with the consulate at the same time as the main application rather than assuming automatic inclusion.
