[Bali](/blog/move-to-lombok-guide-2026) dominates the Indonesia relocation conversation, but the archipelago's 17,000-plus islands include a functioning megacity (Jakarta), a low-cost cultural capital (Yogyakarta), and dozens of second-tier cities where the visa math and cost of living look very different from [Canggu](/blog/canggu-vs-ubud-vs-seminyak-bali-guide-2026) or Ubud.

## Three visa routes, three very different profiles

Indonesia's Second Home Visa (KITAS index E33) targets people with capital rather than income: it requires roughly IDR 2 billion (about USD 130,000) held in an Indonesian state-bank account under the applicant's own name, or alternatively ownership of Indonesian property worth at least USD 1 million. In exchange, it grants five or ten years of residency with no age restriction and no employment or pension income documentation required — it's essentially a savings-based long-stay visa. The E33G remote-worker KITAS, by contrast, is built for people earning from abroad: it typically references a monthly income threshold around USD 2,000, verified through pay stubs or client contracts, and comes with long validity but requires proof the income originates outside Indonesia. The Retirement KITAS (E33F/E33E) sits between the two: available to applicants generally 55 and older, it asks for proof of pension or regular income around USD 1,500/month plus, for some variants, a required deposit — a genuinely different qualification path than either the savings-based Second Home Visa or the income-based E33G.

## Jakarta versus the rest

Jakarta is Indonesia's financial and diplomatic center — the only city with a genuinely deep expat job market, embassies, and international schools at scale — but that comes with Southeast Asia's worst traffic congestion and one-bedroom rents around USD 400-800/month depending on the district (Kemang and SCBD command the premium end; Cikini and Menteng offer more mid-range options). Yogyakarta, by contrast, is Java's cultural and university heart, home to Borobudur and Prambanan temple complexes nearby, with one-bedroom rents closer to USD 200-400/month and a much slower pace of life. Neither city has [Bali](/blog/bali-vs-thailand-expat-guide-2026)'s built-in remote-work infrastructure (co-working density, English-speaking service economy), so plan for a steeper local-language learning curve.

## Bahasa Indonesia is a genuine advantage to learn

Unlike many Asian languages, Bahasa Indonesia uses the Latin alphabet, has no tonal system, and follows largely regular grammar rules — it's routinely cited by linguists as one of the more approachable languages for English speakers to reach conversational fluency in within a few months of consistent study. That said, English proficiency drops off quickly outside Jakarta's business districts and [Bali](/blog/moving-to-bali-guide-2026)'s tourist zones, so even basic Bahasa goes a long way for daily errands, landlord negotiations, and local bureaucracy (immigration offices, kelurahan/village registration) in Yogyakarta or smaller cities.

## Infrastructure varies enormously by island

With 17,000+ islands spanning three time zones, "moving to Indonesia" means radically different infrastructure depending on where you land. Java (Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Bandung, Surabaya) has reliable fiber internet, hospitals with international accreditation, and dense transport networks. Move to Sumatra, Sulawesi, or the outer islands and internet reliability, healthcare access, and even reliable electricity become real planning variables — worth a scouting trip before committing to a lease anywhere outside the Java-Bali corridor.

## The visa application sequence and what to sort out first

Most of these KITAS routes require an initial application through an Indonesian consulate or online e-visa portal before arrival, followed by conversion to the physical KITAS card and a separate KITAP (permanent stay permit) eligibility clock that starts only after a set number of years on renewed temporary permits. A local sponsor — often an immigration agent or lawyer acting as the registered agent — is standard practice for the Second Home and E33G routes, since Indonesian immigration bureaucracy (Direktorat Jenderal Imigrasi) still runs largely through in-person provincial office visits for biometrics and card issuance, even where the initial application went digital. Budget for agent fees on top of the visa's own cost, and confirm current processing times directly with a licensed agent, since Indonesia's immigration rules and digital-portal reliability have shifted meaningfully across the past few visa policy cycles.
