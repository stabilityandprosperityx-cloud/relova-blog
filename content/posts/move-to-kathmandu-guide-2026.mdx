Kathmandu draws a narrow but loyal slice of the expat world: trekking guides, NGO staff, Buddhist and yoga practitioners, and remote workers who want Himalayan access without Western prices. Nepal's on-arrival tourist visa costs $30 for 15 days, $50 for 30 days, or $125 for 90 days, payable in cash or card at Tribhuvan International Airport, and it can be extended online through the Department of Immigration's e-visa portal in 15-day blocks (a minimum extension runs about $45, with roughly $3 per additional day). Tourist visas can be extended for up to 150 days in a calendar year, but they are not a legal path to long-term residence — Nepal has no [digital nomad visa](/blog/best-digital-nomad-destinations-beginners-2026), so most long-stayers either run a visa-run cycle, register a local NGO or business that qualifies for a business/work visa, or study Nepali/yoga/meditation under a student-linked visa sponsored by a registered institution. None of these routes are trivial to arrange from abroad; expect to spend real time at the Department of Immigration office in Kalimati.

## Where people actually live

Thamel is the default tourist-adjacent base — noisy, walkable, full of trekking shops and cafes — with one-bedroom furnished flats running roughly NPR 25,000–45,000 ($185–330) a month. Patan (Lalitpur), across the Bagmati River, is quieter and popular with longer-term expats and NGO staff, with comparable one-bedrooms closer to NPR 20,000–40,000. Budhanilkantha and Bouddha, near the great stupa, suit people prioritizing cleaner air and a slower pace over central-city convenience. Unfurnished long-term leases are usually cheaper than furnished short lets but require a local guarantor or agent, and landlords commonly ask for several months' rent as deposit. A realistic all-in nomad budget — rent, food, a local SIM, occasional taxis, and basic health cover — lands around $600–1,000 a month for a single person living reasonably well by local standards, well below Southeast Asian hubs like Chiang Mai or Bali.

## The trade-off nobody skips: air quality

Kathmandu's valley geography traps pollution, and winter (roughly November through February) is the worst stretch, with AQI readings commonly running 100–200 and spiking higher during dry spells and brick-kiln season. This is not a minor footnote — anyone with asthma, allergies, or young children should plan around it: HEPA air purifiers are available locally but not cheap, N95/KN95 masks are standard daily wear for many residents, and some expats simply leave the valley (for Pokhara, India, or Southeast Asia) during the worst months. Internet reliability varies sharply by building and area — fiber from providers like WorldLink and Vianet is solid in Thamel, Patan, and Baneshwor, but load-shedding and last-mile cabling issues still bite in older neighborhoods, so test the actual connection at a rental before signing anything.

## Getting online, banking, and daily errands

Opening a local bank account as a foreigner generally requires a business or work visa rather than a tourist stamp, so most short-to-medium-term residents rely on a combination of cash, international cards, and services like Wise for receiving foreign income — ATM withdrawal fees add up quickly, and it's worth checking which local banks (Nabil, Himalayan Bank, Standard Chartered) waive fees for foreign cards before relying on any one of them daily. A local SIM from Ncell or Nepal Telecom costs a few dollars and gives reasonably fast 4G in the valley, though speeds drop noticeably outside Kathmandu and Pokhara. Grocery shopping splits between local wet markets, which are far cheaper for produce, and a small number of supermarkets (Bhat-Bhateni is the main chain) that stock imported goods at a real markup — most long-stayers mix both rather than relying on either exclusively.

## Altitude, trekking access, and seasonal planning

Kathmandu itself sits at roughly 1,400 meters, low enough that altitude isn't a daily concern in the city, but it's the launch point for treks into the Everest, Annapurna, and Langtang regions where altitude sickness becomes a real planning factor — anyone basing here specifically for trekking access should budget time (and travel insurance with high-altitude evacuation coverage) accordingly. The pre- and post-monsoon windows, roughly March–May and September–November, are the clearest and most popular months for both trekking and simply living comfortably in the valley; the June–August monsoon brings heavy rain and landslide risk on mountain roads, and the winter months bring the air-quality problems described above. Timing a first visit or move around these seasonal patterns, rather than arriving mid-monsoon or deep winter without expecting it, makes the adjustment considerably smoother.
