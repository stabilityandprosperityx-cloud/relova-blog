Koh Samui runs on a mix of retirees, European and Russian-speaking families, and short-haul tourists, spread across an island of roughly 65,000 registered residents with an expat population informally estimated above 10,000. That blend shows up in daily life more than most travel guides let on — this isn't a backpacker island, it's closer to a small, international, semi-permanent town with a tourism economy layered on top. Long-term legal options split mainly between the Thailand LTR (Long-Term Resident) visa, aimed at wealthier applicants with figures near $80,000 in annual income appearing in official program materials for the relevant category, and the more traditional routes — marriage to a Thai national, an employer-sponsored work permit, or retirement-linked extensions for those over 50 — each with its own income and documentation requirements that are worth confirming against the current Thailand Board of Investment (BOI) list rather than older marketing copy, since LTR program details have been adjusted since launch.

Neighborhood choice on Samui functions almost like choosing a different island. Chaweng is the loudest and busiest — nightlife, the main tourist strip, and the highest concentration of short-term visitors, which some residents love and others actively avoid. Lamai offers a quieter version of the same beach-town formula. Bophut's Fisherman's Village blends boutique hotels with a genuinely good dining scene and a more walkable, less frenetic feel than Chaweng. Maenam, on the island's north side, is where a lot of budget-conscious families and longer-term residents land — it's quieter, more residential, and one-bedroom rentals commonly run THB 8,000 to 14,000 a month, noticeably below the more central options; Bophut one-bedrooms typically run a bit higher, in the THB 10,000 to 18,000 range, reflecting its more polished dining and hospitality scene.

Healthcare access is genuinely good by regional standards, which matters on an island a long way from Bangkok's larger hospital network. Bangkok Hospital Samui carries JCI (Joint Commission International) accreditation, the same standard used to benchmark hospital quality globally, and is generally the go-to for anything serious — expect private-hospital pricing without insurance, so factor a real health insurance policy into your budget rather than treating it as optional. For inter-island and mainland connections, the Surat Thani ferry takes roughly two hours and is weather-dependent enough that it's worth building slack into any travel plans that hinge on it; flights to Bangkok run around an hour and are frequent, making Samui more connected than its island setting might suggest.

Seasonality is worth planning around too — the rainy season, roughly October through December, brings the heaviest and most disruptive weather of the year, with ferry cancellations and flooded roads more common in that window than any other time. Newcomers moving mid-year often don't experience this firsthand until their first full year in, so ask current residents about backup plans (alternative routes off the island, generator access, flood-prone streets to avoid) before committing to a lease in a low-lying area. On the money side, a comfortable monthly budget for a nomad or early retiree runs somewhere between $1,200 and $2,000, covering rent, food, scooter rental or fuel, and private healthcare copays, though that range climbs quickly if you're renting in Bophut or Chaweng rather than Maenam, or paying for international-school tuition for kids. Samui rewards people who choose their neighborhood deliberately rather than defaulting to whichever area looked best on a week-long vacation.

Connectivity for remote work has improved considerably in recent years — fiber internet is now standard in most residential buildings across Chaweng, Bophut, and Maenam, and a handful of coworking spaces have opened to serve the island's growing nomad population, though the scene remains far smaller than Chiang Mai's or Bangkok's. Most remote workers end up working from home or a cafe rather than a dedicated desk, which works fine given the island's relaxed pace but is worth knowing before you assume a Bangkok-style coworking culture. Schooling is another consideration for families: Samui has a small but established international school scene serving the expat and long-term-visitor community, generally cheaper than Bangkok's top-tier international schools but still a meaningful line item worth researching before you commit to a lease near a specific campus.
