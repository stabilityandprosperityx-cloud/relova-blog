[Kotor](/blog/kotor-vs-budva-montenegro-guide-2026) sits at the innermost point of the Bay of Kotor, a dramatic, fjord-like inlet of the Adriatic ringed by steep limestone mountains that drop almost straight into the water — it's the geography, as much as the medieval old town itself, that explains why people choose Kotor over its flashier neighbor Budva. Where Budva built its identity around beach clubs and a nightlife-driven summer economy, Kotor's draw is the UNESCO World Heritage-listed old town: a walled, car-free maze of Venetian-era stone buildings wrapped around a working harbor, with the famous fortress walls climbing the mountainside directly above it. That distinction matters for anyone actually planning to live there, not just visit — Kotor's town center functions more like a historic residential quarter than a resort strip, which shapes everything from rental stock to daily noise levels to how you'll do your grocery shopping.

The trade-off newcomers need to understand before falling for the postcard: Kotor is one of the busiest cruise ports on the Adriatic relative to its size, and the crowding pattern is genuinely predictable rather than random. Large ships dock and disgorge several thousand passengers at a time into an old town that covers only a few hundred meters end to end, and on peak days — typically clustered around the late spring through early autumn cruise season — the narrow stone lanes inside the walls become difficult to move through between roughly mid-morning and late afternoon. Residents who live inside or immediately adjacent to the old walls learn to run errands early or after the ships depart in the evening; those who find the daytime crush intolerable often gravitate to nearby villages along the bay — Dobrota, Muo, or Prčanj — which offer walking or short-drive access to Kotor's old town without living inside the daily cruise-tide. It's a genuinely different lifestyle decision than choosing between two Budva apartment blocks, and worth test-living through at least one high season before committing to a year-long lease inside the walls.

The legal path to residency changed meaningfully as of 17 January 2026. Previously, buying almost any property — even a modest apartment — could support a temporary residence application. Under the new rule, temporary residence linked to property ownership now requires the Tax Administration's assessed value of the property to be at least €150,000; the number that matters is the official tax assessment, not your purchase price or the listing price, so a bargain that looks like €100,000 on paper can still clear the bar if the assessed value differs, and a €150,000 listing can still fall short if it doesn't. Anyone who already held residence through a lower-value property before that date keeps transitional protection on renewal, but new applicants need to budget for the higher threshold or look at Montenegro's alternative path: registering and genuinely operating a local LLC, with share capital starting as low as about €1, provided you can show real business activity rather than a shell registration. From temporary residence, permanent residence generally follows after five continuous years, and citizenship roughly five years after that — around a decade total — and naturalized citizens typically need to renounce prior citizenship, since Montenegro does not broadly permit dual citizenship through naturalization outside specific exceptions.

On cost of living, a one- to two-bedroom in the old town or immediately around it commonly rents near €800 a month, and a comfortable couple's budget — rent, food, utilities, the occasional dinner out — generally lands in the €1,500 to €2,000 range. Property prices for new construction nationally averaged roughly €2,206 per square meter in late 2025, rising to about €2,570 per square meter along the coast, a useful benchmark if you're reverse-engineering whether a specific unit could plausibly clear the new €150,000 tax-assessed threshold. Kotor rewards people drawn to its geography and history rather than its beaches, but it demands a clear-eyed read of both the cruise-season crowding and the tighter 2026 residency math before you commit.
