Lagos is Africa's largest city by most population estimates, home to somewhere between 15 and 20 million people depending on which metro definition you use, and it's Nigeria's undisputed commercial and financial hub — but it demands more intentional planning around infrastructure than most first-world relocations do, and pretending otherwise sets people up for a rough first quarter.

## Choose your neighborhood around infrastructure, not just price

Victoria Island (VI) is the traditional core expat and business district — banks, embassies, and international offices cluster here, and one-bedroom rents commonly run ₦300,000-600,000/month, typically quoted and paid annually in a lump sum, a Lagos rental-market norm that catches first-time movers off guard. Ikoyi, adjacent and generally considered the premium, more secure residential option with better road infrastructure and gated compounds, runs higher at roughly ₦400,000-800,000/month for a comparable one-bedroom. Lekki, particularly Lekki Phase 1 and the newer Lekki-Epe corridor, has become the default choice for many younger expats and returnees for its newer housing stock and lower relative cost versus VI/Ikoyi, though it comes with a longer, traffic-dependent commute into the VI/Ikoyi business core. Whichever district you pick, verify the building's actual power and water setup before signing — advertised amenities and lived reality diverge more in Lagos real estate than almost anywhere else, and a "24-hour power" claim needs to be confirmed with current tenants, not the agent.

## Power is the infrastructure problem that shapes daily life

Nigeria's national grid delivers famously unreliable supply, with gaps commonly cited in the 12-16 hours a day range depending on district and substation — meaning most residential and commercial buildings run on private generators as a baseline expectation, not a backup contingency. Budget for diesel or petrol costs as a genuine recurring line item, and when apartment-hunting, ask specifically about the building's generator capacity, fuel-cost-sharing arrangement among tenants, and whether solar/inverter backup supplements the generator — buildings with robust inverter systems command a real rent premium and are worth it for anyone working remotely on a schedule that can't tolerate multi-hour outages.

## Visa reality: sponsorship-dependent, not open-door

Nigeria does not offer a digital nomad visa or straightforward long-stay tourist-to-resident pathway as of 2026. Most foreign professionals enter on a Temporary Work Permit (TWP) for short-term assignments or a subject-to-regularization Combined Expatriate Residence Permit and Aliens Card (CERPAC) tied to sponsored employment — meaning you generally need a Nigerian-registered employer or your own registered business to sponsor the residency process. This is a materially different model from Southeast Asia or Latin America's nomad-friendly visa landscape, and anyone planning to relocate without a confirmed local employer or established business entity should treat visa sponsorship as the first item to solve, not a later formality.

## Budget realistically, and separate lifestyle from safety costs

A realistic all-in expat monthly budget — rent, driver or ride-hailing transport (traffic makes a personal driver common practice among longer-term expats), private healthcare, groceries with a meaningful import-goods premium, and basic security measures — lands around USD 2,000-4,000/month for a single professional in VI, Ikoyi, or Lekki, scaling up with family size and international school fees, which are a significant separate line item for anyone relocating with children. Traffic (Lagos "go-slow") is a genuine daily-life variable worth planning routes and work hours around, not a minor inconvenience — a cross-town trip that looks like 20 minutes on a map can regularly take 90 minutes to two hours during peak periods.

## Healthcare and security planning

Private healthcare in Lagos, concentrated around facilities in VI, Ikoyi, and Lekki, generally offers a materially higher standard of care than public hospitals, and most expats carry private international health insurance rather than relying on local public facilities for anything beyond routine care — budget this as a distinct line item from the general monthly figure above, since serious treatment or medical evacuation coverage adds real cost. Security is genuinely area-dependent: VI, Ikoyi, and the more established parts of Lekki have visible private security, gated compounds, and lower reported incident rates than some other parts of greater Lagos, but common-sense precautions (avoiding travel after dark in unfamiliar areas, using vetted drivers, registering with your embassy) remain standard practice even in the safer expat-heavy districts. Talk to current residents in your target building or estate before signing a year-long lease — Lagos real estate listings routinely oversell security and power reliability relative to what tenants actually experience.
