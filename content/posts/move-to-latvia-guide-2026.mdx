Riga's UNESCO-listed Art Nouveau district is the postcard, but the practical story for 2026 movers is two visa routes that work very differently from how forum threads still describe them, plus a rental market that has stayed genuinely affordable by Western European standards.

## The [Startup](/blog/move-to-finland-guide-2026) Visa dropped its capital requirement

Latvia's [Startup](/blog/move-to-ireland-guide-2026) Visa has been reformed, and the older "€50,000 investment or €25,000 with accelerator backing" framing that still circulates online is out of date. The program now runs with **no fixed minimum capital requirement** on the company itself — what actually gets checked is personal financial capacity to live in Latvia while the business finds its feet, generally documented through bank statements showing roughly **€8,800** available for an adult applicant (with a lower threshold per accompanying child). The emphasis has shifted toward proving an *actually operating* business — real customer transactions and verifiable market demand — rather than a pitch deck and a bank transfer. Processing is quoted at around 30 days once a complete file is in, but "complete" now means operating evidence, not just a funding letter, so budget more prep time than the old capital-based version required. Confirm the current documentation checklist with the Investment and Development Agency of Latvia (LIAA) before you assemble a file, since this is a program that has visibly moved since the last time most guides were written.

## The EU [Blue Card](/blog/move-to-belgium-guide-2026) is a job-contract route, not a bank-balance one

Latvia's EU Blue Card works on the same logic as the rest of the EU version of the scheme: it requires a signed job offer or contract with a Latvian employer at or above a published salary floor, recalculated periodically against Latvia's national average wage. Figures circulating around **€14,900 a year** are the kind of number that moves with each year's wage statistics — treat it as a floor to re-verify against the current year's official gazette figure rather than a fixed target, since it is explicitly wage-indexed and not a static law. Unlike the [Startup](/blog/best-countries-engineers-abroad-2026) Visa, there is no self-employment version: you need an employer willing to sponsor, and the contract terms (minimum duration, job classification) matter as much as the salary line.

## Riga rent is still genuinely cheap for the EU

A one-bedroom in central Riga typically runs **€450–750 a month**, which undercuts most Western and even several other Baltic capitals for comparable space. The spread depends heavily on building era — Soviet-era panel blocks sit at the bottom of that range, restored Art Nouveau buildings near the old town toward the top — and on whether heating is centrally metered, which matters more than it sounds like in a city where winter humidity works its way into poorly insulated older stock. Utilities in an older building can add a meaningful line to a "cheap rent" budget between November and March, so ask specifically about heating costs before signing a winter lease.

## Why Latvia over Estonia or Lithuania

The three Baltic states pitch similar packages, and Latvia's actual differentiator is scale without Tallinn's tourist-driven cost inflation: Riga is bigger and more international than Vilnius, with a longer runway of English-language services, while staying meaningfully cheaper than Tallinn's now well-established digital-nomad-driven rental market. If your business genuinely needs Estonia's e-Residency infrastructure specifically, that is a different calculation — but for someone simply choosing a Baltic base on cost and livability, Riga's current rent bands are doing real work in Latvia's favor.
