## What This Guide Covers Beyond Tax and Banking

Most guides aimed at Americans moving to [Lisbon](/blog/lisbon-american-taxes-banking-2026) lead with tax and banking — the FEIE exclusion, FBAR reporting, FATCA-related account friction — and those are genuinely important, but they're covered thoroughly elsewhere. What trips Americans up in practice tends to be a different, more mundane set of issues that don't show up until you're actually living in [Portugal](/blog/move-to-lisbon-family-guide-2026) day to day, starting with something as basic as your driver's license.

## Why US Licenses Don't Simply Transfer

Portugal does not have a blanket reciprocal driving-license agreement covering US states the way it does with EU and many other countries — and unlike some European countries that maintain exchange agreements with a handful of individual US states, Portugal's list of countries eligible for a straightforward license exchange does not currently include the United States. In practice, that generally means Americans relocating to [Lisbon](/blog/move-to-lisbon-budget-guide-2026) need to sit a full Portuguese driving test — theory and practical — to convert to a Portuguese license once their US or International Driving Permit privileges run out (typically after a set window from establishing residency, commonly cited around six months to a year depending on your specific visa and residency status). This is a genuinely disruptive discovery for a lot of Americans who assumed a valid US license would simply convert on request, and the driving school and exam process can take weeks to months to complete, so it's worth starting the research and paperwork early rather than waiting until your existing permit is about to expire.

## Why US Health Coverage and Medicare Don't Travel

Health insurance is the second place US-specific assumptions cause real friction. American private health insurance plans, including most employer-sponsored coverage, generally do not extend meaningful coverage once you've relocated abroad, and Medicare in particular does not cover care received outside the United States except in narrow, specific circumstances. That means Americans moving to [Lisbon](/blog/athens-vs-lisbon-expat-guide-2026) need to actively arrange either Portuguese private health insurance or, once eligible, access to the Serviço Nacional de Saúde (SNS), Portugal's public healthcare system — neither of which happens automatically just because you've moved. There's typically a gap between landing and having either option fully active, so budget for private international health insurance to bridge that period, and don't assume any US coverage travels with you the way a debit card does.

## Shipping Belongings and Voltage Differences

Shipping belongings from the US is another area where the practical reality diverges from what people expect. International moving companies that specialize in US-to-Europe household shipments exist and work well, but full container shipments typically take six to ten weeks in transit plus customs clearance on the Portuguese side, and duty exemptions for personal household goods generally require documentation proving the items were owned and used before your move — not simply an inventory list. Most experienced movers end up shipping a much smaller subset of belongings than they originally planned, both because of cost and because European apartment sizes and voltage differences (Portugal runs 230V, versus 120V in the US) make a lot of American furniture and appliances impractical to bring anyway.

## Bureaucracy Pace and Customer Service Culture Shock

Finally, the culture-shock points that catch Americans specifically, more than other nationalities, tend to cluster around pace and customer service norms. Portuguese bureaucracy moves on its own schedule — appointment backlogs at AIMA (Portugal's immigration and asylum agency) and at local Finanças or Câmara Municipal offices are common and not a sign anything has gone wrong, just the normal pace of the system. Customer service culture is also genuinely different: the immediate, highly attentive service Americans are used to in restaurants and shops is not the local default, and interpreting a slower, less overtly friendly interaction as rudeness rather than simply a different service norm is one of the most common early friction points Americans report. None of this is a reason to avoid the move — it's simply worth knowing before you land, so the first few months feel like an adjustment rather than a series of unpleasant surprises.
