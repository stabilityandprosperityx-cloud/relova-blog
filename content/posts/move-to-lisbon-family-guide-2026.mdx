## International School Versus Public School Costs

Moving to [Lisbon](/blog/move-to-lisbon-american-guide-2026) with kids raises a set of questions that a solo or couple relocation simply doesn't: which school system, which neighborhood commute, and who's watching your toddler while you attend your first AIMA appointment. The school decision tends to dominate family planning, and it's genuinely a fork in the road rather than a minor preference. International schools teaching in English (British, American, or IB curricula) commonly run €8,000 to €15,000 a year at the primary level, climbing to roughly €16,000 to €26,500 a year for the IB Diploma track at the top schools, with bilingual Portuguese-English options generally cheaper, around €7,500 to €14,000. On top of tuition, budget for one-off enrollment fees (€800 to €3,500), annual registration (€150 to €400), and a school bus if you're not within walking distance (€2,200 to €3,800 a year) — for two kids in a mid-tier international school, a realistic first-year total including one-off costs commonly lands in the €42,000 to €62,000 range. The alternative — enrolling in Portugal's free public school system — is dramatically cheaper but means your kids learn in Portuguese from day one, which works well for younger children who pick up language quickly and poorly for teenagers arriving mid-secondary-school with no Portuguese background; most families choosing the public route also budget for private Portuguese tutoring in the first year to smooth that transition.

## Restelo, Parque das Nações, and the Western School Belt

Most of [Lisbon](/blog/move-to-lisbon-budget-guide-2026)'s international school campuses cluster in the western belt — Cascais, Estoril, Sintra, and Oeiras — which is the single biggest reason families relocating for school access tend to settle outside central [Lisbon](/blog/lisbon-american-taxes-banking-2026) rather than in it. Restelo, a leafy, established residential district on the way toward that western belt, is a longtime favorite with international families for its larger apartments, green space, and easier access to both the western-belt schools and central [Lisbon](/blog/lisbon-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026). Parque das Nações, the modern riverside district built for Expo '98, is the other common family pick — newer buildings, wider streets, more playgrounds and family-oriented amenities than the older, narrower-laned central neighborhoods, though it sits further from the western schools. Families who choose central Lisbon addresses for the walkability and culture generally accept a 25- to 50-minute commute to western-belt schools as the trade-off; families who prioritize school proximity often end up basing themselves in Cascais or Oeiras directly and treating central Lisbon as a weekend destination rather than a daily commute.

## Creche Waiting Lists and Private Nurseries

Childcare for younger children follows its own separate track and its own separate waiting list. Portuguese creches (daycare centers) — public, private, and the subsidized "IPSS" nonprofit-run centers — serve children generally from a few months old through age three, with public and IPSS options considerably cheaper than private international nurseries but also considerably harder to secure a spot in, especially mid-year; families often need to apply months in advance, sometimes before they've even landed. Private international nurseries catering specifically to expat families are easier to get into on short notice but carry meaningfully higher monthly fees, generally more in line with private-school pricing than public-system pricing. If childcare timing matters for your relocation date, start that search in parallel with your visa application rather than after you arrive.

## Pediatric Care Through SNS and Private Hospitals

Pediatric healthcare access follows the same public-versus-private split as adult care. Portugal's public SNS system includes pediatric care and is generally well regarded, but access requires legal residency to be established first, and appointment availability varies by region — the western-belt municipalities generally have solid public health center coverage, but non-emergency specialist waits can run longer than private options. Most families carry private health insurance through at least the first year, both to bridge the gap before SNS access is active and because several private hospitals in the Lisbon area (Hospital CUF and Hospital da Luz networks among them) offer dedicated pediatric units with English-speaking staff, which matters in the early months before your Portuguese is functional enough to navigate a medical appointment solo.

## Family Reunification Rules Under Law 61/2025

Finally, family reunification rules changed under Law 61/2025 in a way that specifically affects how families should sequence their move: adult dependents — a spouse or parents — who don't travel together with the main visa applicant now generally face a two-year wait tied to the principal applicant's legal residence before they can join, unless a specific exception applies. The practical fix most immigration advisors recommend is filing the entire family together at the consulate or VFS Global appointment in your home country from the outset, rather than assuming you can send the primary applicant ahead and bring the rest of the family over "next summer" — that assumption is exactly what the new rule penalizes.
