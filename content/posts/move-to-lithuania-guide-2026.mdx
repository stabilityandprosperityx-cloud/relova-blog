Lithuania doesn't get the same relocation-content attention as Portugal or Spain, but it offers a genuinely compelling combination for the right profile: full EU and Schengen membership, the euro as currency since 2015, a flat and comparatively simple tax system, and a capital city with a real, fast-growing tech sector rather than just a scenic backdrop. Vilnius has become a notable hub for fintech and engineering talent — Revolut, among other companies, has built a significant engineering presence there — which means the city has a denser professional and social infrastructure for tech workers than its relatively modest size would suggest.

For non-EU founders and remote entrepreneurs, the main dedicated pathway is Lithuania's [Startup](/blog/move-to-ireland-guide-2026) Visa, which public program materials commonly reference alongside a capital requirement around €15,000 and, more importantly, a defensible, innovative business plan that Lithuania's [startup](/blog/move-to-albania-guide-2026) agency, [Startup](/blog/move-to-amman-jordan-guide-2026) Lithuania (part of Verslo Lietuva, Lithuania's enterprise agency), needs to evaluate and approve before the visa moves forward. This isn't a passive-income or simple remote-employment route — it's specifically built for people actually founding or co-founding an innovative company in Lithuania, and the approval process involves real scrutiny of the business plan's substance, not just proof of funds. Confirm current capital thresholds and evaluation criteria directly with Verslo Lietuva before committing to lease coworking space or make other financial commitments, since program details are revised periodically.

Tax structure is one of Lithuania's clearer selling points: personal income tax is a flat 20 percent, and the standard corporate tax rate is 15 percent, both simpler to plan around than the tiered, threshold-heavy systems common elsewhere in Western Europe. That said, "flat rate" doesn't mean "no compliance obligations" — if you're invoicing clients across the EU, you'll still need to manage payroll registration correctly and understand permanent establishment (PE) rules, which determine whether your business activity creates a taxable presence in Lithuania versus wherever your clients are based. This is genuinely worth a session with a Lithuanian accountant early on rather than assuming the flat rates mean the paperwork is equally simple.

Housing costs vary meaningfully across Lithuania's three main cities, and the differences map fairly directly onto what each city offers. Vilnius, the capital and largest city, commands the highest rents — one-bedroom apartments commonly run €500 to €800 a month — justified by its concentration of [startup](/blog/move-to-bahrain-guide-2026)s, larger job market, and the best international flight connections in the country. Kaunas, Lithuania's second city and a major university town, runs noticeably cheaper at €400 to €650, trading some of Vilnius's job density for lower costs and a strong student-driven social and cultural scene. Klaipėda, the country's main port city on the Baltic coast, is the most affordable of the three at €350 to €550, built around port industry and logistics rather than tech, and it's the natural pick for anyone prioritizing coastal access and a quieter pace over Vilnius's more career-dense environment.

Lithuania suits a specific but real niche well: EU-market-facing founders who qualify for the Startup Visa, remote workers who want genuine EU and Schengen access without the cost of Western Europe's major capitals, and anyone drawn to a smaller, well-connected Baltic base with a straightforward flat-tax system. It's a less obvious pick for retirees or passive-income movers, since Lithuania's visa infrastructure is built more around work and entrepreneurship than the passive-residency routes common in Southern Europe — worth keeping in mind before comparing it directly against a country like Portugal or Italy that's built its immigration system around a broader range of applicant profiles.

Day-to-day infrastructure across all three cities is solid by regional standards: reliable fiber internet, an efficient public transit network within Vilnius and Kaunas, and English widely spoken in tech and service industries, particularly among younger residents. Winters are long and dark, similar to the rest of the Baltics and Scandinavia, which is worth factoring in if you haven't lived at that latitude before — most newcomers adjust within a season or two, but it's a real, not cosmetic, part of relocating there.
