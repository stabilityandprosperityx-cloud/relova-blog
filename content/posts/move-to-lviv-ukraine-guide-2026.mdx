This guide is deliberately narrower in scope than most of what you'll find on this blog. Ukraine has been under martial law since February 2022, and Lviv, despite sitting in the country's far west, is not exempt from that reality. Nothing below is written to talk anyone into or out of a move — it is what the practical, logistical picture actually looks like as of 2026, and it changes often enough that every figure here needs re-checking against a current source before you act on it.

## Lviv is safer than the front, not safe

Lviv's distance from active combat lines makes it statistically one of Ukraine's lower-risk major cities, and that distinction is real — but it is a distinction of degree, not of safety in any absolute sense. The city has taken direct hits: a drone and missile strike reached the historic center in March 2026, and a July 2026 missile attack killed one person and injured more than thirty, including children. Ukraine recorded roughly 5,920 air strikes nationwide in June 2026 alone. Any relocation plan needs an actual answer to what happens if an air-raid alert sounds at 3 a.m. — where the nearest shelter is, what a go-bag contains, and what the exit plan looks like — not a hope that the war stays elsewhere.

## Entry is visa-free; staying is not automatic

US citizens can currently enter Ukraine visa-free for stays up to 90 days within a 180-day window, with a valid passport, proof of onward travel or accommodation, and — practically, if not always formally required at the border — proof of health and war-risk insurance. Standard travel insurance policies routinely exclude war-related injury by default; anyone relocating needs a policy that explicitly names war-risk coverage, and this is worth confirming in writing with the insurer rather than assuming a "comprehensive" label covers it. Staying beyond 90 days requires applying for a temporary residence permit through Ukraine's State Migration Service, a process that runs on wartime-adjusted rules and processing capacity that has shifted more than once since 2022 — verify the current procedure and required documents directly with the migration service or a Ukraine-based immigration lawyer rather than relying on a pre-2022 description of how it used to work.

## Martial law affects movement, mostly for Ukrainian men

Martial law restrictions on leaving Ukraine have historically applied to Ukrainian men of a broad military-age range, not to foreign nationals — but the specific age band has changed over the course of the war, with a 2026 easing allowing men aged 18–22 to travel abroad more freely than the wider group. This detail is genuinely fluid and matters most if you are a dual national, married to a Ukrainian citizen, or planning a family relocation involving Ukrainian men of any age — confirm the current rules with the State Border Guard Service or a Ukrainian immigration lawyer before finalizing any plan involving a Ukrainian citizen's travel, since getting this wrong can mean someone in your household cannot leave when you expect them to be able to.

## Rent and banking limits shape day-to-day life

A one-bedroom apartment in central Lviv commonly rents for **UAH 15,000–25,000 a month**, translating to a genuinely low **USD 600–1,000** all-in monthly budget by European standards — one of the clearest financial arguments for the city among people already committed to the move for other reasons. Banking has its own wartime rules: Ukrainian residents currently face a cap of **100,000 hryvnia a month** on transfers abroad to another person's bank card, and cash leaving the country is capped around **€10,000** without additional declarations. These limits apply to residents moving money out of Ukraine rather than to foreigners bringing money in, but they shape how you'd manage finances once genuinely settled — a foreign-currency account held outside Ukraine, accessed via international card, is the more practical setup for day-to-day spending than relying on transfers through a Ukrainian bank.
