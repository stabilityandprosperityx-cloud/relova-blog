**How to move to Malaysia in 2026** comes down to picking the right lane among a genuinely broad set of legal pathways, then matching your income and paperwork to whichever one actually fits. The headline program is Malaysia My Second Home (MM2H), which has been restructured more than once in recent years and remains the subject of ongoing relaunch discussion. As of 2026, planning-stage figures circulating for a revived or adjusted MM2H tier cite offshore income requirements around RM 40,000 a month alongside a fixed deposit near RM 1 million — a meaningfully higher bar than the original 2000s-era MM2H, which was famous for being one of the most accessible long-stay visas in Southeast Asia. Given how often MM2H's tiers, deposit rules, and income floors have shifted, anyone serious about this route should check the current requirements directly on the official MM2H portal the week they apply rather than trusting older blog posts or forum threads, including this one.

For remote workers who don't want to clear MM2H's higher bar, DE Rantau — Malaysia's dedicated digital nomad pass — is usually the more realistic entry point. It's commonly referenced with an income requirement near RM 24,000 a year (roughly USD 5,000, though check current FX-adjusted guidance) and an application fee around RM 1,060. DE Rantau is explicitly built for location-independent professionals working for employers or clients outside Malaysia, and it's renewable, making it a workable multi-year base for people who aren't ready to commit to MM2H's deposit requirements.

On cost of living, Kuala Lumpur remains the default landing point for most newcomers, and one-bedroom apartments in well-connected areas typically run RM 1,800–3,000 a month, with plenty of variation depending on proximity to the LRT/MRT network and whether the building includes a pool or gym — amenity-rich condos in KL are often cheaper than equivalent rentals in Western cities, which surprises a lot of first-time arrivals. Penang, the country's second major expat hub and a genuine food and heritage destination in its own right, runs noticeably cheaper at roughly RM 1,200–2,000 for a comparable one-bedroom, while offering a slower, more walkable old-town lifestyle in George Town alongside modern condo options on the island's newer side.

Healthcare is one of Malaysia's stronger selling points for long-stay expats: private hospitals in KL and Penang are frequently cited by relocation communities as offering strong quality at a fraction of US or even Western European private-care costs, and MM2H applicants have historically been required to carry adequate health insurance as part of the program — a requirement worth confirming under whichever current tier you're applying through. Banking, by contrast, has gotten more cautious in recent years across Southeast Asia generally, and non-resident account opening in Malaysia often requires an in-person visit, proof of local address, and sometimes a letter from your visa sponsor, so it's worth budgeting real time for this step rather than assuming it happens same-day.

A sensible approach for anyone serious about the move is a staged 90-day plan: spend the first month confirming which visa category actually fits your income profile and gathering apostilled documents, the second month on a scouting trip to test KL or Penang neighborhoods and open a local bank account, and the third month finalizing housing and the visa application itself once your paperwork is airtight. Building in a 15–25% budget contingency across that whole process — for unexpected fees, delayed appointments, or a longer-than-planned hotel stay while you house-hunt — tends to be the difference between a smooth relocation and a stressful one.
