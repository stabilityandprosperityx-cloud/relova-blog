The Maldives runs on a structure most relocation guides gloss over: it's a tourism-dependent economy spread across roughly 1,200 coral islands where foreigners cannot own land outright, work is legally employer-sponsored rather than freelance-friendly, and daily life outside the resort bubble looks nothing like the honeymoon marketing.

## There is no independent work visa

Unlike countries with points-based or freelancer-friendly permits, working legally in the Maldives requires an actual local employer. That employer applies for an Employment Approval (EA) through the Permits and Foreign Employment Division of Maldives Immigration before you arrive, and once you're in the country you're required to obtain your physical work permit within 15 days of arrival. The tourist visa — a free 30-day entry granted to essentially all nationalities on arrival — explicitly does not authorize work; overstaying its terms or working on it is an immigration violation, not a gray area. In practice, the realistic paths in are tourism and hospitality (resort staff, dive instructors, seaplane and marine operations), teaching, and a smaller pool of professional roles tied to specific employers; there is no meaningful remote-work or digital-nomad visa track as of 2026, so freelancers hoping to "just work from a Maldivian island" on tourist status are operating outside the rules.

## Land and property: leasehold, not freehold

The Maldivian Constitution prohibits foreigners from owning freehold land — this is a hard legal line, not a soft custom. What foreign investors can access is long-term leasehold under the Land Act (2002), typically structured as an initial term with renewal options that can extend the effective control period to roughly 50 years, or ownership via a locally incorporated company with Maldivian majority shareholding. Foreign nationals who invest above a set threshold (commonly cited around USD 100,000 in property or business investment) may qualify for a residence permit valid for a limited multi-year term, but this is an investor-residency track, not a path to full property ownership.

## Money: income tax and the cost of living in Malé

The Maldives is often summarized as a "zero income tax" country, and that's true for the historical baseline, but the country introduced a formal personal income tax on higher earners in recent years, administered by the Maldives Inland Revenue Authority — so "zero percent" should not be assumed as a blanket rule without checking your specific income level and residency status against current MIRA thresholds. Regardless of tax treatment, expect friction opening a local bank account without proof of residency or an employer letter; this is one of the more commonly cited early frustrations among new arrivals. Malé, the dense capital packed onto a tiny footprint, has a real (non-resort) rental market, with one-bedroom apartments commonly running USD 400-700 a month — modest by resort-marketing standards but a genuine cost relative to local wages.

## Daily life beyond the resort islands

Alcohol sale and consumption is restricted to licensed resort islands and a handful of designated establishments; it is not generally available on "local" inhabited islands where most residents and non-resort workers actually live, a fact that surprises people who only know the Maldives from luxury travel content. Many expats based on local islands plan periodic trips to Sri Lanka or elsewhere in the region for variety and access to goods and services not readily available locally — worth budgeting for as a recurring cost, not a one-off.

## Healthcare and getting between islands

Healthcare quality varies enormously across the archipelago: Malé has the country's main referral hospitals and reasonable private clinic options, but many inhabited and resort islands have only basic medical facilities, and anything serious typically means an emergency seaplane or speedboat transfer to Malé, or an evacuation abroad for the most severe cases. Private international health insurance with medical evacuation coverage is close to essential for anyone based outside the capital, and many employers in the resort sector build it into their staff packages given the logistical reality of island-based living. Inter-island transport itself is a genuine planning consideration — domestic flights and speedboat transfers between atolls can be expensive and infrequent, so budget both time and money if your role or lifestyle involves regular movement between islands rather than staying put on one.

## Climate and the practical rhythm of island life

The Maldives sits close to the equator with a consistently warm, humid climate year-round, split loosely into a drier northeast monsoon season (roughly December to April) and a wetter southwest monsoon (May to November) that brings more frequent rain and rougher seas — worth factoring into any inter-island travel plans or outdoor-dependent work. Daily life on local (non-resort) islands runs at a noticeably slower pace than the resort marketing suggests, with limited shopping options, conservative dress norms in public areas, and a tight-knit community structure that takes some adjustment for arrivals used to larger, more anonymous cities.
