Malta's **Nomad Residence Permit** is restricted to non-EU, non-EEA, and non-Swiss nationals working remotely for a foreign employer or client base — EU citizens don't need it at all, since they already have freedom-of-movement rights. The income test sits at **€42,000 gross per year**, roughly **€3,500 a month**, and critically it has to come from *active* remote work — employment, freelancing, or ownership of a foreign company — because passive income like savings interest or investment returns doesn't satisfy the requirement. The permit itself carries a **€300 application fee per applicant plus €100 for the residence card**, runs for an initial **one year, renewable up to three additional times for a four-year maximum**, and typical processing lands around **30 working days** from when Residency Malta confirms your proof of funds. Tax treatment is the part worth reading twice: the first **12 months are 0% tax** on the qualifying remote income, after which a **flat 10% rate** applies going forward under Malta's dedicated Nomad Residence Permits (Income Tax) Rules — a meaningfully better deal than most EU jurisdictions offer remote workers, but one that only kicks in after that first tax-free year ends.

## Global Residence Programme: a different door for a different buyer

The Nomad permit isn't Malta's only relevant route — the **Global Residence Programme (GRP)** targets non-EU nationals who want Maltese tax residency without working locally, typically retirees or those living on foreign investment income. GRP applies a **flat 15% tax rate** on foreign income remitted to Malta, subject to a **minimum annual tax** (commonly cited around €15,000, though the exact figure and qualifying conditions are worth confirming with a Maltese tax advisor since GRP rules carry property and income thresholds that shift the calculus meaningfully case by case). GRP and the Nomad permit solve different problems — GRP for people relocating capital and lifestyle, the Nomad permit for people relocating an active remote income stream — and conflating the two is the most common mistake in casual research on Malta relocation.

## Sliema versus Gozo: the real rent spread

A one-bedroom in **Sliema**, Malta's most expat-dense seafront town and the default landing spot for English-speaking newcomers, commonly runs **€1,000–1,600 a month** — reflecting genuine demand from both the Nomad Residence Permit crowd and Malta's long-established iGaming and financial-services expat population. **Gozo**, the smaller sister island a short ferry ride away, drops to roughly **€600–1,000** for comparable space, trading Sliema's restaurant density and nightlife for a slower, more rural pace and a ferry commute if your work ever requires being on the mainland. Malta's small size — the whole archipelago is smaller than many single cities — means the Sliema-to-Gozo distance is more about lifestyle than logistics; day trips between the two are entirely normal.

## English as the quiet advantage

Malta's status as an English-speaking EU member state (one of only two, alongside Ireland, post-Brexit) is easy to undersell but genuinely simplifies everything from banking paperwork to school enrollment to simply reading your own residence permit conditions without a translator — a structural advantage that partly explains why Malta punches above its size in nomad-visa popularity despite a housing market that isn't actually cheap by Mediterranean standards.

## Schengen, EU access, and what the permit doesn't grant

Malta has been a full Schengen and EU member since 2004 and 2007 respectively, which means a Maltese Nomad Residence Permit — while not itself an EU work authorization — does grant Schengen-area travel rights that make weekend trips to Sicily, Rome, or anywhere else in the zone genuinely frictionless. What it doesn't grant is the right to work for a Maltese employer or set up a Malta-facing local business; the permit is scoped strictly to remote work for entities outside Malta, and crossing that line without switching to an appropriate work-authorization category risks the permit itself.

Malta in 2026 is a **€42,000-income, 0%-then-10%-tax, €1,000–1,600-for-Sliema** proposition for remote workers, with GRP running a parallel and distinct 15%-flat-tax track for non-working residents. Confirm which door actually fits your income type before you file either application.
