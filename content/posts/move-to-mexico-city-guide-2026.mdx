## Adjusting to CDMX's High Altitude

**Move to Mexico City in 2026** and the first thing that actually hits you isn't the paperwork — it's the altitude. CDMX sits around 2,240 meters (roughly 7,350 feet) above sea level, and most newcomers underestimate what that means physically: shortness of breath climbing stairs, faster dehydration, a stronger hangover from the same drink, and for some people genuine altitude-adjustment symptoms in the first few days. It typically resolves within a week or two as your body acclimates, but it's worth building a few easy days into your arrival schedule rather than diving straight into apartment-hunting marathons and back-to-back social plans.

## The Temporary Resident Visa Route

Legally, most long-stay foreigners in CDMX use Mexico's Temporary Resident Visa, applied for at a Mexican consulate in your home country before arrival — Mexico doesn't offer a dedicated digital nomad visa, so remote workers typically qualify either by showing consistent monthly income (commonly benchmarked in the range of several thousand dollars over recent months, with the exact UMA-linked figure adjusted periodically, so check current consulate guidance) or by showing sufficient savings. The temporary resident card is issued for one year and renewable up to four years total before converting to permanent residency, which is a meaningfully longer runway than most comparable Latin American programs offer.

## Roma Norte, Condesa, and Polanco Rents

Housing in CDMX varies enormously by neighborhood, and this is where the city rewards research over guesswork. Roma Norte, the walkable, café-dense, gallery-filled district that's become the default expat magnet, typically runs MXN 15,000 to 25,000 a month for a one-bedroom — a wide range that reflects everything from older walk-ups to fully renovated units with amenities. Condesa, its slightly more polished, tree-lined neighbor built around Parque México, tends to run a notch higher, roughly MXN 18,000 to 30,000, trading a bit of Roma's grit for more manicured streets and a marginally older, quieter crowd. Polanco, the city's most upscale district — think wide avenues, luxury retail, and proximity to major corporate offices — commands the highest rents of the three and suits people prioritizing polish and security over walkable bohemian character. All three neighborhoods have driven visible gentrification and rent increases over the past several years as remote-worker demand has grown, which has become a genuine point of local tension worth being aware of and sensitive to as a newcomer.

## Getting Around and Healthcare Costs

Day-to-day logistics are where CDMX quietly excels. The Metro is extraordinarily cheap — rides run around MXN 5 regardless of distance — and covers most of the city, though it gets seriously crowded at rush hour and safety awareness (bag security, standard urban caution) matters more there than in Roma or Condesa's daytime streets. Ride-hailing apps are widely used and generally considered safer than hailing a taxi off the street. Healthcare is a genuine strength: private hospitals in flagship networks (ABC Medical Center and similar) offer quality care that draws medical tourists from the US, at a fraction of American private-insurance costs, and many expats opt for private insurance or even self-pay for routine care given how favorable the pricing is.

## Air Quality, Traffic, and Realistic Budgeting

Air quality and traffic are the two most common complaints from longer-term residents — CDMX sits in a valley that traps pollution on bad days, and driving anywhere during peak hours can turn a 15-minute trip into an hour, which is part of why so many expats deliberately choose to live within walking distance of work and social life rather than commuting across the city. Between rent, food, transport, and a comfortable social budget, a reasonable planning range for a single remote worker in one of the popular central neighborhoods runs meaningfully above what generic "Mexico" cost-of-living averages suggest, so it's worth budgeting from CDMX-specific numbers rather than national ones, with a 15–25% buffer for the inevitable unexpected costs of a first few months.
