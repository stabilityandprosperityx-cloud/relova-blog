Montenegro has quietly become one of the more accessible digital nomad destinations in Europe, partly because its income bar is set relative to local wages rather than a flat figure pitched at wealthy remote workers, and partly because it's outside the EU and Schengen, which means different day-count rules than the countries around it.

## The income requirement is tied to the minimum wage, not a fixed number

Montenegro's [digital nomad visa](/blog/kotor-vs-budva-montenegro-guide-2026) sets its income floor at three times the national minimum wage, which is why the exact euro figure moves as the minimum wage is adjusted. As of the current program, that works out to roughly €1,350 per month in verifiable remote income — applicants should confirm the exact current figure against Montenegro's Ministry of Interior guidance before applying, since it will shift again if the minimum wage is revised. The visa is issued for an initial two years and can be renewed once for another two years, giving a maximum stay of four years under the program, after which a mandatory cooling-off period applies before reapplying. It's also worth noting the program has a stated end date in current guidance (currently modeled around the end of 2026), so timing an application with enough runway for processing matters.

## Application costs are modest relative to the paperwork involved

The application fee itself is relatively low, commonly cited around the tens of euros, but total setup costs run considerably higher once you factor in document translation and notarization, apostilles for foreign-issued documents, and mandatory health insurance — realistic all-in setup costs are often quoted in the €800–2,000 range depending on how much paperwork needs professional translation. Processing through Montenegro's administrative system commonly takes 40–60 days, so applying well ahead of an intended move date is important.

## Income tax sits in a genuinely competitive band

Montenegro's personal income tax structure has moved toward a progressive model in recent years, with rates commonly discussed in the roughly 9–15% range depending on income level — meaningfully lower than most Western European countries, which is part of the country's appeal for remote workers and retirees alike. [Digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-kotor-montenegro-guide-2026) holders should clarify with a local advisor whether their specific income is taxed in Montenegro at all versus in their home country, since tax residency and visa status are governed by separate rules, and the [digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-serbia-guide-2026) does not automatically make someone a Montenegrin tax resident.

## Cost of living: Podgorica is inexpensive, the coast is not

Podgorica, the capital, remains one of the cheaper capital cities in Europe, with one-bedroom rents commonly in the €300–500 range. The Adriatic coast — Budva and the Bay of Kotor in particular — runs considerably higher due to tourism demand and limited housing stock in historic areas, with one-bedroom rents often €500–900 and spiking further during peak summer season. Many remote workers split the difference by basing in Podgorica or a smaller coastal town outside the main tourist centers.

## Citizenship is a long game, not a quick add-on

Montenegro's standard naturalization path for long-term residents runs around a decade of continuous legal residence before citizenship eligibility, considerably longer than several neighboring countries' naturalization timelines. For most digital nomad visa holders, this is a non-factor given the program's own four-year maximum duration — anyone planning for a longer-term future in Montenegro needs to look at separate residence permit categories that offer a continuous path toward that naturalization clock.

## Why non-EU and non-Schengen status changes the travel math

Montenegro sits outside both the EU and the Schengen Area, which cuts two ways for movers. On one hand, it means the Schengen 90/180-day clock doesn't apply to time spent in Montenegro, making it a genuinely useful base for people trying to stretch time in Europe beyond what Schengen alone allows. On the other hand, it means Montenegro's own entry rules, visa reciprocity, and residence permit categories operate independently of EU frameworks, so assumptions that carry over from EU relocation guides (freedom of movement, EU-wide permit recognition) simply don't apply here. Montenegro has been an EU accession candidate for years and periodically signals progress toward membership, but residents should plan around current rules rather than anticipated EU integration timelines, since accession processes in the region have historically run far longer than initial projections.
