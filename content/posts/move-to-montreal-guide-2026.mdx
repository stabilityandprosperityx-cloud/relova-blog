Montreal is a **French-speaking** city in **Quebec**. Government offices, most schools, and a large share of everyday bureaucracy run in French. English exists — especially downtown and in tech — but families with children should budget **language time**, not a weekend app. Canada has **no digital-nomad visa**. Staying long-term means an immigration status (skilled worker, provincial selection) or, for some nationalities aged **18–30/35**, a **working-holiday** visa.

The rest of this guide is the Quebec-specific path (**PSTQ**), why the monthly math still beats Toronto and Vancouver, and what daily life actually feels like.

## French, and the immigration door that is not “just Canada”

Quebec selects its own skilled workers through the **Programme de sélection des travailleurs qualifiés (PSTQ)**. French ability is not a nice-to-have on this track; it is a **core filter**. Federal Express Entry logic from other provinces does not carry over unchanged. If your plan is “I’ll land and learn French later,” you are describing a tourist stay, not a PSTQ file.

Work the language in parallel with the paperwork: classes, a placement test strategy, and honest hours per week. Children in the public system will be in French. That is a feature for long-term integration and a shock if you expected an English suburb with maple syrup.

Working-holiday holders should still treat French as the difference between a hospitality job and a trapped-in-the-expat-bar year. Remote workers on visitor time need to watch the clock: Canada will not convert “I have a laptop” into residence.

## Why the monthly numbers still beat Toronto and Vancouver

A newcomer single in Montreal often lands around **$2,500–3,200 a month**, with rent **$1,200–1,800**. Toronto comparable all-in is more like **$3,500–4,500**. Vancouver sits in the same painful band as Toronto. That gap is the reason people still choose Montreal after they hear about French forms.

Childcare is the second spreadsheet. A **regulated Quebec daycare** is about **$9.65 a day**. Other provinces are usually higher; the federal political target of **$10 a day** is not yet a lived reality everywhere. A family with **two children under five** in Montreal can be **$450–900 a month** ahead of Toronto or Vancouver on care alone. That is not a lifestyle tweet. It is a lease-versus-daycare trade.

IRCC still wants to see settlement funds in the region of **$13,000 CAD** for a single and **$17,000 CAD** for a couple as a planning floor for many economic programs. Those figures are **not** a first-year budget. They are the “do not arrive empty” test. First-year real spend runs higher once you add deposits, winter kit, and the month you overlap housing.

## Culture and the shape of a week

Montreal earns the lazy nickname “Paris of North America” because festivals, music, and food are dense relative to rent. Concerts and dinners cost less than the equivalent night in Toronto. Winter is still winter: budget coats, and do not romanticise January courtyards.

Daily life is bilingual in pockets and French at the clerk’s window. Learn enough to handle RAMQ queues, school emails, and the dépanneur without performing helplessness. Join one recurring activity in French even if your job is English; otherwise you will recreate an anglophone island and then complain the city is “cliquey.”

Housing hunt in French even if you negotiate in English: listings, lease vocabulary, and the Régie rules are easier when you can read them. Budget a professional translation of your lease if your French is not yet contract-grade. Winter is the other hidden cost — transit still works, but you will spend more on clothing and indoor leisure than a July visit suggested. Put that in the $2,500–3,200 envelope rather than treating the first summer as the real city.

Montreal in 2026 is cheaper than Canada’s two most expensive metros, with a **French-gated** skilled path and **no nomad visa**. If you will do the language work, the $2,500–3,200 single band and the $9.65 daycare are the practical reasons to pick it. If you will not, look at Toronto’s English bureaucracy and pay the rent.
