Morocco sits nine kilometers from Spain at its closest point, which makes it one of the few places you can build a genuinely Europe-adjacent life at a fraction of European rent — but the residency process runs through French-language bureaucracy that rewards patience and paperwork discipline more than any online form.

## The Carte de Séjour: how residency actually works

Most long-stay foreigners in Morocco operate on a **Carte de Séjour** (residence card), applied for at the local prefecture or wilaya after arriving on a standard 90-day tourist entry. The card is typically issued for one year initially and renewed annually — renewal requires a signed and registered lease (*contrat de bail*), proof of stable income or savings (bank statements are standard), and a certificate of no criminal record, both from your home country and, after your first year, from Morocco itself. There is no fixed published minimum income threshold the way Portugal or Spain publish one; adjudication is more discretionary at the prefecture level, so working with a local *fiduciaire* (accountant) or immigration facilitator to assemble a complete file the first time is worth the fee — refiling after a rejection adds months.

## Marrakech vs. Casablanca: cost and character

**Marrakech** remains the default landing city for lifestyle-driven expats: the Medina and Gueliz districts offer riads and modern apartments alike, with a one-bedroom typically running MAD 4,000–7,000/month (roughly USD 400–700 at current rates), and a large enough resident foreign community that English gets you further than official policy would suggest. **Casablanca**, Morocco's commercial capital, is the pick for anyone working locally or building a business — it has the country's main stock exchange, most multinational regional headquarters, and better-paying local job opportunities, but rent runs higher, MAD 5,000–9,000/month for a comparable one-bedroom, reflecting its status as a working city rather than a lifestyle destination.

## Money, banking, and the dirham

The Moroccan dirham (MAD) is not fully convertible, which matters more than most newcomers expect: moving money in is straightforward, but repatriating large sums back out requires documentation trails, and foreign remittances that are properly declared and channeled through Morocco's official banking system (rather than cash) generally receive more favorable tax and exchange treatment than undocumented transfers. Opening a local bank account (Attijariwafa Bank and BMCE are common choices for foreigners) is worth doing in your first weeks, both for rent payments — landlords increasingly prefer bank transfer over cash — and to establish the transaction history that later Carte de Séjour renewals and any property purchase will ask for.

## Language and daily administration

French remains the practical working language of Moroccan administration, courts, and much of business, alongside Modern Standard Arabic and Darija (Moroccan Arabic) for daily life — English fluency is common among the young and in tourism-facing roles but doesn't reliably carry you through a prefecture appointment. Investing in even conversational French before or during your first year measurably speeds up everything from utility hookups to residency renewals, since French-speaking foreigners are visibly processed faster at government counters than those relying on translation apps.

## What the ferry link to Europe actually changes

Morocco's geography is one of its most underrated practical advantages: a ferry from Tangier Med to Algeciras or Tarifa in southern Spain takes roughly an hour, and combined with low-cost European carriers serving Marrakech, Casablanca, and Fes directly, it means Schengen visits, family trips home, or a quick medical or shopping run to Europe are all realistic weekend options rather than major expeditions. For anyone whose family, business, or healthcare provider is still in Europe, that proximity meaningfully changes the calculation compared with a relocation to a similarly priced destination in, say, Southeast Asia or Latin America, where the same trip means a full day of flights each way. It's also why a growing number of Morocco-based remote workers structure their year around a Schengen-adjacent rhythm — long stretches in Marrakech or Casablanca punctuated by short, easy hops to Europe that don't eat into the 90/180-day Schengen allowance the way a full relocation there would.
