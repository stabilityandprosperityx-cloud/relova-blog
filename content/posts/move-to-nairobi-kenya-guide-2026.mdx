Kenya's digital nomad route, officially the **Class N permit**, is real and processed through the eFNS immigration portal — but published figures for its income and fee requirements disagree with each other badly enough in 2026 that quoting a single number here would be actively misleading. Some current guides cite a flat **$1,000/month** income proof requirement with roughly **$200-1,200** in combined processing and issuance fees; others cite an annual income bar as high as **$55,000** and total costs near **$1,500**. That's not a rounding difference, it's two different programs' worth of numbers circulating under the same name — treat every figure you read about Class N, including the ranges above, as a starting point to verify directly against Kenya's Directorate of Immigration Services before you file, not as settled fact.

## What is consistent across sources

Despite the fee and income confusion, a few things hold steady: the permit is issued for one or two years and is renewable, applicants must show income from a foreign-registered employer or foreign clients (Kenyan-sourced income doesn't qualify), and proof typically means three months of bank statements or payslips rather than a single snapshot figure. The application runs through Kenya's eFNS online portal rather than a paper process, which is a genuine improvement over how this used to work — but portal requirements and fee schedules have shifted enough in the last two years that a screenshot from last year's application is not reliable evidence of this year's rules.

## Westlands, Kilimani, and the rent math

Westlands one-bedrooms commonly list for **KES 60,000-100,000** a month (roughly **$465-775** at typical exchange rates), and it's Nairobi's default expat and tech-worker anchor — walkable-ish, well served by restaurants and coworking spaces, and the neighborhood most relocation agents will point you toward first. Kilimani runs slightly cheaper, commonly **KES 55,000-90,000**, with a younger, cafe-and-apartment-block feel and a bit more variability block to block in terms of security and building quality. Lavington sits at the premium end, often **KES 70,000-120,000**, and is the default choice for families prioritizing space, quieter streets, and proximity to international schools over walkability to nightlife.

## Cashless by default, but keep notes on hand

M-Pesa's mobile-money network has over 50 million active users in Kenya and functions as the default payment rail for everything from rent-adjacent transactions to market stalls to splitting a bill at a restaurant — setting up an M-Pesa line in your first week is not optional if you want to function normally, not a nice-to-have. That said, keep small-denomination shilling notes on hand regardless: security guards, some market vendors, and informal transactions still run on cash, and showing up cashless to a situation that expects a tip or a small fee is a recurring friction point new arrivals report.

## Safety and neighborhood-level planning, not blanket avoidance

Nairobi's expat-heavy neighborhoods generally rely on compound-style housing with gates and private security rather than open-street living, and that's the norm rather than a red flag — most mid-to-upscale apartment blocks in Westlands, Kilimani, and Lavington are built around this model. Vet the specific building and street rather than the neighborhood as a whole; security quality varies block to block even within a single "safe" area, and a local relocation agent or an existing expat contact is worth more here than any generic city-level safety rating.
