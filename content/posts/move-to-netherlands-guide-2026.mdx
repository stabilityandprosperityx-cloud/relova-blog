**How to move to the Netherlands in 2026** depends heavily on which door you're walking through, because the Dutch system offers genuinely different pathways depending on your nationality, profession, and whether you're employed or building something of your own. For skilled workers with a Dutch job offer, the Highly Skilled Migrant (HSM) scheme is the standard route, and it runs on salary thresholds set annually by the IND (Dutch immigration service). 2026 planning tables commonly cite roughly €6,245 a month for applicants 30 and over, and about €5,688 a month for those under 30 — these thresholds are indexed and republished by the IND each year, so anyone building a job search or offer negotiation around them should pull the current figures directly from the IND rather than relying on last year's numbers, since even small misses can sink sponsorship.

Americans specifically have an unusual advantage here: the Dutch-American Friendship Treaty (DAFT) gives US citizens a self-employment route that sidesteps the HSM salary requirement entirely, requiring instead a relatively modest capital investment in a Dutch business (commonly cited around €4,500, though this should be confirmed with current guidance) and a viable business plan. It's one of the more accessible self-employment visas in Europe precisely because it exists as a bilateral treaty rather than a general EU scheme, and it's become a well-worn path for American freelancers and small business owners who want Dutch residency without needing a sponsoring employer. Recent graduates of Dutch or other qualifying universities have their own option too: the orientation year (zoekjaar) permit, which grants a year of unrestricted work rights to search for employment or start a business, without the HSM salary floor applying during that window — genuinely useful for anyone finishing a Dutch master's degree who isn't ready to commit to a specific employer yet.

Tax planning matters enormously here, mostly because of the 30% ruling — a expat tax break that shields a portion of salary from Dutch income tax for qualifying skilled migrants. It has been restructured to phase down over time: commonly described as 30% tax-free for the first 20 months, dropping to 20% for the next 20 months, then 10% for a final 20 months, rather than the flat multi-year benefit it used to be. Eligibility timing, the exact phase-down schedule, and how it interacts with your specific salary and start date are genuinely worth a paid consultation with a Dutch tax advisor before you finalize a job offer or plan your finances around the assumption of a full flat-rate benefit — the phased structure changes the math meaningfully for anyone staying multiple years.

Housing is where a lot of the Netherlands' appeal collides with reality. Amsterdam one-bedroom rentals typically run €1,800–2,500 a month, reflecting a housing market that's been under sustained pressure for years, with limited new construction relative to demand and a regulatory environment that makes it hard to add supply quickly. Rotterdam, the country's less glamorous but genuinely livable second city, offers meaningfully better value at roughly €1,300–1,800 for a comparable one-bedroom, alongside a more modern skyline (having been largely rebuilt after WWII bombing), a strong food and culture scene of its own, and an easy 40-minute train connection to Amsterdam for anyone who wants the job market without the capital's rent.
