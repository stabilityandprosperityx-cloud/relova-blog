**Move to Nice, France in 2026** and you're buying into the Côte d'Azur lifestyle at a genuine discount to its most famous neighbor, Monaco, while still paying meaningfully more than most of France outside [Paris](/blog/paris-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026). Nice is expensive by French provincial standards — its Mediterranean coastline, direct rail and air links, and status as the unofficial capital of the Riviera all bake a premium into rents and daily costs that towns further inland don't carry. But set against Paris, or against Monaco itself, Nice looks like the practical choice: real Mediterranean weather, genuine beach access, and a walkable historic center, at a fraction of Monegasque or Parisian housing costs.

For most non-EU citizens planning a stay beyond 90 days, the standard route is a French long-stay visa (visa de long séjour), with an official filing fee typically around EUR 99 — modest compared to the overall cost of relocating, but worth budgeting alongside translation and apostille costs for supporting documents, since French consular processing is document-heavy and inconsistent turnaround times between consulates are a common source of delay. Once in France, the visa functions as a residence permit for the first year, after which most people transition to a carte de séjour renewal process through their local prefecture — a step that's more bureaucratically involved than the initial visa application and worth researching well ahead of your first renewal deadline.

Housing varies significantly by which part of Nice you choose. Cimiez, the hillside neighborhood known for its Roman ruins, the Matisse Museum, and a distinctly upscale, quiet residential character, runs roughly EUR 950–1,400 for a one-bedroom — you're paying for green space, architecture, and distance from the tourist crush of the Promenade des Anglais. Libération, a more central, workaday neighborhood built around a genuinely excellent daily market, offers stronger value at roughly EUR 800–1,200, trading some of Cimiez's polish for a livelier, more local feel and easier access to the train station and central amenities.

The detail that makes Nice genuinely distinctive among French cities, though, is its proximity to Monaco: a roughly 20-minute train ride puts you in one of the world's wealthiest micro-states, and this has created a real cross-border work dynamic — some Nice residents commute into Monaco for finance, hospitality, or luxury-sector jobs that pay Monegasque wages while living at Nice's comparatively lower cost of living, a genuinely favorable arbitrage that doesn't exist in most of France. It's worth researching Monaco's own work-authorization rules separately if this is part of your plan, since working in Monaco while resident in France carries its own tax and social-security considerations distinct from ordinary French employment.

Climate is the other headline draw: Nice averages around 300 sunny days a year, a genuinely rare figure for France and one of the primary reasons the Riviera became a resort destination for European aristocracy in the first place, and remains one for remote workers today. Combined with easy TGV rail access to the rest of France and a well-served international airport (Nice Côte d'Azur, France's third-busiest), Nice offers a rare combination for this price point: genuine Mediterranean lifestyle without total isolation from the rest of the country or Europe.

Day-to-day life leans heavily on the outdoors: the Promenade des Anglais offers miles of beachfront walking and cycling, the old town (Vieux Nice) is dense with markets, cafés, and Italian-influenced Niçoise cuisine that reflects the region's historical ties to Italy as much as to France, and the surrounding hills make weekend hikes and day trips into the Alps genuinely accessible without a long drive. French bureaucracy still applies in full here, though — healthcare registration, tax residency declarations, and the eventual carte de séjour renewal all follow the same national rules as anywhere else in France, so Nice's Riviera lifestyle shouldn't be mistaken for lighter administrative requirements than the rest of the country.
