**Moving to Norway** outside the Nordic/EEA free-movement zone runs through UDI, the Norwegian Directorate of Immigration, and the permit you qualify for depends almost entirely on whether you have a Norwegian employer lined up or plan to work for yourself.

## The skilled worker permit needs a job offer that clears a salary floor

Norway's skilled worker permit requires a concrete job offer from a Norwegian employer, relevant qualifications (typically a completed degree or vocational certification matching the role), and a salary that meets UDI's periodically adjusted minimum threshold — commonly cited around NOK 312,000 a year (roughly EUR 27,000) as the regulatory floor, though this figure is reviewed and can shift, so it is worth confirming the current number directly against UDI before relying on it in an application. That floor sits well below Norway's average salary, commonly referenced around NOK 620,000 a year, which underlines that the permit threshold is a legal minimum for eligibility, not a realistic target salary — most successful applicants clear it by a wide margin because Norwegian employers sponsoring foreign hires are generally filling genuinely skilled roles that pay accordingly.

## Self-employment is a separate, harder route with no fixed salary test

Unlike the skilled worker permit, Norway's self-employment residence permit does not hinge on a single salary number — instead it requires a detailed business plan, evidence of sufficient funds to support both the business and your own living costs during the [startup](/blog/move-to-finland-guide-2026) period, and often registration steps with Norwegian business authorities before or alongside the immigration application. UDI evaluates the business plan's viability directly, which makes this route more subjective and generally slower and more document-heavy than the employer-sponsored skilled worker path — freelancers and remote business owners considering Norway should budget more lead time and expect more back-and-forth with caseworkers than a straightforward job-offer-based application.

## Oslo rent absorbs the biggest chunk of a newcomer's budget

A one-bedroom apartment in central Oslo commonly runs NOK 15,000–22,000 a month, among the higher end for Nordic capitals, and that figure typically does not include utilities or the internet/electricity costs that can add several hundred NOK more in winter months given Norway's heating demands. Outside central Oslo — Grünerløkka's outer edges, Sagene, or towns on the metro line like Lørenskog — rent drops meaningfully while staying within a reasonable commute, which is where most newcomers land once the initial city-center search proves expensive.

## Healthcare runs on a deductible ceiling that resets your costs mid-year

Norway's public healthcare system (Folketrygden) requires residents to pay a modest co-pay (egenandel) for GP visits, specialists, and prescriptions up to an annual ceiling, after which a frikort (exemption card) is issued automatically and further covered care for the rest of the calendar year becomes free. The exact ceiling amount is adjusted periodically, so check the current figure via Helsenorge or HELFO directly rather than budgeting off an old number — the mechanism matters more than the specific figure: expect real but capped out-of-pocket costs early in the year, dropping to near zero for covered services once the ceiling is hit.

## Citizenship is measured in years lived, not years of good intentions

Standard naturalization generally requires having lived in Norway for 8 of the last 11 years, a requirement extended from the previous 7-of-10 rule in 2022. Faster tracks exist for specific situations — a shorter residence requirement for those meeting a defined income threshold, an even shorter path for people married to or in a registered partnership with a Norwegian citizen, and a notably fast 2-year track for citizens of the other Nordic countries. Anyone planning around eventual Norwegian citizenship should map which track actually applies to their situation early, since the difference between the standard and fastest tracks is several years.
