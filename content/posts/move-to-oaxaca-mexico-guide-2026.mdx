**Move to Oaxaca, Mexico in 2026** and you're choosing one of the country's most culturally distinctive cities — a UNESCO World Heritage colonial center, the beating heart of Mexico's indigenous Zapotec and Mixtec traditions, and increasingly a magnet for digital nomads priced out of Mexico City or drawn by Oaxaca's food scene, which many consider the best in the country. It's also gotten notably more expensive at the visa counter recently: from January 2026, Mexican immigration fees roughly doubled, with a one-year temporary resident card now running about MXN $11,140 and permanent residence around MXN $13,579. Mexico still has no dedicated digital nomad visa — the country runs on two main tools instead. The FMM tourist card, available on arrival or applied for in advance, can run up to 180 days, but that maximum isn't guaranteed; the immigration officer at entry sets the actual stamped duration, so don't book housing or make plans assuming you'll automatically get the full six months. For anyone planning to stay longer or more securely, the Temporary Resident Visa — applied for at a Mexican consulate before arrival — is the real long-stay tool, valid initially for one year and renewable up to four years total before conversion to permanent residency.

Qualifying for Temporary Resident status runs through income or savings thresholds tied to Mexico's UMA (a government-indexed economic unit updated periodically, with the most recent adjustment reflected from mid-2025). Current guidance puts the income floor near $4,400 a month evidenced over the last six months, or alternatively roughly $72,000–75,000 in savings shown over the prior year — two distinct paths depending on whether you're demonstrating ongoing income or a lump sum, and worth discussing with the specific consulate handling your application since document requirements and acceptable proof formats vary somewhat by location.

Oaxaca remains genuinely one of Mexico's more affordable cities to live in, and that affordability is part of what's driving its growing digital-nomad popularity relative to pricier CDMX. Comfortable solo budgets — covering a decent apartment, eating out regularly, and normal social spending — typically run $1,600–2,400 a month, while people willing to shop local markets rather than expat-oriented grocery stores and lean into peso-priced daily life can live nearer $800–1,400. Furnished one- and two-bedroom apartments in the popular central areas run roughly 8,000–15,000 pesos a month (about $400–750), a fraction of equivalent Roma Norte or Condesa pricing in Mexico City — genuinely one of the biggest reasons people choose Oaxaca over the capital, alongside the different pace of life and closer connection to indigenous craft, mezcal production, and regional cuisine that Oaxaca is famous for. It's the same national Mexican visa system as Mexico City or anywhere else in the country — there's no separate Oaxaca-specific process — so the visa mechanics above apply identically regardless of which city you land in.

Safety is worth addressing directly, since it's often a concern that keeps people from considering Mexico at all: Oaxaca state's homicide rate runs around 8 per 100,000, meaningfully below Mexico's national average of roughly 26, making it one of the comparatively safer states in the country, though normal urban precautions still apply as they would in any city. Internet infrastructure is solid in the areas expats and nomads actually cluster in — fiber connections in Centro (the historic core) and Jalatlaco (a quieter, artsy neighborhood just northeast of downtown) generally handle video calls and remote work without issue, though it's worth testing any specific building's connection before signing a longer lease.

One planning detail that catches newcomers off guard: Oaxaca has two major cultural events that spike both prices and crowds significantly — the Guelaguetza festival in July, a massive celebration of the state's indigenous cultures with dance, music, and crafts drawing visitors from across Mexico and abroad, and Día de Muertos in late October, when Oaxaca becomes one of the most sought-after places in the country to experience the holiday's altars, parades, and cemetery vigils. Housing during both periods should be booked months ahead, since short-term rental prices climb sharply and availability tightens fast.
