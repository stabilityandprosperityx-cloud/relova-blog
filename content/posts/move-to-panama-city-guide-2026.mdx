## Latin America's Most American-Feeling Capital

**Move to Panama City in 2026** and you're landing in Latin America's most distinctly American-feeling capital — a skyline of glass towers that wouldn't look out of place in Miami, an economy built directly around the Panama Canal, and a currency that's literally the US dollar. This guide focuses on what it actually means to live in the city itself, day to day; for the national visa mechanics that apply whether you settle here or elsewhere in the country, see the companion [Move to Panama guide](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/move-to-panama-guide-2026), which covers the Friendly Nations Visa, Pensionado program, and territorial tax system in full.

## Casco Viejo's Colonial Charm and Rising Prices

What makes Panama City distinctive within Panama is the sheer range of neighborhood character packed into a relatively compact urban footprint. Casco Viejo, the restored colonial old town on a peninsula jutting into the bay, is where history and gentrification collide most visibly — narrow cobblestone streets, restored Spanish-colonial buildings now housing boutique hotels and rooftop bars, and a UNESCO World Heritage designation that's driven steady foreign investment and rising prices over the past decade. It's the neighborhood most visitors picture when they think of "old Panama," and it's increasingly popular with remote workers who want walkability and character over modern high-rise convenience.

## Punta Pacifica and Costa del Este for a Corporate Lifestyle

Punta Pacifica, by contrast, is Panama City's most polished modern district — the address of choice for banking and finance professionals, built around gleaming residential towers (including some of Latin America's tallest buildings), international-standard hospitals, and a genuinely cosmopolitan feel that draws comparisons to Miami's Brickell. Costa del Este, a planned business district further east, has emerged as another magnet for corporate professionals and families, with wide streets, newer construction, and proximity to Tocumen International Airport that appeals to people who travel frequently for work. Each of these areas commands a different price point and lifestyle, and it's worth spending real time in each — not just scrolling listings — before committing to a lease, since the character difference between Casco Viejo's historic charm and Costa del Este's suburban-corporate feel is genuinely stark for a single city.

## Living on the US Dollar

The US dollar being Panama's official currency (alongside the Panamanian balboa, which is pegged 1:1 and used mostly for coins) is a quietly enormous practical advantage for American expats specifically, and a meaningful convenience for anyone earning in dollars generally — there's no currency conversion friction, no exchange-rate risk eating into your purchasing power, and pricing that's directly comparable to US costs without mental math. This dollarization has existed since Panama's independence-era monetary arrangements and remains one of the most stable features of the country's economy.

## The Canal Economy's Pull on Jobs and Infrastructure

The Panama Canal's economic gravity shapes the local job market more than most newcomers expect. Beyond the canal authority itself, the logistics, shipping, banking, and insurance sectors that cluster around canal-adjacent trade form a genuine professional economy — Panama City has long positioned itself as a regional hub for companies doing business across Latin America, drawn partly by the territorial tax system (foreign-source income generally sits outside Panamanian taxation) and partly by the sheer logistics advantage of being adjacent to one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints. For remote workers this mostly matters indirectly — it's part of why the city has strong internet infrastructure, international schools, and a large expat professional community — but for anyone seeking local employment rather than remote work, the canal economy is genuinely the center of gravity to understand.

## Budget, Internet Speed, and School Costs

On cost of living, a comfortable couple's budget in Panama City typically runs $2,500–3,500 a month covering a good apartment, dining out, and normal expenses — noticeably higher than inland or mountain towns like Boquete, which run closer to $1,500–2,500 for a comparable lifestyle, reflecting the capital's status as Panama's most expensive market. Internet is a genuine strength: average national speeds were measured around 185 Mbps in early 2026, and Panama City itself typically outperforms that national average given its dense fiber infrastructure. Families relocating with children have real options too — international schools (offering IB or American curricula) run $10,000–20,000 a year, while bilingual private schools offer a more affordable middle ground at roughly $3,000–8,000 annually.
