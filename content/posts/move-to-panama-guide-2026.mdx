**How to move to Panama in 2026** starts with understanding that Panama runs one of the more genuinely accessible residency systems in Latin America, built around two headline programs that serve very different profiles of applicant. The Friendly Nations Visa is the workhorse route for most people: it's open to roughly 50 nationalities that Panama classifies as having close diplomatic, professional, or economic ties to the country — including the US, Canada, the UK, most of the EU, and a range of other nations — and it requires either an investment (commonly referenced around $200,000 in real estate or a qualifying economic tie) or a job contract with a Panamanian company, leading to two years of temporary residence before conversion toward permanent residency. Older guidance circulating online about a much smaller $5,000 deposit shortcut is outdated — that route was tightened following regulatory changes in 2021, and relying on it is a good way to build a plan around a pathway that no longer exists, so confirming the live investment or employment requirement on Panama's official immigration portal before committing money is essential.

The second major pathway, and arguably Panama's best-known program internationally, is the Pensionado visa — designed for retirees with a guaranteed lifetime pension. It commonly requires proof of at least $1,000 a month in pension income (with some additional amount per dependent), and in exchange it grants not just a straightforward path to permanent residency but access to Panama's Law 6 discount program, which gives Pensionado holders meaningful price reductions — commonly cited in the 15–50% range — on everything from healthcare and entertainment to certain transport and utility costs. It's a genuinely generous program by regional standards, part of why Panama has long been a fixture on "best places to retire" lists, though it's worth confirming the current exact income floor and dependent add-ons with official sources since these figures are periodically adjusted.

What ties both programs together, and what draws people to Panama generally regardless of which visa route they take, is the territorial tax system: Panama taxes income earned within its borders but generally leaves foreign-source income — pensions from abroad, dividends, remote salaries paid by companies outside Panama — outside the Panamanian tax net. For remote workers and retirees living on foreign income, this is often the single biggest financial argument for choosing Panama over a worldwide-taxation country, though the specifics depend heavily on your home country's own tax rules and any relevant tax treaty, so a conversation with a cross-border tax adviser is worth having before assuming a blanket exemption applies to your situation.

On cost of living, Panama City one-bedroom apartments commonly run $700–1,200 a month depending on neighborhood and building quality, with plenty of variation between older, more affordable areas and the glossier high-rise districts favored by finance professionals — for city-specific neighborhood detail, the dedicated [Panama City guide](/blog/move-to-panama-city-guide-2026) breaks down Casco Viejo, Punta Pacifica, and Costa del Este individually. Outside the capital, smaller towns and mountain communities like Boquete offer noticeably lower costs, appealing to retirees and remote workers who don't need big-city amenities. One underrated practical advantage across the whole country: Panama uses the US dollar as its official currency, which removes exchange-rate friction entirely for American expats and simplifies budgeting and international transfers for almost anyone earning or holding savings in dollars.

Processing timelines deserve realistic expectations too: official government targets are often optimistic, and practical residence processing across both Friendly Nations and Pensionado applications commonly takes three to six months in reality once document review, biometrics, and any requests for additional evidence are factored in — plan finances and housing around that longer window rather than the fastest-case scenario. Citizenship, for those who eventually want it, typically follows five years of continuous permanent residence plus a basic Spanish-language interview, a meaningfully lighter requirement than some European naturalization processes but still enough of a commitment that most people treat Panama primarily as a long-term residency base rather than a fast track to a second passport.
