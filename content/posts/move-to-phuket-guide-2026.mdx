**Moving to Phuket** in 2026 usually starts with a visa decision, not a housing search, because the island's rental market and school system only make sense once you know how long you're legally allowed to stay. For most remote workers, that means the **Destination Thailand Visa (DTV)**, launched in 2024 as Thailand's answer to the digital nomad boom. It grants **180 days per entry**, extendable for another **180 days from inside the country**, for a fee of roughly **10,000 THB (about $280)**. Applicants typically show around **$15,000–16,000** in bank funds, and — unlike a tourist visa — DTV holders can legally work remotely for a foreign employer or freelance clients without a Thai work permit, provided the income stays foreign-sourced.

Higher earners have a stronger option in the **Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa**, which runs up to **10 years** and is aimed at people with income from roughly **$80,000 a year** or investments starting around **$500,000**. LTR holders get a preferential **17% flat tax rate** on employment income, a meaningful discount against Thailand's normal progressive bands, plus multiple-entry convenience and less frequent renewal admin. Retirees generally go a different route: the **Non-Immigrant O-A visa**, open to applicants **50 and older**, which requires about **THB 800,000** sitting in a Thai bank account or income of at least **THB 65,000 a month**. It is explicitly a non-working visa, so anyone hoping to freelance on the side needs a different category. At the top end, **Thailand Elite** membership packages run **5 to 20 years** and start around **600,000 THB**, buying fast-track airport service and long-stay convenience without the recurring income proof other visas demand — but membership alone doesn't confer the right to work.

Budgeting for Phuket depends heavily on which corner of the island you land in. A comfortable solo lifestyle generally lands in the **$1,200–2,500 a month** range once rent, food, transport and the occasional beach-club afternoon are factored in. A well-located studio or one-bedroom typically rents for **12,000–25,000 THB a month (roughly $330–700)**, with the top of that range buying proximity to the beach or a newer building. Families weighing international schools should expect tuition in the neighborhood of **THB 200,000–450,000 a year per child**, though some schools quote in dollars closer to **$5,500–21,000**, so it's worth getting a direct quote rather than relying on averages. Where you settle shapes the whole experience: **Bang Tao and Laguna** cluster the island's strongest international schools and family infrastructure, **Rawai and Nai Harn** in the south offer a quieter, more residential expat scene away from the tourist strip, and **Kathu**, inland near Patong, trades beach proximity for noticeably better rental value.

Banking has gotten more complicated for short-stay visa holders. Many branches now prefer applicants to hold a longer-term visa — LTR, SMART, or Non-B — together with a Certificate of Residence before opening an account, which leaves DTV holders needing patience or, often, a paid agent to smooth the process. Most visa categories also require health insurance, commonly framed around minimums of **400,000 THB** in inpatient coverage and **40,000 THB** outpatient, so shop for a policy that clears those thresholds rather than a bare-bones travel plan. Property ownership has real limits worth understanding before you fall in love with a villa: foreigners can own **condominium units outright**, subject to a building-wide cap of **49% foreign ownership**, but cannot own land directly. Villas are almost always structured on **30-year renewable leases** instead, which is a fundamentally different (and less secure) form of tenure than freehold condo ownership.

Healthcare on the island is a genuine strength: Phuket has several internationally accredited private hospitals that regularly serve medical tourists as well as residents, offering care that's often faster and more comfortable than equivalent treatment in many Western countries, at a fraction of the cost. That quality is exactly why most visa categories mandate minimum insurance coverage rather than leaving it optional — private hospital bills without coverage can escalate quickly for anything beyond routine care. Day-to-day life on Phuket also runs on a distinctly seasonal rhythm: the dry season from roughly November through April brings the best weather and the heaviest tourist crowds, while the wetter months from May through October see quieter beaches, lower short-term accommodation prices, and the occasional tropical storm that longer-term residents learn to plan around rather than fear.
