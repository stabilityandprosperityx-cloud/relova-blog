## The Trade License and Residence Permit Route

**Moving to Prague** appeals to a specific type of relocator: people who want genuine Central European culture, a walkable historic core, and EU access without Western European prices. The city has long drawn freelancers and remote workers partly because the Czech **trade license (živnostenský list)** is one of the more accessible self-employment routes in the EU — registration typically costs around **CZK 1,000**, a modest administrative fee rather than a serious barrier, and the license itself covers a wide range of freelance activities from consulting to design to software development. That said, a trade license alone establishes your right to invoice as self-employed; it doesn't by itself grant a non-EU citizen the right to reside, so non-EU freelancers typically pair it with a long-term residence permit for business purposes, a process that involves proving accommodation, health insurance, and sufficient funds well before you can start legally invoicing clients from inside the country.

## Renting in Vinohrady, Žižkov, and Nové Město

Housing costs vary a lot depending on how central you want to be. One-bedroom apartments in the core expat-friendly districts — think **Vinohrady**, **Žižkov**, or the edges of **Nové Město** — typically run **CZK 18,000 to 32,000 a month**, a wide enough band to reflect the real gap between an older building a short tram ride from downtown and a renovated flat within walking distance of Old Town Square. Prague's rental market has tightened in recent years as the city's international profile has grown, so budgeting toward the upper half of that range is the safer assumption for anyone who wants a genuinely central location without a lengthy search.

## Transit, Beer, and Everyday Costs

Daily logistics in Prague are where the city quietly overperforms its price point. Public transport is excellent and cheap by Western European standards: a monthly transit pass runs around **CZK 670**, covering the metro, tram, and bus network across the entire city, which makes car-free living not just possible but genuinely convenient even for people living outside the absolute center. The metro and tram network in particular is dense enough that most neighborhoods worth living in sit within a 20–30 minute commute of downtown. Beyond transit, day-to-day costs — groceries, casual dining, beer (still famously cheap relative to quality), and services — remain noticeably below what a comparable Western European capital would charge, even as the city has grown more expensive relative to its own recent history.

## Building in a Budget Buffer for Setup Costs

A reasonable planning buffer is **15–25%** above your baseline monthly budget, covering currency conversion if your income arrives in euros, dollars, or another non-koruna currency, the setup costs of registering a trade license and opening a Czech bank account, and the general friction of a first few months where paperwork inevitably takes longer than expected. ## Public Insurance and Prague's Expat Network

Healthcare is worth planning early. Trade license holders and other self-employed residents pay into the Czech public health insurance system directly, with contributions tied to declared income rather than a flat fee, and coverage through the public system is generally solid for routine and emergency care once you're properly enrolled. Many expats also carry supplementary private insurance for faster access to English-speaking doctors or specialists, since the public system, while functional, can involve longer wait times for non-urgent specialist appointments. On the social side, Prague's international community is well established enough that building a network isn't difficult — coworking spaces, language exchange meetups, and a genuinely large expat population across the tech and creative sectors mean the first few months rarely feel isolating, even before your Czech improves enough for deeper local integration.
