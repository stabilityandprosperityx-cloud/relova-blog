Act 60's Individual Resident Investor decree is the reason most people research Puerto Rico at all, and 2026 is the year its terms actually changed. Legislation extending the program — commonly referenced as **Act 38-2026 / HB 505** — pushed the program's life out to **2055**, but it also drew a hard line: anyone who becomes a **bona fide Puerto Rico resident by December 31, 2026** locks in the historic **0% tax rate on qualifying interest, dividends, and post-relocation capital gains**. Apply after that date and you're routed into a new **4% rate on passive income** instead. That deadline is the single most consequential fact in this whole guide for anyone weighing the move for tax reasons — it changes the entire relocation timeline from "someday" to "this year or a materially worse deal."

## What the decree actually requires, ongoing

Beyond the relocation date, Act 60 comes with recurring obligations that catch people who only read the headline rate. Holders owe an **annual charitable donation of $10,000**, split evenly between two Puerto Rico-based nonprofits with at least one focused on child poverty — an obligation that begins in your second taxable year, not your first. You must **purchase a Puerto Rico home within two years** of your decree, held personally or through a qualifying trust (explicitly not an LLC), and use it as your principal residence purchased from an unrelated party. There's also a recently added **six-year non-residency lookback**, meaning recent Puerto Rico residents attempting to re-qualify face new scrutiny, plus an **annual $5,000 compliance report fee** and a **biennial compliance certificate** confirming you're still meeting all three bona fide residency tests: physical presence, tax home, and closer connection to Puerto Rico than anywhere else.

## The 183-day test is stricter than it sounds

The **presence test** requires roughly **183 days physically in Puerto Rico** within the tax year to satisfy bona fide residency — and "roughly" is doing real work in that sentence, because the IRS and Puerto Rico's own Treasury apply detailed day-counting rules (including exceptions for medical treatment and disaster evacuation) that make casual self-tracking risky. Anyone serious about the decree should be logging travel dates in something more rigorous than memory from day one, since a failed presence test doesn't just cost the tax benefit — it can trigger IRS scrutiny given the well-documented federal crackdown on Act 60 claims in recent years, aimed specifically at residents who claim the benefit without genuinely relocating their life and business to the island.

## Condado rent and the services-tax detail

A one-bedroom in **Condado**, San Juan's premium beachfront district and the default landing spot for relocating professionals, runs **roughly $1,500–2,500 a month** — priced closer to a mainland US coastal city than a typical Caribbean market, reflecting both genuine demand from Act 60 arrivals and Condado's built-in tourism infrastructure. Separately, Puerto Rico's **Act 60 export-services** track (for businesses, not individual investors) offers a **4% corporate tax rate** on income from qualifying services exported outside Puerto Rico — a distinct benefit from the individual investor decree, worth not conflating with your personal income tax planning if you're running a services business rather than living on investment income.

## Still the US, with real practical upsides

The advantage that a pure tax-rate comparison misses: Puerto Rico is a US territory, so relocating here involves no visa, no work permit, and no currency conversion for US citizens — the US dollar is the local currency, mail and banking integrate with mainland systems, and Puerto Rico-based businesses can still access the US market and federal programs in ways a genuinely foreign relocation cannot replicate. That structural simplicity is a real part of the pitch alongside the tax rate itself, and it's why Act 60 draws people who'd otherwise dismiss "moving abroad for taxes" as too much friction for the benefit.

Puerto Rico in 2026 is a **December-31-deadline, $10,000-annual-donation, 183-day-presence, $1,500–2,500-for-Condado** proposition. If the 0% rate is the point of the move, the calendar — not the paperwork — is now the binding constraint.
