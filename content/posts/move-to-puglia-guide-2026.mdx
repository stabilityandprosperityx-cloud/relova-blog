Puglia sits at the heel of Italy's boot, stretching roughly 800 kilometers of Adriatic and Ionian coastline between whitewashed hill towns, olive groves, and the conical trulli houses of the Valle d'Itria. It's cheaper than Tuscany or the Amalfi Coast, better connected than Sicily thanks to Bari's international airport, and — for retirees specifically — home to one of Italy's most generous tax incentives.

## The 7% flat tax is the real draw for retirees

Italy's regime forfettario for foreign pensioners lets qualifying retirees pay a flat 7% tax on all foreign-source income — pensions, investments, rental income abroad — for ten years, instead of Italy's standard progressive rates that can exceed 40%. As of Law 34/2026 (effective April 2026), the eligible-municipality population cap was raised from 20,000 to 30,000 residents, which pulled towns like Ostuni and Manduria into eligibility and brought Puglia's list of qualifying comuni to over 230. To qualify, you need to receive a pension from a foreign entity, have been non-resident in Italy for the five tax years prior, and genuinely register residency (iscrizione anagrafica) in an eligible town — a rented or owned home and utility bills in your name, not just a mailing address. This regime is retiree-specific; it does not apply to working-age remote employees or freelancers, who fall under Italy's separate [Digital Nomad Visa](/blog/move-to-italy-guide-2026) or standard tax brackets instead.

## Where people actually settle

Bari is the practical anchor — it has the region's main airport (direct flights to London, Paris, and several German cities), the best hospitals, and a working port, with one-bedroom rents around €450-700/month. Lecce, nicknamed the "Florence of the South" for its baroque architecture, trades some infrastructure for lifestyle and university-town energy, with 1BR rents closer to €400-600/month. Smaller coastal towns like Ostuni ("the White City") and Polignano a Mare offer the postcard version of Puglia — cliffside views, whitewashed centro storico streets — at €350-550/month for a one-bedroom, though summer tourist season pushes short-term rental prices up sharply and thins out long-term inventory.

## Visas and the practical setup

Non-EU nationals without an Italian passport route (many Argentinians and Brazilians of Italian descent qualify for jure sanguinis citizenship, worth checking before applying for any visa) typically enter Puglia via Italy's Elective Residency Visa (for those with passive income and no intent to work locally) or the Digital Nomad Visa launched in 2024 for remote employees and freelancers earning above roughly €28,000/year from non-Italian clients. Both require applying at an Italian consulate before arrival, health insurance, and proof of accommodation. Budget realistically for €1,000-1,600/month as a full nomad or retiree living cost outside the tax-incentive calculation — that covers rent, food, utilities, and local transport in a mid-size Puglia town, though Bari and beach-town summer premiums push that higher.

## Getting around without a rental car

Trenitalia and Italo connect Bari, Lecce, and Brindisi reasonably well, but the smaller towns — the ones with the best trulli and coastline — often require a car. Renting long-term or buying a used vehicle is common practice among Puglia's growing expat retiree community, many of whom cluster in Ostuni's international circle or Ceglie Messapica's slower-paced version of the same trend.

## Healthcare and the residency paperwork sequence

Once you've secured your visa and arrived, registering with the Servizio Sanitario Nazionale (SSN) at the local ASL (Azienda Sanitaria Locale) office gives access to Italy's public healthcare system — a straightforward process for residents holding a valid permesso di soggiorno, though the exact enrollment fee and process differ slightly by region. Puglia's regional health authority handles this locally in Bari, Lecce, and Brindisi provinces, and most expats find care quality solid in the larger cities, with smaller-town facilities sometimes requiring a drive to a provincial hospital for specialist treatment. Sequence matters here: apply for your codice fiscale (Italian tax code) first at the Agenzia delle Entrate, since it's a prerequisite for nearly everything that follows — opening a bank account, signing a residential lease, registering with the ASL, and enrolling in the 7% tax regime itself.
