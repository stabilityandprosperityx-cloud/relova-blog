Two visa routes cover most people moving to [Rome](/blog/naples-vs-rome-vs-milan-expat-guide-2026) in 2026, and picking the wrong one wastes months. The **[Digital Nomad Visa](/blog/move-to-italy-guide-2026)**, formalized by a 2024 government decree, is for non-EU remote employees and freelancers working for clients outside Italy: it requires **€28,000/year** in income for a single applicant (€33,600 with a spouse, plus roughly €1,500 per child), at least six months of prior remote-work history, "highly skilled" status via a degree or five years' relevant experience, and **€30,000** in Schengen-wide health coverage. It's valid 12 months and renewable indefinitely, with permanent residency reachable after five years. The older **Elective Residency Visa** targets non-EU retirees and anyone living on passive income (pensions, dividends, rental income) rather than active work — it has no employer-facing income test but demands proof you won't need to work locally, which the consulate scrutinizes hard. Confusing the two at application stage is the single most common rejection reason expat forums report.

## The permesso di soggiorno clock starts the moment you land

Whichever visa you enter on, Italian law requires you to apply for your **permesso di soggiorno** (residence permit) within **eight working days** of arrival, at the local Questura via a kit purchased at the post office (Poste Italiane) for around **€30**, plus a variable visa-processing fee. After your fingerprint appointment, the permit itself typically takes **four to eight weeks** to be ready for collection, longer in Rome specifically because of appointment backlogs at the Questura relative to smaller cities. Book the post-office kit appointment before you're jet-lagged and disorganized — missing the eight-day window creates a paperwork problem that's genuinely harder to fix than getting it right the first time. August is a dead month for Italian bureaucracy; if your arrival date is flexible, target a June or September start so your appointment window doesn't collide with ferragosto closures.

## Rent by neighborhood, not by "Rome average"

**Trastevere** one-bedrooms commonly run **€900–1,500/month** — you're paying for cobblestone charm and a five-minute walk to everything, plus tolerating tourist foot traffic outside your window most evenings. **Prati**, near the Vatican, sits similarly at **€1,000–1,600** but reads more residential-professional, with less nightlife noise and better proximity to international schools and embassies. **Pigneto**, east of the historic center, is the value play at **€700–1,100** — a working-class-turned-creative district with a genuine local food scene and improving metro access, without the postcard premium. Utilities, condo fees (if buying), and the mandatory TV license tax (canone RAI, bundled into electricity bills) add a few hundred euros a year that first-timers routinely forget to budget.

## Registering as a resident, not just a visa-holder

Getting the permesso is not the same as being registered at your local **anagrafe** (civil registry) — that's a separate step (*residenza*) that Italian bureaucracy expects but doesn't chase you to complete, and it's the record that determines your access to the national health service (SSN) and your tax residency status. Skipping it is common among people who plan to stay "just a year or two," but if you're on the digital nomad or elective residency track for the long haul, register early — retroactive registration is more paperwork, not less.

Rome in 2026 rewards people who treat the eight-day permesso deadline as non-negotiable and pick their visa category based on whether they're working or living off passive income — get that distinction wrong at the consulate stage and you're refiling, not adjusting.
