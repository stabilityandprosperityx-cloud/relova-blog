Rwanda is the easiest East African country to simply show up in: visitors from nearly every nationality can get a visa on arrival or apply online in advance through the government's IremboGov portal, no sponsor letter or invitation required for a standard 30-day tourist entry. What follows from there — Kigali's genuinely unusual cleanliness (plastic bags have been banned since 2008, and a mandatory monthly community cleanup called Umuganda keeps streets in visibly better shape than most regional capitals), reliable fiber internet, and one of the safest walking-around cities in the region — is why it has quietly become a serious remote-work base rather than just a gorilla-trekking stopover.

## Visa and longer-stay options

For stays beyond the standard tourist window, Rwanda has been actively expanding its remote-work and longer-term entry options as part of a broader push to position Kigali as a regional tech and conference hub (it already hosts the Africa headquarters for several global tech and NGO organizations). Programs and eligibility criteria for remote-worker permits have shifted more than once in the past few years, so the single most important piece of advice here is to check the current requirements directly on **IremboGov** (irembo.gov.rw), Rwanda's official e-government portal, before finalizing any plans — published income thresholds and permit categories for remote workers have not been fully stable year to year, and third-party guides (including this one) can lag behind the government's own updates.

## Where to live and what it costs

**Kimihurura**, Kigali's most established expat and diplomatic neighborhood, sits close to the city's embassies, international schools, and a growing cluster of coworking spaces and specialty cafés — one-bedroom apartments here commonly run **USD 400–700/month**. **Kiyovu**, an older hillside residential area closer to the city center, offers a similar profile at a modest discount, typically **USD 350–600/month**, with somewhat older housing stock but strong walkability to downtown. Both neighborhoods see seasonal rent pressure tied to conference and tourism calendars — Kigali hosts a steady stream of international conferences that can temporarily tighten short-term rental supply.

## Budgeting for gorilla trekking

If Rwanda is on your list partly for its mountain gorilla trekking in Volcanoes National Park, budget seriously for it: permits issued by the Rwanda Development Board run **around USD 1,500 per person per trek**, among the most expensive wildlife-viewing experiences in the world, and they sell out months in advance during peak season (June–September and December–February). It's a one-time splurge most residents do once or twice during a longer stay rather than a recurring expense, but it needs its own line item — don't fold it into a monthly cost-of-living estimate.

## Connectivity, safety, and daily practicalities

Kigali's internet infrastructure is a genuine draw for remote workers: 4G coverage is broad and reliable across the capital, fiber connections are increasingly available in the neighborhoods expats favor, and mobile money (via MTN Mobile Money and Airtel Money) is deeply embedded in everyday commerce, making cashless daily life more practical here than in many wealthier countries. Rwanda also consistently ranks as one of the safer countries in Sub-Saharan Africa for walking around at night, a reputation that's held up well against the region's average and is part of why Kigali attracts a disproportionate number of solo remote workers and families relative to its size. The trade-off is that Rwanda remains a small, English-French-Kinyarwanda trilingual market with a limited range of specialty imported goods compared with larger regional hubs like Nairobi — worth knowing before you assume you'll find every home-country grocery staple on a Kigali supermarket shelf.
