Santiago, Chile — Andean skyline, not the Galician pilgrimage city of the same name — runs one of South America's more straightforward remote-work residency systems, wrapped around a rental market that varies more by comuna than almost any other capital on the continent.

## Chile skipped the "[digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-albania-guide-2026)" branding, not the pathway

Chile has no visa formally called a "digital nomad visa." What functions as one is the **temporary residency visa** for remote workers with income from abroad, and while there is no single officially published minimum, immigration lawyers and relocation services consistently recommend documenting at least **USD 1,500–2,000 a month** in stable, verifiable income to clear review comfortably. Processing has been running **six to eight months** in 2026, which is long enough that you should not plan a departure date around when the visa lands — start the file well before you intend to move, and budget the application fee, roughly **USD 300** though it varies by nationality under Chile's reciprocity-based fee schedule. The visa itself grants up to **two years**, renewable, with a path to permanent residency after two continuous years — treat the first grant as a real commitment window, not a trial period you can casually let lapse.

## Start-Up Chile is a grant program, not a visa shortcut

Start-Up Chile, run by the government's economic development agency CORFO, offers qualifying founders **equity-free grants reported up to USD 80,000** alongside a dedicated one-year visa track for the founding team — a genuinely different and more generous package than the older "$50,000" figure still floating around some guides. It is competitive and selection-based, built for founders launching an actual company in Chile, not a remote-income placeholder for people who just want faster paperwork. If your real goal is simply living in Santiago while working for a foreign employer, the standard temporary residency visa is the correct and simpler route; Start-Up Chile is worth pursuing only if you are genuinely building a Chile-based venture.

## Providencia, Las Condes, and Bellavista price very differently

**Providencia** is the balanced default for most relocating professionals — walkable, well-served by the Metro, dense with cafés and coworking space — with one-beds typically running **CLP 600,000–900,000** a month. **Las Condes**, home to Santiago's corporate towers and the Sanhattan business district, runs noticeably higher at **CLP 700,000–1,100,000** for comparable finishes, reflecting proximity to multinational offices and higher-end new-build stock. **Bellavista**, the nightlife and arts district at the foot of Cerro San Cristóbal, is the cheapest of the three at **CLP 500,000–800,000**, trading some polish and quiet for bars, live music, and walking distance to the funicular. Sync any Chilean rent quote against a current CLP/USD rate before comparing to home-country prices — the peso has moved enough over recent years that a stale exchange-rate assumption will throw off your budget more than the rent itself does.

## The tax bracket that catches high earners off guard

Chile's income tax is progressive and tops out with a marginal rate that reaches roughly **40%** for the highest earners, a detail that matters specifically for anyone becoming a Chilean tax resident (generally triggered after 183 days in a 12-month period) rather than simply working remotely for a foreign employer while paying tax elsewhere. Whether your foreign-sourced income is taxed in Chile at all, and how any home-country tax treaty interacts with it, is genuinely case-specific — this is the one area of a Santiago move worth a paid consultation with a Chilean tax accountant before you cross the residency threshold, not after.
