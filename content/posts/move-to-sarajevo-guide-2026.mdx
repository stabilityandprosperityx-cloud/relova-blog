Sarajevo is not on the digital-nomad-visa lists, and that is the first thing to get straight before you fall for the €400 central one-bed number. **Bosnia and Herzegovina has no dedicated remote-work visa** — unlike Croatia, Serbia, or Albania next door. Most long-stay foreigners get in through a **temporary residence permit** tied to employment, business registration, family reunification, or property ownership, filed at the local **Service for Foreigners' Affairs** office. Budget for translated, apostilled documents and a police-clearance certificate from your home country; processing commonly runs **one to three months**, and you generally need to apply from outside Bosnia or hold a valid basis to convert status once you land.

## The currency board nobody mentions

The **konvertibilna marka (BAM)** is not a floating currency you need to hedge against — it has been pegged by currency board since **1997** at **1 EUR = 1.95583 BAM**, full stop, no central-bank discretion to devalue it. That fixed peg is one of the underrated reasons Sarajevo budgeting is easier than in Serbia or Turkey: a rent quoted in marks today is the same euro number next year. It also means BAM prices track eurozone inflation more than domestic wage growth, which is part of why rents have crept up faster than local salaries over the past few years.

## Two tax systems, one country

Bosnia is split into two entities with **separate tax administrations**, and which one taxes you depends on where you're registered. The **Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBiH)** — which includes Sarajevo — charges a **flat 10% personal income tax**. **Republika Srpska**, the other entity, charges **8%** on most income, with a reduced **2%** rate for small entrepreneurs on gross revenue. VAT is a single countrywide **17%**, administered by the state-level Indirect Taxation Authority, so at least that number doesn't change when you cross the inter-entity boundary line on your commute to Jahorina. If you're freelancing rather than employed locally, note that Bosnia has no digital-nomad tax carve-out — you're either a Bosnian tax resident paying FBiH rates on worldwide income after 183 days, or you're structuring income through your home country and treating Sarajevo as a long tourist-plus-residence-permit stay, which needs its own paperwork trail.

## What a one-bedroom actually costs

Numbeo's 2026 data puts a **one-bedroom in central Sarajevo at roughly 794 KM a month (~€406)**, and about **542 KM (~€277)** outside the centre — genuinely cheap by Western European standards, but worth weighing against the average net salary in the city, which sits around **1,813 KM (~€927)** a month. That means a well-located central one-bed can eat close to half of a typical local paycheck; it's the expat arriving with euro or dollar income who finds Sarajevo comfortable, not necessarily the neighbor renting it out to you. Utilities, a starter grocery run, and public transport (Sarajevo's tram and trolleybus network is old but functional and cheap) add maybe another 150–250 KM before you've bought anything discretionary.

## Property and the reciprocity trap

Foreigners can buy real estate in Bosnia, but ownership by individuals is generally governed by a **reciprocity principle** — Bosnia allows a foreign national to own property if that person's home country allows Bosnian citizens to own property there. In practice this is usually handled cleanly for EU, US, UK, and most Western passport holders, but it is a real legal check a notary will run, not a formality to skip. Buying through a locally registered company sidesteps some of this friction and is the route many longer-term investors and business-based residents use instead.

## Why the mountains change the calculus

Sarajevo's compressed geography is genuinely unusual for a European capital: **Jahorina**, the 1984 Winter Olympics venue, is roughly a **30–40 minute drive** from the city centre, and **Bjelašnica** is similarly close on the other side of the valley. That is a shorter commute to a real ski resort than most Alpine capital-adjacent cities offer. It also means winter air quality in the valley itself — Sarajevo sits in a bowl and has a well-documented smog problem from wood and coal heating between November and February — is a genuine trade-off against the mountain access, and worth experiencing in person during a scouting trip before committing to a year-long lease.
