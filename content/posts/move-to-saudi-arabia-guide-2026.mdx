Saudi Arabia's **Premium Residency** ("Saudi Green Card," launched 2019, expanded steadily since) is the first real exit from the kafala sponsorship system anywhere in the Gulf, and it comes in more flavors than the marketing suggests. The **Permanent Track** costs a one-time **SAR 800,000** (roughly **$213,000**) and asks for five-plus years of financial documentation showing genuine wealth — advisors generally tell clients to expect scrutiny unless liquid net worth clears **$1 million or more**. The **Limited Track** is the one most working expats actually use: **SAR 100,000 a year** (about **$27,000**), renewable annually with no fixed end date, tied to qualifying employment or business activity. There's also an **Investor Track** requiring roughly **SAR 7 million (~$1.87M)** deployed into a Saudi company or qualifying real estate, and a no-fee **Talent Track** reserved for PhD-level specialists sponsored by institutions like KAUST or NEOM in Vision 2030 priority sectors — AI, biotech, renewables. None of these tracks replace a work visa for a standard employed role; most arriving professionals still land on employer-sponsored *iqama* status and only look at Premium Residency once they want independence from a single sponsor.

## Zero income tax, but not zero cost

The headline that pulls people in is real: Saudi Arabia levies **0% personal income tax** on salaries, and there's no capital gains tax on individuals either. What isn't zero is **GOSI** — the General Organization for Social Insurance — which both Saudi nationals and, in narrower categories, expatriate employees contribute to depending on their contract structure; and a **15% VAT** on most goods and services, raised from 5% back in 2020 and unchanged since. Businesses pay a flat **20% corporate income tax** on foreign-owned entities, or **Zakat** (a religious wealth levy, typically 2.5%) on Saudi- and GCC-owned shares of a company. If you're setting up as a contractor rather than drawing an employer payroll, that corporate distinction is the first thing a tax advisor will walk you through — it changes your effective rate more than the "0% income tax" headline implies.

## Riyadh rents and the compound question

A one-bedroom in central Riyadh commonly runs **SAR 3,000–6,000 a month** depending on building age and whether it's inside a Western-oriented compound with pool and gym access — compounds sit at the top of that range and add a premium for the on-site schools and social clubs that make family relocation easier. Jeddah runs slightly cheaper for comparable space; Riyadh has pulled ahead on price as Vision 2030 mega-projects (NEOM, Qiddiya, the Riyadh metro) concentrate hiring and housing demand in the capital. Budget separately for a car or ride-hailing subscription — Riyadh's metro opened in stages through 2024–2025 but doesn't yet cover every district — and for the alcohol-free, gender-segregated-in-places lifestyle adjustment that catches first-time arrivals off guard more than any visa paperwork does.

## The sponsorship reality most guides skip

Even with Premium Residency on the table, the practical default for the large majority of arriving professionals is still an employer-sponsored *iqama*, which ties your legal status to that employer far more tightly than a Western work permit does — changing jobs typically requires your current sponsor's cooperation or a formal exit process. Read your offer letter's exit and transfer clauses before you read the salary line. Family visas for a spouse and children ride on the primary *iqama* holder's status, and school-year timing around September intake windows is worth planning six months out, not six weeks.

Saudi Arabia in 2026 is a **0%-income-tax, sponsor-anchored** market with a genuine — if expensive — residency escape hatch once you've been there long enough to qualify. Get the Premium Residency tier right for your actual net worth before you pay a fee that assumes you're wealthier than you are.
