Sicily is the largest island in the Mediterranean and, for expats, one of the best value-for-money entry points into EU living: full Schengen and Italian legal infrastructure, genuinely warm winters compared to the mainland north, and rents that undercut nearly every other large Italian city. The trade-off is a slower, more informal pace of bureaucracy and business than [Milan](/blog/move-to-milan-guide-2026) or Rome — worth knowing going in rather than discovering on arrival.

## Palermo vs Catania: two very different bases

Palermo, the regional capital, is the bigger and more historically dense of the two — a Norman-Arab-Byzantine old center, a genuinely large expat and international-student population anchored around the university, and a one-bedroom apartment in a well-connected neighborhood (Libertà, Politeama, the edges of the historic center) typically running EUR 400-650 a month. Catania, on the east coast at the foot of Mount Etna, is smaller but has the advantage of Fontanarossa Airport being minutes from the center rather than a taxi ride out of town, plus a livelier late-night and university-driven social scene; a comparable one-bedroom there runs EUR 350-600. Ortigia, the tiny historic island core of nearby Syracuse, is a third option worth naming separately — a walkable, postcard-pretty old town increasingly popular with remote workers and retirees, though rents there run higher than either Palermo or Catania proper given the limited housing stock and tourist demand.

## Connectivity and day-to-day practicality

Sicily has decent but not fast internal transport: trains between Palermo and Catania take around three hours despite the modest distance, so most people traveling between the two cities drive or fly. Both Palermo (Falcone-Borsellino) and Catania airports have direct connections to major mainland Italian cities and a growing number of international routes, which matters if you're commuting back to a home country periodically. Internet infrastructure in the two main cities is solid — fiber is widely available in central neighborhoods — though smaller towns and rural areas can lag noticeably, worth checking building-by-building if you're planning remote work from a smaller comune.

## Healthcare and daily practicalities

Sicily is fully part of Italy's Servizio Sanitario Nazionale (national health service), so once you're a registered resident with a codice fiscale (tax code) and an Italian residency address, you can register with the SSN and access a general practitioner and public hospital care at low or no direct cost — a real advantage over countries where residents must arrange private insurance from scratch. Many expats still carry supplemental private coverage for shorter wait times on non-urgent specialist care, since public system wait times in Sicily can run longer than in northern Italy. Day-to-day life leans heavily on local markets (Palermo's Ballarò and Vucciria markets, Catania's La Pescheria fish market) for produce and fish, which are both cheaper and fresher than supermarket equivalents — worth building into your routine early rather than defaulting to the nearest Conad or Lidl.

## What a realistic monthly budget looks like

A single person or couple living modestly but comfortably in Palermo or Catania — rent, groceries, transport, some dining out — typically lands in the EUR 1,000-1,700 a month range, among the lowest of any major EU city with this level of infrastructure and international connectivity. If you're moving as a retiree rather than a remote worker, it's worth knowing that Sicily is one of the regions eligible for Italy's 7% flat tax on foreign income for retirees relocating to smaller towns; our [retirement-focused guide](/blog/retire-to-italy-guide-2026) covers the income thresholds and eligibility mechanics of that regime in full, since the details (population caps, which comuni qualify) matter more than we can do justice to here.
