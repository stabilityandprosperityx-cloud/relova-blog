**Moving to Slovakia**, and specifically to **Bratislava**, appeals to a niche but growing group: people who want genuine EU access and proximity to Vienna without paying Vienna prices. The two capitals sit close enough that the **Railjet** train connects them in roughly **one hour** station to station, which has turned Bratislava into something of a commuter-adjacent base for people earning Austrian or broader Western European salaries while living on a Slovak cost structure — a genuinely unusual arbitrage in the EU. One-bedroom rents in Bratislava typically run **€700–1,100 a month**, and it's worth confirming with any specific listing whether heating is included in that figure, since Central European rentals split fairly evenly between all-inclusive and utilities-separate arrangements.

For non-EU professionals, the **EU [Blue Card](/blog/lisbon-vs-barcelona-expat-guide-2026)** is the main skilled-worker route into Slovakia, and current tables put the salary floor somewhere around **€14,000 a year**, though that figure moves with Slovak wage statistics and should be reconfirmed against europa.eu or the relevant Slovak ministry before an employer files paperwork on your behalf — treating a specific number as permanently fixed is one of the more common mistakes in EU [Blue Card](/blog/move-to-lima-guide-2026) planning generally, since every member state recalculates its threshold periodically. Slovakia's economy leans heavily on automotive manufacturing — Volkswagen, Kia, and a dense supplier network anchor a meaningful share of national employment — which gives the country unusually stable payroll and industrial employment relative to its size, even as IT and services have grown into a second major pillar in Bratislava specifically.

Language is worth being honest about. Bratislava's IT and international business scene runs comfortably in English, and plenty of expats build a full professional and social life without more than basic Slovak. But that changes quickly outside the capital's international bubble: shop-floor roles in manufacturing, interactions with local bureaucracy, and day-to-day life in smaller towns still reward at least conversational Slovak, and credibility with local colleagues on the production floor in particular tends to track language effort more than it does in a pure tech office. For anyone eyeing eventual citizenship, Slovakia's naturalization path generally runs around **8 years** of qualifying residence along with a **B1-level** Slovak language requirement — a meaningfully longer and more language-dependent route than some neighboring countries, so it's a factor worth weighing early if citizenship, not just long-term residency, is the goal.

Day-to-day logistics benefit from Bratislava's compact size and its cross-border position: the Vienna commute covers roughly **60 kilometers**, and regional ticket packages covering both the Austrian and Slovak legs make regular cross-border travel for work or shopping genuinely routine rather than a special occasion. Anyone comparing Bratislava against other Central European bases should also look at the [Czech Republic guide](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/move-to-czech-republic-guide-2026), which covers a similarly positioned but larger neighboring market.

Healthcare in Slovakia runs through a mandatory public health insurance system, funded through payroll or self-employment contributions once you're formally resident and working, and coverage is generally adequate for both routine and emergency needs. As with much of Central Europe, some expats supplement public coverage with a private policy for faster specialist access, particularly outside Bratislava where the public system's capacity is more stretched. Day-to-day cost of living beyond rent stays comfortably low by EU standards — groceries, dining out, and local transport all price well below Vienna, Munich, or other nearby Western European hubs, which is a big part of why the Bratislava-Vienna commuter pattern has become genuinely popular rather than a theoretical arbitrage. Banking and general administrative processes have modernized considerably in recent years, though non-EU applicants should still expect the residence permit process itself, rather than day-to-day banking, to be the slower part of settling in.
