## E-7, F-1-D, and Choosing the Right Visa Category

**Moving to South Korea** as a foreigner runs through a handful of distinct legal pathways, and getting the category wrong is the single most common reason applications stall. Traditional employment sponsorship through the **E-series visas** (E-7 for skilled professionals being the most common route for foreign hires) remains the default for people joining a Korean company. Alongside it, South Korea introduced a **Digital Nomad Visa (F-1-D)** aimed at remote workers employed by foreign companies, and eligibility discussions around it commonly cite an income requirement in the neighborhood of **$84,000 a year** — a meaningfully higher bar than most European digital nomad visas, reflecting Korea's more selective approach to the category. That figure is worth confirming directly against the current Korean immigration portal before filing, since income thresholds for newer visa categories tend to get refined in the first several years after launch.

## Seoul's Jeonse System vs. Busan's Lower Costs

Budgeting for Korea is very much a city-first exercise, and treating "Korea" as a single cost-of-living number is one of the fastest ways to underfund a move. In **Seoul**, a one-bedroom apartment typically runs **KRW 1,000,000–1,800,000 a month**, though Seoul's rental market also commonly involves **jeonse** or large **key money (key deposits)** arrangements that function very differently from Western month-to-month renting — worth researching specifically before committing to a lease, since a large upfront deposit in exchange for reduced or zero monthly rent is a genuinely distinct system many newcomers aren't prepared for. **Busan**, Korea's second city and a genuinely livable alternative with a real coastline, generally runs **20–30% cheaper than Seoul** across housing and daily costs, making it worth serious consideration for remote workers who don't need Seoul's specific job market or nightlife density.

## NHIS Enrollment and Korea's Internet Infrastructure

Healthcare access runs through South Korea's **National Health Insurance Service (NHIS)**, which most long-term visa holders are required to enroll in once resident. Monthly contributions are often quoted around a baseline near **KRW 150,000**, though the actual amount scales with reported income and household size rather than sitting at a flat rate, so treat that figure as a rough anchor rather than a fixed cost. On the infrastructure side, South Korea's internet remains among the fastest in the world, with urban connections commonly exceeding **200 Mbps** and genuinely reliable coverage even outside the capital — a real advantage for anyone doing video-heavy remote work.

## Building a Contingency Budget for Bureaucratic Delays

A sensible way to budget is to build three scenarios — lean, realistic, and a stress-case that assumes visa delays or a weaker won — and hold a **15–25% contingency** above your realistic number, since deposit structures, apostille processing for foreign documents, and the general friction of Korea's more document-heavy bureaucracy tend to add unplanned cost in the first few months. ## Banking, the ARC, and Social Integration by City

Banking and daily administration in Korea reward preparation. Opening a bank account as a foreigner typically requires your Alien Registration Card (ARC), which itself takes several weeks to process after arrival, so budget for a cash-heavy or card-limited first month before your Korean banking and mobile phone setup is fully operational. Once established, though, Korea's digital infrastructure for everyday life — mobile payments, delivery apps, banking apps — is genuinely best-in-class and quickly becomes one of the more convenient parts of daily life. Social integration varies significantly by city: Seoul's international districts (Itaewon, parts of Hongdae) offer an easy on-ramp for English speakers, while Busan and smaller cities reward at least basic Korean more quickly. Either way, most long-term expats find that investing in language study within the first year meaningfully changes the quality of daily life, from grocery shopping to navigating the healthcare system beyond the basics NHIS enrollment covers.
