## Digital Nomad Visa, Non-Lucrative Visa, and Family Income Math

**Moving to Spain as a family** is fundamentally a different project than a solo remote-work relocation — it's a visa-plus-school-plus-healthcare exercise with a realistic **6–12 month** timeline from decision to settled daily life, and the visa math scales with every additional person in the household. On the **[Digital Nomad Visa](/blog/move-to-malaga-guide-2026)**, the most common route for families with a remote-working parent, the main applicant needs to show income around **€2,849 a month** (roughly 200% of Spain's minimum wage following the **February 2026** increase), plus an additional **€916** for the first dependent and **€305** for each additional dependent after that — which works out to about **€4,070 a month pre-tax** for a family of three. The alternative **[Non-Lucrative Visa](/blog/spain-non-lucrative-visa-guide-2026)** route, aimed at families living off savings or passive income rather than active remote work, requires roughly **€28,800 a year** for the main applicant plus **€7,200 a year** per dependent, but comes with a hard restriction: no work of any kind, including remote work for a foreign employer, which makes it a poor fit for anyone still actively earning. Spain's **Golden Visa**, once a popular route for wealthier families buying property, closed to new applicants on **3 April 2025**, so it's no longer part of the realistic pathway list regardless of budget.

## Family Reunification for Staggered Arrivals

For families who don't qualify through employment or savings alone, **reagrupación familiar** (family reunification) is the other major route worth understanding — it allows a resident in Spain (typically the visa-holding parent) to bring a spouse and children over once that resident has held valid status for a minimum qualifying period, generally around one year, and can demonstrate adequate housing and income to support the family being reunified. This matters most for families where one parent secures a Spanish work visa or job offer first and the rest of the household follows once that base residency is established, rather than the whole family applying together from day one. It's a genuinely different administrative track from the Digital Nomad or Non-Lucrative routes, with its own income and housing-adequacy documentation, and worth discussing with an immigration lawyer specifically if your family's situation involves staggered arrivals rather than a single joint application.

## Public School Immersion vs. International School Tuition

School choice is where a lot of the real budget variation lives. A family of four relying on **public school** typically spends **€2,000–4,500 a month** overall depending on city — Barcelona and Madrid sit at the top of that range, while Valencia, Málaga, and Seville run meaningfully cheaper. Spain's public schools accept resident children regardless of their existing Spanish level, and younger kids in particular tend to adapt to full Spanish-language instruction within a year or so through structured immersion support most schools provide. **International school** is the other path, adding roughly **€500–1,500 a month per child** on top of general living costs (other estimates put full annual tuition closer to **€5,000–15,000** depending on the school and curriculum — British, American, and IB options all exist across the major cities). The choice between the two often comes down to age and continuity: younger children generally do better long-term integrating into the Spanish public system, while teenagers aged **14 and up** frequently stay in international school specifically to preserve continuity toward a home-country or IB university pathway rather than switching systems mid-adolescence. ## Empadronamiento, the TIE, and Enrolling Kids in Care

Healthcare for kids runs through Spain's **public health system (Sistema Nacional de Salud)** once the family is properly registered, and Spanish pediatric care is genuinely strong by international standards — well-staffed, broadly accessible, and free at the point of use for enrolled residents. The catch is that enrollment is gated behind administrative registration, not automatic on arrival: **empadronamiento**, registering your address at the local town hall, is the essential first step after landing, because without it you cannot enroll children in either public school or the public health system. Non-EU visa holders also need to obtain their **TIE** (foreigner identity card) within **one month** of entry. One trap worth planning around explicitly: much of Spain's local administration slows dramatically or effectively closes during **August**, the traditional national holiday month, so scheduling your empadronamiento or TIE appointment for late July or August risks weeks of delay right when your kids need to be enrolled in school — plan arrival timing around that calendar reality wherever your visa timeline allows.
