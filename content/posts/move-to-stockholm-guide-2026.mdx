## Sweden's New 90% Work Permit Salary Floor

**Moving to Stockholm** in 2026 means navigating one of the more significant work-permit shifts anywhere in Europe this year. As of **1 June 2026**, a standard Swedish work permit (**arbetstillstånd**) requires a salary at **90% of Sweden's median wage**, which currently puts the floor at **SEK 33,390 a month** — a meaningful jump from the previous **80%** standard, which had sat around **SEK 29,680–30,640**. Sweden still has no dedicated digital nomad visa, so anyone relocating without an existing EU right to work needs to come in through employment sponsorship, the **EU Blue Card**, or a self-employment track, making this salary threshold the single most consequential number in the whole relocation.

## The Transition Window and Shortage Occupation Carve-Out

There is a transition window that matters for anyone already in Sweden on an older permit. If your permit was issued **before 1 June 2026** and you file your renewal between **1 June and 1 December 2026**, the older **80%** threshold can still apply to that renewal — a grace period clearly designed to avoid immediately displacing people already settled under the old rules. From **1 December 2026** onward, though, every renewal has to clear the new **90%** floor regardless of when the original permit was issued, so anyone renewing later in the year should budget for the higher number rather than assume the transition protects them indefinitely. There's also a carve-out for roughly **152 shortage occupations** — spanning medicine, nursing, and a range of IT and engineering roles — where employers can hire at **75% of median wage** instead of the standard 90%, a genuinely useful discount for people in in-demand fields, though the specific list of qualifying occupations is worth checking against the current Migration Agency register before negotiating a job offer around it, since shortage lists get revised periodically as labor market conditions shift.

## Why the EU Blue Card Has Gotten More Attractive

The **EU Blue Card** route has actually gotten more attractive alongside these changes. Its salary requirement holds steady at **SEK 52,000 a month**, well above the standard work-permit floor, but validity has been extended from two years to **four years**, cutting the renewal burden roughly in half for qualifying skilled professionals. One new requirement worth flagging: from **1 June 2026**, anyone staying in Sweden for **under one year** must separately hold full private medical insurance, a distinct obligation from the general permit requirements that's easy to overlook if you're focused primarily on the salary threshold. Permanent residence, for those planning a longer horizon, typically follows **four years of continuous permit-based stay within the preceding seven years** — a rule that hasn't changed alongside the salary reforms and remains the standard path to long-term settlement.

## Cost of Living and the Real Tax Bite

Once the visa question is settled, Stockholm's cost of living is straightforwardly Nordic: expensive by most European standards but paired with strong public services and infrastructure. A comfortable solo budget typically runs **$2,000–2,950 a month** (roughly **SEK 22,000–32,000**), covering a reasonable apartment, food, transport, and normal social spending. Taxes are the other half of the honest budgeting picture: municipal income tax runs around **32%**, and a national state tax of up to **25%** kicks in on income above roughly **SEK 642,100 a year**, pushing marginal rates on higher earners toward **57%** in combination. That gap between gross salary — the number that clears the Migration Agency's threshold — and actual take-home pay is significant enough that anyone comparing a Swedish job offer to an offer elsewhere should model net income explicitly rather than comparing headline salaries. ## Navigating the Bostadskö Rental Queue

Housing in Stockholm has its own quirks worth understanding before you arrive: the city runs a heavily regulated rental market with long public queues (**bostadskö**) for coveted first-hand contracts, which pushes many newcomers toward the secondary sublet market or corporate housing arrangements in their first year while they build up queue time. That system genuinely shapes the practical experience of settling in more than salary or tax numbers alone — budgeting extra time and, often, extra cost for temporary housing in the first several months is worth planning for explicitly rather than assuming a straightforward apartment search on arrival. On the upside, once housing is sorted, Stockholm's public transport, healthcare quality, and general urban livability rank among the best in Europe, which is a large part of why the salary and tax trade-offs continue to draw skilled workers despite the cost.
