Switzerland's immigration system splits cleanly along one line: EU/EFTA nationals move under the Agreement on the Free Movement of Persons, with relatively light-touch registration, while non-EU/EFTA nationals face a capped quota system and employer sponsorship requirements that make Switzerland one of the harder Western European countries to move to on a work basis. The tax and cost picture then splits again — not by nationality this time, but by canton, since Switzerland's 26 cantons set their own tax rates on top of the federal rate, and the gap between the cheapest and most expensive is enormous.

## Permits: B, L, and the non-EU/EFTA quota

EU/EFTA nationals can enter Switzerland to look for work and register for a permit relatively easily, generally without needing employer sponsorship secured in advance. Non-EU/EFTA nationals face a materially different process: employers must prove no suitable EU/EFTA or Swiss candidate was available, and even qualifying hires draw against an annual federal quota — the Federal Council maintained the 2025 allocation into 2026 at 4,500 B permits (initial residence permits, typically tied to a specific employer and canton, valid one to five years depending on contract length) and 4,000 L permits (short-term permits, generally under a year, for project-based or seasonal work) for non-EU/EFTA nationals. These quotas are split between the federal government and the cantons and can be exhausted well before year-end in a strong hiring market, so timing an application early in the calendar year improves your odds.

## From B to C: the path to settlement differs by nationality too

The B permit is renewable but tied to continued employment and canton residence; the C permit (settlement permit) is the more durable status, granting broader work and residence flexibility. Here nationality matters again: EU/EFTA nationals (and, by long-standing administrative practice, US, UK, and Canadian nationals) can generally apply for a C permit after five years of continuous lawful residence, while most other non-EU/EFTA nationals need ten years, with the final five typically required to be held continuously on a B permit. Swiss citizenship sits beyond that — typically requiring at least ten years of residence (with some cantons weighting recent years more heavily) plus successful integration and language requirements, so the realistic total timeline from arrival to a Swiss passport commonly runs well over a decade for non-EU/EFTA nationals.

## Zug vs Geneva vs Zurich: the tax gap is not trivial

This is where "moving to Switzerland" as a single financial decision breaks down. Zug, a small canton south of Zurich long known as a magnet for holding companies and high earners, has some of the lowest tax rates in the country — maximum marginal income tax rates around 22%, among the cheapest of all 26 cantons. Zurich, the largest city and financial hub, sits meaningfully higher, with maximum marginal rates around 40%, while Geneva, French-speaking and home to a large international-organization and banking community, runs highest of the three at maximum marginal rates above 43%. For someone earning a solid professional salary, the difference between basing yourself in Zug versus Geneva can be tens of thousands of francs a year — a genuine reason many well-paid professionals choose a Zug or Zurich-adjacent canton over Geneva if their employer and lifestyle needs allow the flexibility.

## What it costs to actually live there

Rent tracks the same divide loosely: a one-bedroom in Zurich typically runs CHF 2,000-2,600, while Geneva runs slightly higher at CHF 2,200-2,800, reflecting both cities' status as Switzerland's most expensive rental markets. Layer onto that Switzerland's mandatory private health insurance (Krankenversicherung), which every resident must buy individually within three months of arrival — premiums commonly run CHF 350-500 a month per adult depending on canton, deductible level, and age, with no employer subsidy built in the way many other European systems provide. Switzerland's unemployment rate has stayed low by European standards, commonly cited around 2.9%, a reflection of the tight, competitive labor market that also explains why the non-EU/EFTA quota system exists in the first place — Swiss authorities protect the domestic labor pool tightly even as they compete globally for skilled talent.
