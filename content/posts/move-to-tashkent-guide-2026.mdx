Uzbekistan is one of the less-discussed entries on the Central Asian budget-relocation circuit, and it earns the comparison mostly on cost, not on bureaucratic ease — the country is easier to enter than to settle in long-term, and the two should not be confused.

## Getting in the door versus staying

Tourism and short-stay e-visas process online through the official e-visa portal (e-visa.gov.uz) for around **USD 20**, typically covering a single- or double-entry stay of up to 30 days — fine for scoping the city, useless as a residence strategy. The route built specifically for tech workers is the **IT Visa**, expanded in scope during 2026 to cover IT Park resident-company founders and executives, foreign investors, and Digital Startup Program founders. The financial bar on the investor track is specific: applicants generally need to show **USD 30,000** either invested directly into an Uzbek IT entity or held as an equivalent ownership share in that company's charter capital. Treat this as the headline threshold to verify against the current IT Park rulebook at filing time, since program scope has been widened more than once in the last two years and eligibility categories are the part most likely to keep shifting.

## The IT Park ecosystem is the actual draw

Tashkent's IT Park special economic zone is the mechanism behind most of the country's tech-sector growth story: resident companies get corporate tax relief and simplified customs treatment, and the zone has grown past **1,000 resident firms**, a figure worth treating as a directional growth signal rather than a hard headcount you can verify against a public registry on any given day. If you are relocating for a specific employer rather than to found something, confirm your prospective employer's actual IT Park resident status directly — the tax and visa benefits attach to the company's registration, not to the industry generally.

## What rent and daily costs look like

Tashkent remains genuinely cheap by European or even most Asian relocation-hub standards. A one-bedroom in **Yunusabad**, one of the more expat-oriented northern districts with decent international-school access, typically runs **USD 120–200** a month, and comparable units in other central districts land in a similar band. Eating out, taxis (Yandex Go dominates), and utilities all sit well under Western European costs, which is the entire pitch for people testing Central Asia as a low-burn base — but budget separately for a health insurance policy that actually covers evacuation, since the local private hospital network, while improving, is not yet at the depth you'd find in Almaty or Tbilisi for complex care.

## The practical friction points

English is not widely spoken outside IT Park and a handful of central-Tashkent business venues — Russian is the more useful second language for daily logistics, banking, and dealing with a landlord or a utility office. Registering your address with local authorities (propiska-style registration, a holdover administrative habit from the Soviet system) is still a real requirement for anyone on a residence-track visa, not a formality to skip, and landlords who are unfamiliar with renting to foreigners can slow this down more than the visa process itself. Banking access for foreigners has improved but international card acceptance outside central Tashkent is patchy — carry cash and set up a local account early rather than relying on a foreign card for daily spending.
