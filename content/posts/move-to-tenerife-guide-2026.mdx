**Move to Tenerife 2026** means living under Spanish national immigration law while paying island prices and island taxes. The legal framework is identical to the mainland — the same **Non-Lucrative Visa**, the same **Digital Nomad Visa**, the same NIE/TIE bureaucracy — but daily life is shaped by the Canary Islands' separate tax regime and a rental market that has heated up faster than almost anywhere else in Spain.

## Visa routes that actually fit Tenerife

Most non-EU arrivals choose between two national visas, and there is no Tenerife-specific or Canary-specific route. The **Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV)** is aimed at people living off savings or passive income: it requires proof of roughly **€28,800 per year** for a solo applicant (400% of Spain's IPREM index, plus more for dependents), and it explicitly bans any work, remote or local, for a Spain-based or foreign employer. It suits retirees and financially independent movers who don't need to earn while in Spain. The **Digital Nomad Visa**, by contrast, is built for people who keep working for non-Spanish employers or clients while living on the islands, and comes with a notably favorable tax regime for qualifying applicants in their first years. Full mechanics — income documentation, minimum contract length, and the work ban specifics — are covered in the dedicated [Spain non-lucrative visa](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/spain-non-lucrative-visa-guide-2026) and [Spain digital nomad visa](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/spain-digital-nomad-visa) guides. Whichever route you take, arriving with a visa is only step one: you still need to register for your **NIE** (foreigner identification number) and then convert to a **TIE** residence card in person, and island administrative offices are not known for speed. Budget weeks, not a weekend, for this part, and expect to make at least one follow-up trip to a Extranjería office.

## Rent, budgets, and the IGIC advantage

Housing costs vary sharply by where on the island you land. Studios in **Santa Cruz de Tenerife**, the island's working capital and a far less touristy base than the south coast, often start near **€500 a month**. In the tourist belt around **Costa Adeje**, where most short-term rental competition and English-speaking amenities are concentrated, one-bedroom apartments more typically start from **€800 a month** and climb quickly from there in peak season. For a single person, a genuinely comfortable monthly budget — rent, a car, groceries, and the occasional restaurant meal — tends to land around **€2,200–3,000**. It's possible to live on **€1,500–2,000** if you skip car ownership and rely on the island's bus network, and cook at home most nights. Context matters here: local average pay across the Canaries sits around only **€1,200 a month**, which is worth keeping in mind both for your own budgeting sanity check and for understanding why your spending as an incoming expat can look conspicuously high to Canarian neighbors on local wages. Families should plan differently — a non-housing monthly basket for a family of four is often cited around **€2,886** on Tenerife, and slightly higher, near **€3,008**, on Gran Canaria. One genuine advantage of island life is tax: the Canaries run their own indirect tax, **IGIC**, at a standard rate of **7%**, compared with the mainland's **21% IVA**. That difference quietly softens the cost of everything from electronics to restaurant meals to professional services.

## Housing market heat and schools

The rental and sale market has moved fast enough that older cost-of-living guides are now unreliable. Canary property prices rose roughly **15.4% year-on-year through 2025**, among the steepest increases anywhere in Spain, and rents climbed by around **12.8%** over the same period. If you're working from a quote or listing you saw even a year ago, assume it no longer reflects what's actually available — reverify current asking prices before committing to a budget. For families relocating with children, international schooling is a major line item: fees commonly start around **€3,500 a year** at primary level and can rise to **€8,000 a year** or more for older students at established international schools, on top of enrollment and material fees that many schools charge separately.
