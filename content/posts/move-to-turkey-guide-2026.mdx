Turkey draws two very different kinds of movers: people chasing a fast route to a second passport through real estate, and people who just want an affordable, sunny base with a genuinely low cost of living. Both paths run through the same national immigration system, but the eligibility bar, paperwork, and monthly budget realities differ enormously depending on which one you're pursuing.

## Citizenship by investment versus ordinary residence

Turkey's **citizenship-by-investment (CBI)** program is the fastest route to a Turkish passport available to foreign nationals, and it's built around a **$400,000** real estate purchase that must be held for a minimum of **three years** before the property can be sold. The property has to be appraised at or above that threshold by a government-approved appraiser, and the application still requires standard due diligence, translated and apostilled documents, and processing time measured in months rather than weeks — treat any promise of an instant passport with skepticism and verify current thresholds directly on Turkey's official investment portal before wiring any money, since program rules and required amounts have shifted more than once in recent years. For the much larger group of people who aren't pursuing citizenship, Turkey's short-term residence permit is the standard route: it requires proof of sufficient means to support yourself, with planning conversations commonly modeling an income threshold in the range of roughly **₺9,000 a month**, though the exact figure is set and periodically revised by Turkish immigration authorities, so confirm the current number before you file rather than relying on last year's guide. Whichever pathway you take, build a document matrix — issue dates, apostille status, expiry windows — early, since a single expired or improperly certified document can cost you a month of rework.

## Real costs: Istanbul, Antalya, and the lira problem

Turkey's cost of living remains genuinely low by Western European or North American standards, but budgeting only in lira is a mistake given how volatile the currency has been. In Istanbul's expat-friendly neighborhoods, a one-bedroom apartment typically runs **₺25,000–45,000 a month**, while Antalya, the Mediterranean coastal city popular with retirees and remote workers, tends to run cheaper, often **₺15,000–25,000** for a comparable unit. Because the lira has swung sharply against major currencies in recent years, it's far safer to think in USD or EUR terms and re-price your Turkish rent and expenses in hard currency every few months rather than assuming a lira figure from six months ago still holds. Build three budget scenarios — lean, realistic, and stress-case — and keep a contingency of **15–25%** on top, since both currency swings and unexpected renewal fees are common enough that a thin budget gets blown quickly. ## Healthcare, banking, and settling in

Private health insurance is worth budgeting for even if you qualify for the public system, since English-language service and appointment speed are noticeably better at private hospitals in Istanbul, Antalya, and Izmir. Opening a Turkish bank account as a foreigner generally requires your residence permit or an active application receipt, plus a tax number obtainable from the local tax office in a same-day visit in most cities — do this early, since rent payments, utility setup, and even SIM card registration often assume a local account or at least a local tax number. Landlords commonly ask for several months' rent upfront or a local guarantor, so factor that into your arrival cash reserve rather than assuming a standard one-month deposit will be enough everywhere.

## A 90-day sequencing plan

Use the first 90 days to lock legal status before optimizing lifestyle. In the first month, prioritize your visa or CBI application filing, secure short-term housing so you're not signing a long lease sight unseen, and get your tax number. In the second month, convert to a longer-term lease once you know the neighborhood you actually want, open your bank account, and register with a health insurer. By month three, you should have your residence permit card in hand (or CBI application progressing with your lawyer), a stable housing situation, and a realistic hard-currency monthly budget based on actual spending rather than guidebook estimates.
