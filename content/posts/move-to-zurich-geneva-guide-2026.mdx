Switzerland doesn't ration non-EU work permits by demand — it rations them by a fixed annual number set in Bern, and that number has stayed flat for 2026. The federal quota is **4,000 L permits** (short-term, under a year) and **4,500 B permits** (initial residence, generally renewable annually) for third-country nationals, unchanged from 2025. As of last September roughly half of both pools were still unclaimed, plus leftover unused allocations carried from earlier years — but quotas are split among cantons and released in batches through the year, so "half the annual number is free" nationally says nothing about whether Zürich's or Geneva's slice still has room in the month you need it.

## The quota is the real gate, not your résumé

A Swiss job offer for a non-EU/EFTA national is not a permit. Your prospective employer applies to the canton for a quota slot and a permit on your behalf, and if the canton's allocation for that period is exhausted, the process stalls regardless of how strong your offer is — this is the single most common thing relocation guides gloss over. EU/EFTA nationals bypass this entirely under free movement rules and only need to register after arrival, which is the practical reason Swiss tech and finance recruiters lean so heavily on EU hires. If you're outside the EU/EFTA, ask your employer directly, before signing anything, whether they've confirmed quota availability with the canton — a verbal "we'll sort the visa" is not the same as a secured slot.

## Zürich versus Geneva rent, city by city

Zürich one-bedrooms (2.5-room flats in Swiss listing convention) run roughly **CHF 1,900-2,100** in asking rent, climbing to **CHF 2,700-3,200** for a 3.5-room family-sized unit. Geneva runs close behind at **CHF 1,850-2,050** for a one-bedroom and **CHF 2,600-3,000** for larger units — the two cities trade places depending on the specific listing pool in a given month, and both sit roughly 30-50% above the Swiss national average. These are advertised rents before Nebenkosten (utilities and building-service charges), which commonly add another **CHF 150-300** a month — budget for the all-in figure, not the headline listing price. Lausanne, an hour from Geneva by train, is meaningfully cheaper — roughly CHF 1,450-1,650 for a one-bedroom — and is a genuine option for anyone not tied to a specific employer's office days.

## Two different expat cities wearing one passport

Zürich runs on German and on finance and engineering money — banks, insurers, and a real tech cluster cluster near the lake and along the Limmat, and English gets you further in an office than it does at the Coop checkout. Geneva runs on French and on the UN, WHO, WTO, and the broader international-organization and diplomatic ecosystem, plus private banking and watchmaking-adjacent trade; you'll hear more English day to day through the IO community, but French still runs the landlord conversations and the school system. Pick the city for the language and industry fit first — the rent gap between them is a rounding error next to the difference in daily life.

## What a Swiss salary has to cover

Recruiter-cited bands put Zürich tech and finance offers commonly in the **CHF 100,000-180,000** range depending on seniority, with Geneva's international-organization and legal roles landing somewhat lower on average but with different tax treatment for UN-affiliated staff. Whatever the headline number, mandatory Swiss health insurance (private, not payroll-deducted the way it works in most of the EU) runs several hundred francs a month per adult and is a legal requirement within three months of arrival, not an optional add-on — budget it as a fixed cost alongside rent, not as an afterthought once the apartment is sorted.
