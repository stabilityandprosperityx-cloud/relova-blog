Moving a pet internationally is fundamentally a sequencing problem — miss one step's timing window and the whole plan can slip by months, not days. The order that trips people up most: a pet needs an ISO-compliant 15-digit microchip implanted *before* the rabies vaccination is administered (a rabies shot given before the chip often doesn't count toward official timelines), and most destinations then require a minimum wait after that rabies vaccination before travel or before a rabies antibody titer test can even be drawn.

## The EU route

Entering the EU (or moving between most EU states with a pet) runs on the EU Animal Health Certificate system: microchip first, rabies vaccination second, then a 21-day wait from the date of vaccination before the pet can travel — this applies whether it's a primary vaccination or a lapsed booster being restarted. The certificate itself must be issued by an accredited vet within 10 days of travel and is valid for onward EU travel for four months. Departure countries with rabies-free or controlled status (like the UK, which left the EU pet passport scheme after Brexit) follow a different, generally faster process — confirm your specific departure country's category before booking flights, since a certificate issued under the wrong category gets rejected at the border.

## UK, Australia, and Japan each add their own layer

The UK requires dogs to receive tapeworm (Echinococcus) treatment from a vet 24–120 hours before arrival, a narrow window that catches people out constantly since it must be recorded on the pet's health certificate with exact treatment time and product name. Australia is the strictest major destination on this list: pets typically face a mandatory quarantine period (commonly cited around 10 days at the government-run Mickleham facility near Melbourne, though this varies by pet's origin-country risk category) with costs frequently exceeding AUD 2,000, plus a lengthy pre-export protocol of blood tests and waiting periods that can take months to complete before the flight is even booked. Japan runs on titer-based timelines managed by its Animal Quarantine Service (equivalent to MAFF import rules) — a rabies antibody titer test must show a passing result, followed by a waiting period that can run up to 180 days if the process isn't started early or a step is delayed, making Japan one of the longest lead-time destinations for pet relocation globally. Airlines add their own constraint on top of all this: in-cabin pet allowances are commonly capped around 8 kg including the carrier, pushing larger dogs into cargo or specialist pet-relocation services regardless of what the destination country's rules require.

## Booking the flight itself

Not every airline carries pets, and among those that do, policies on cabin weight limits, approved carrier dimensions, and which routes even allow pet transport at all vary enough that it's worth confirming directly with the airline rather than assuming a general international pet-travel guide applies. Brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds like bulldogs and pugs face additional restrictions or outright bans on many carriers due to breathing-related health risks at altitude, and summer heat embargoes are common for cargo-hold pet transport on routes through hot climates. Specialist pet relocation companies (PetRelocation, Jetpets, and similar) exist specifically to manage this multi-country paperwork chain end to end, and for anyone moving a pet through a high-friction destination like Australia or Japan, the fee is often worth paying simply to avoid a documentation error that costs weeks of delay or, worse, a pet being denied boarding.

## Building the actual timeline

Because so many of these steps have mandatory waiting periods that can't be compressed no matter how much money is thrown at the problem, the single biggest mistake people make is starting pet paperwork at the same time as the rest of the move rather than months ahead of it. A reasonable rule of thumb: start the microchip-and-rabies sequence at least four months before a planned departure for EU-bound moves, and six months or more for Australia or Japan given their quarantine and titer-testing timelines. Building a simple checklist — chip date, vaccination date, titer test date (if required), certificate issue window, and the airline booking — with each step's earliest and latest possible date keeps the paperwork chain from becoming the thing that delays an otherwise-ready move.
