Bali has spent the better part of a decade as Southeast Asia's default digital nomad magnet, and for good reason — reliable enough infrastructure, a genuinely low cost of living relative to a Western salary, and an established expat and remote-work community that makes the first few months easier than in most emerging nomad destinations. It's not the right fit for everyone, but for remote workers whose income doesn't depend on being in a specific timezone or city, it remains one of the easiest places in the world to test out a long-term move.

## Visa routes: E33G, Second Home, and the B211A

Indonesia doesn't have a single obvious "Bali visa" — like most digital nomad destinations, the pathway you choose depends on your income and how long you intend to stay. The **E33G** remote-worker visa route is the closest thing Indonesia has to a purpose-built nomad visa, and planning discussions around it commonly cite an income requirement in the range of **$2,000 a month**, though you should verify the current published threshold and required proof of income directly on Indonesia's official immigration portal before applying, since digital nomad visa programs across the region have a track record of adjusting requirements. For people planning a longer horizon or bringing more capital, Indonesia's **Second Home visa** offers a longer-term residence option tied to a financial deposit or investment rather than employment, and is worth investigating if a multi-year stay is the goal rather than a testing period. For shorter, more exploratory stays, the **B211A** visit visa is the most commonly used entry tool: it typically starts around **60 days** and can be extended through fairly standard workflows, making it a reasonable choice for people who want to spend a season in Bali before committing to a longer legal pathway. Whichever route you pick, build a document matrix — issue dates, apostille status, expiry windows — early on, since Indonesian immigration processes reward organized paperwork and punish sloppy timelines.

## What it costs to live comfortably

Nomad monthly budgets in Bali, covering rent, food, transport, coworking, and insurance, commonly land in the **$1,500–2,500** range for a comfortable, non-frugal lifestyle, though it's genuinely possible to spend less if you're not renting a private villa. Rent is the biggest variable and depends heavily on which part of the island you choose — a decision covered in far more depth in the dedicated [Canggu vs Ubud vs Seminyak guide](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/canggu-vs-ubud-vs-seminyak-bali-guide-2026), which breaks down the character and cost differences between Bali's main expat hubs. As a general planning anchor, treat your first budget estimate as provisional: build lean, realistic, and stress-case versions, and keep a **15–25%** contingency on top, since currency conversion timing and unexpected visa-related costs are common enough on a first move to Bali that a thin budget gets stretched quickly.

## Internet, healthcare, and who Bali actually suits

Internet quality on the island has genuinely improved over the past several years, with fiber increasingly common in the main expat areas, but consistency still varies block by block — it's worth testing actual speeds at the specific property you're considering before signing a longer lease, rather than trusting a villa listing's advertised connection. Private healthcare in the main hubs is generally reliable for routine and even moderately serious issues, though anything requiring advanced specialist care often means a medical evacuation to Singapore or Australia, so travel insurance with real evacuation coverage is worth budgeting for rather than skipping. Bali suits remote workers with genuine location independence, a tolerance for occasional bureaucratic friction, and realistic expectations about internet and infrastructure reliability outside the main hubs — it's a considerably better fit for someone testing a nomad lifestyle for a few months than for someone who needs guaranteed enterprise-grade uptime every single day.
