Before you sign a lease abroad, the conversation that actually determines whether your move works is the one with your employer — not your landlord. Getting formal sign-off to work remotely from another country protects both you and the company from a surprisingly technical risk: permanent establishment.

## Why your employer's lawyers care where you sit

Permanent establishment (PE) is a tax concept that determines whether your presence in a foreign country creates a taxable business footprint for your employer there — and it's the single biggest reason companies hesitate to formally approve cross-border remote work, even when they're happy with your performance. PE risk generally rises when an employee has authority to sign contracts on the company's behalf, or maintains a long, fixed presence in one location that starts to look like a de facto branch office rather than a remote employee working from home. Some countries are known among corporate tax and immigration teams as stricter PE environments — Germany and France are commonly cited in this category, meaning employers are more cautious about approving open-ended remote arrangements there, sometimes requiring additional legal review or an Employer of Record (EOR) structure before saying yes. Other jurisdictions are generally modeled as lower-friction from a PE standpoint, with Georgia and the UAE frequently mentioned in this context, partly due to their tax treaty structures and generally business-friendly regulatory posture — though "lower friction" doesn't mean zero risk, and your company's legal team, not general online guidance, should make the final call for your specific role and seniority.

## Framing the ask to your employer

When you raise this with HR or your manager, come prepared with more than a request — bring a proposal that addresses their actual concerns. Employers evaluating a remote-work-abroad request are typically weighing three things: tax and PE exposure, payroll and benefits compliance in the new country, and whether your role specifically requires contract-signing authority or client-facing responsibilities that would increase PE risk. If your role doesn't involve signing contracts or maintaining a fixed client-facing presence, say so explicitly — it directly addresses the biggest risk factor companies screen for. It also helps to research whether your company already uses an Employer of Record service, since many mid-size and large employers use EOR providers like Deel or Remote (commonly priced around **$599 a month** per employee) or Oyster (around **$499 a month**) to legally employ staff in countries where they have no formal entity — proposing this route, rather than asking the company to set up a costly local subsidiary, can turn a hard "no" into a workable "yes."

## Budgeting for the transition period

Even with employer approval in hand, plan your own finances with the same discipline you'd apply to a full relocation: build lean, realistic, and stress-case budget scenarios, and keep a **15–25%** contingency on top for the inevitable gap between your last paycheck under the old arrangement and your first payment under whatever new structure — EOR, direct foreign payroll, or contractor conversion — your employer settles on. This transition period is also when negotiation surveys and benchmarking data are most useful: recent remote-work compensation surveys are worth reviewing to understand what other companies are offering for similar cross-border arrangements, since that data gives you leverage in your own negotiation rather than having to guess what's reasonable to ask for.

## Sequencing the conversation with your relocation timeline

Don't treat the employer conversation as a formality to squeeze in after you've already committed to a move — sequence it first. Getting written approval, ideally with specifics on payroll structure and any role adjustments, protects you from a scenario where you've signed a lease and shipped belongings only to learn your employer's legal team won't approve the arrangement.
