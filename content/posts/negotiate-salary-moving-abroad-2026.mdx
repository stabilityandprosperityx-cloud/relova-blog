Negotiating salary for a move abroad is not a single-number conversation — it's several overlapping ones: cost-of-living adjustment, currency risk, tax equalization if you're American, and whether your employer even structures pay by location at all. Treating these as one blended "moving bump" is the most common mistake people make, and it usually leaves money on the table.

## COLA, geo-tiers, and what to ask outright

**Cost of Living Adjustment (COLA)** offsets city-to-city gaps—but ask whether it is **reviewed annually** or locked once. A fixed COLA erodes fast in high-inflation markets. Large employers often use **geo-tiers** that swing pay **5–30%** by location; remote-first firms more often use one rate. Ask directly: *“How does your compensation structure account for remote employee location?”* Separating **COLA** from a **promotion increase** gives you two negotiation tracks instead of one blended “move bump.”

## Currency risk and U.S. tax equalization

If salary is in one currency and rent in another, FX moves matter—**GBP/USD** swung from about **1.55 to 1.07 and back toward 1.26** across 2015–2025. Ask about **currency protection** or splitting pay across currencies. U.S. citizens remain taxed on worldwide income; request **tax equalization** help as an explicit package item, not a hope.

## Non-cash items that beat a small raise

Visa support, **3–6 months** temporary housing, dual-country tax filing help, and relocation costs paid **directly by the company** (not as a taxable cash bonus) often outweigh a thin percentage raise. Put them in writing beside base salary.

## A practical sequence for the conversation

Walking into a relocation salary negotiation with a clear sequence beats winging it. Start by gathering hard data: compare your current and destination cities using **Numbeo** or a similar cost index, and go in with a specific dollar or percentage gap in mind rather than a vague sense that "it's more expensive there." Next, confirm how your company's compensation model actually works — ask directly whether they use geo-tiers or pay a single location-agnostic rate, since this single answer determines which arguments will land and which won't. From there, keep your negotiation components separate rather than blended: treat any COLA adjustment as its own line item, distinct from a promotion or performance-based raise, so that if one changes later the other isn't automatically affected. Address currency risk explicitly by asking whether the company offers currency protection or the option to split pay across currencies, particularly important if your rent and daily expenses will be in a currency different from your salary currency. Push for the non-cash benefits — visa sponsorship, temporary housing, dual-country tax preparation help, and company-paid (not taxable cash) relocation costs — since these often carry more real value than a marginal percentage increase in base pay. Finally, get clarity on how any COLA adjustment will be reviewed going forward: an annual reset that tracks inflation and exchange rates is worth considerably more over time than a one-time fixed amount that quietly erodes in a high-inflation destination.

## Timing the ask and getting it in writing

When you raise this negotiation matters almost as much as what you ask for. The strongest leverage point is typically before you've signed a lease or committed to a specific destination — once your employer knows you're already financially and logistically committed to a move, some of your negotiating power evaporates. If the relocation is tied to an internal transfer or a new role at your current company, treat it as a distinct negotiation from your existing compensation review cycle, since blending the two often means the relocation-specific asks get lost in a broader annual raise conversation that was never designed to cover moving costs, visa sponsorship, or currency exposure. Whatever you agree to, get every element in writing — the COLA figure and its review schedule, the currency arrangement, the non-cash benefits and their dollar-equivalent value — in an actual amended offer letter or relocation agreement, not just a verbal understanding from a call with your manager. Verbal agreements about relocation packages have a way of quietly disappearing when a new manager takes over or when the company's relocation policy changes, and a written record is the only real protection against that.
