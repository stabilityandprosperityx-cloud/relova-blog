Becoming a Dutch citizen is a genuinely long-horizon project — five years minimum of lawful residence, a real language exam, and a naturalization process that municipalities process at noticeably different speeds. It's also one of the more restrictive countries in Western Europe on dual nationality, which shapes the decision for many long-term residents as much as the timeline does.

## The residence and integration requirements

Dutch naturalization requires **five years of continuous, lawful residence** in the Netherlands as the baseline eligibility window — interruptions or gaps in your residence permit history can reset or complicate this clock, so keeping your permit renewals current and gap-free matters more than people expect going in. Alongside the residence requirement, applicants must pass Dutch integration exams demonstrating at least **B1-level Dutch**, delivered through the **inburgering** (civic integration) system. Preparing for this through a private course, including the required exam components, commonly costs around **€225** per exam sitting for the components themselves, though full inburgering course packages — especially for people who need to build Dutch from scratch rather than brush up existing skills — can run considerably higher, sometimes **€5,000–10,000** for a comprehensive private course when no subsidy applies. It's worth checking whether you qualify for municipal integration subsidies, since eligibility and support programs vary and can meaningfully offset this cost.

## Fees, processing time, and what to expect from the IND

The formal naturalization application itself carries a government fee, cited in recent **IND** (Dutch Immigration and Naturalisation Service) fee schedules around **€188** — though fees are periodically revised, so confirm the current published rate on the IND's official site before you file rather than budgeting off a prior year's number. Processing time is one of the more variable parts of this process: it commonly stretches **12 to 18 months** in many municipalities, since the process involves both IND review and a municipal component, and different municipalities have historically processed applications at noticeably different speeds. Applicants planning a specific milestone — a job change, a family event, a passport renewal deadline — around their citizenship should build in slack for this range rather than assuming the shorter end will apply to them specifically.

## The dual citizenship question

This is the detail that changes the calculus for a lot of long-term residents: the Netherlands restricts dual citizenship more than many people expect going in. Most naturalization applicants are required to renounce their prior nationality as a condition of becoming Dutch, unless they fall under one of a limited set of exceptions — these include being a national of another EU member state in some circumstances, holding refugee status, being married to or in a registered partnership with a Dutch national in certain configurations, or a handful of other narrowly defined categories. Anyone for whom retaining their original citizenship matters — for property rights, family inheritance law, or simply personal identity — should research their specific exception eligibility carefully and, ideally, consult an immigration lawyer before committing to the naturalization track, since renunciation is not easily reversible.

## What Dutch citizenship actually buys you

The practical upside is real: a Dutch passport ranks among the world's most powerful for travel, offering visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to roughly **190 destinations**, along with full EU freedom of movement, work, and residence rights across the bloc. It's worth noting, separately, that the Netherlands' well-known **30% ruling** — a favorable tax regime for certain incoming skilled workers — helps with income tax during your working years in the country but does nothing to shorten the citizenship timeline itself; the two are entirely separate tracks, and conflating them is a common source of confusion for new arrivals.
