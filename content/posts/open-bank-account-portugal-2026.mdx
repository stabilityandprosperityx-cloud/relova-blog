Opening a [Portuguese](/blog/move-to-algarve-portugal-guide-2026) bank account as a foreigner is a genuinely well-trodden process by European standards, but it starts with a piece of paperwork that surprises a lot of newcomers: you need a tax number before a bank will even talk to you.

## The NIF comes first, always

Every bank in Portugal, without exception, requires a valid **NIF** (Número de Identificação Fiscal, Portugal's individual tax identification number) before opening any kind of account. This isn't a formality you can skip or work around — it's woven into how Portuguese financial institutions verify identity and report to tax authorities, so getting your NIF is genuinely the first task on your Portugal banking checklist, before you even start comparing bank options. Non-residents can obtain a NIF either in person at a local tax office (Finanças) or, more commonly for people not yet in the country, through a fiscal representative — a local person or firm who acts as your tax contact of record in Portugal, a service several relocation and legal firms offer specifically for this purpose. Once you have your NIF in hand, the actual account-opening process becomes far more straightforward.

## Where to actually open an account

Portugal's major traditional banks all handle non-resident and foreigner onboarding, though the experience varies in speed and English-language support. **Millennium BCP**, Portugal's largest private bank, is commonly cited as one of the more foreigner-friendly options, with decent English support in branches in Lisbon and Porto. **Novobanco** and **BPI** are similarly well-established options that many relocation guides and immigration lawyers routinely recommend for expats, particularly useful if you want a bank with a wide physical branch network for in-person banking. **ActivoBank**, Millennium's digital-first subsidiary, has become popular among younger expats and remote workers who want a mostly app-based banking experience without sacrificing the backing of an established Portuguese institution. Alongside the traditional options, many expats — especially those still in the process of establishing full residency — use **Revolut** or similar digital-first, multi-currency accounts as a bridge or complement to a local account, since these are typically faster and easier to open remotely, even though they don't fully replace a Portuguese IBAN for things like receiving a local salary or setting up recurring Portuguese direct debits.

## What it actually costs and what to expect as a non-resident

Monthly account fees at Portuguese banks typically run **€5–15**, though most banks waive this fee once you meet a minimum activity threshold — such as a set number of card transactions per month — or maintain a minimum account balance, so it's worth asking specifically about fee-waiver conditions rather than assuming the advertised fee is what you'll actually pay. Non-resident account opening is genuinely available at all the major banks named above, but expect the process to take longer and require more paperwork than resident account opening — proof of address (sometimes from your home country if you haven't secured Portuguese housing yet), your NIF, passport, and sometimes proof of income or employment. Some banks are notably faster for non-residents who show up in person with complete paperwork versus those who try to open remotely, so if you're able to handle the NIF and initial branch visit during a scouting trip before your full move, it often smooths the rest of the process considerably.

## Living with Multibanco and MB Way once you're set up

Once your account is active, day-to-day banking in Portugal becomes genuinely frictionless thanks to two interconnected systems. **Multibanco**, Portugal's near-universal ATM and payment network, lets you pay bills, top up mobile phones, buy event tickets, and withdraw cash from tens of thousands of machines nationwide — it's a much more versatile system than a typical ATM network elsewhere in Europe. **MB Way**, the linked mobile payment app, has become the dominant way Portuguese residents split bills, pay small businesses, and send money to each other instantly, and not having it can make you feel noticeably behind the curve socially, since many smaller vendors and even friends splitting a restaurant bill will default to asking for an MB Way transfer. Getting both set up shortly after opening your account is worth prioritizing, since together they cover the overwhelming majority of everyday payment needs in Portugal.
