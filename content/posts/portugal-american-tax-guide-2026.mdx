Moving to [Portugal](/blog/portugal-nhr-tax-regime-guide) as an American doesn't simplify your taxes — it doubles the paperwork, because U.S. citizens owe the IRS on worldwide income regardless of where they live, while Portugal separately taxes residents on their income under its own rules. The two systems interact in specific, mechanical ways that are worth understanding before you file rather than after.

## FEIE versus the Foreign Tax Credit

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion lets qualifying expats exclude roughly $130,000 of foreign-earned income from U.S. tax for the 2026 tax year (the figure is inflation-adjusted annually), provided you meet either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test (330 days outside the U.S. in a 12-month period). FEIE is straightforward and popular, but it only excludes earned income — wages and self-employment income — not passive income like dividends, rental income, or capital gains.

The Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) works differently: instead of excluding income, it credits U.S. tax dollar-for-dollar against income tax already paid to Portugal. For most Americans in Portugal, Portuguese tax rates on ordinary income are higher than U.S. federal rates once you're above modest income levels, which means the FTC frequently eliminates U.S. tax liability entirely and can even generate carryover credits for future years — often a better outcome than FEIE for higher earners, since FEIE caps out and doesn't help with passive income at all. The two elections aren't always compatible in the same return for the same income category, and switching between them has multi-year lock-in rules, so this is a genuine case where a qualified cross-border preparer earns their fee rather than a DIY approach.

## What the Portugal-U.S. tax treaty actually governs

The Portugal-U.S. double taxation treaty allocates taxing rights on specific income categories — pensions, dividends, interest, and capital gains — between the two countries, and generally exists to prevent the same income from being fully taxed twice. In practice it doesn't eliminate U.S. filing obligations (citizenship-based taxation means Americans file regardless), but it does provide relief mechanisms and specific sourcing rules that determine which country has primary taxing rights on a given pension payment or dividend stream. Private pensions and IRA/401(k) distributions, for instance, are generally treated differently from government pensions under most U.S. tax treaties, so the source of your retirement income matters for how the treaty applies.

## FBAR and FATCA: two separate reporting regimes

Two overlapping but distinct U.S. reporting requirements apply to anyone with foreign financial accounts. FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) triggers once the aggregate value of your foreign accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year — a low bar that catches most expats with even a modest Portuguese checking and savings account combined. It's filed separately from your tax return, directly with FinCEN, and penalties for willful non-filing are severe. FATCA reporting (IRS Form 8938, filed with your return) has higher thresholds that vary by filing status and residency — for expats filing jointly abroad, the threshold is substantially higher than FBAR's, but it captures a broader range of foreign financial assets, not just accounts. The two forms ask overlapping but not identical questions, and both are required if you cross their respective thresholds — filing one doesn't excuse the other.

## Social Security and the totalization agreement

The U.S.-Portugal totalization agreement prevents Americans working in Portugal (and Portuguese nationals working in the U.S.) from paying into both countries' social security systems simultaneously for the same work. For salaried employees typically posted to Portugal for a limited period, the agreement generally keeps them in the U.S. system; for those who become Portuguese tax residents long-term or who are self-employed, Portuguese Segurança Social contributions usually apply instead. The agreement also allows totalizing contribution years across both systems to help meet minimum eligibility thresholds for benefits in either country — useful if your career was split between the two.

## IFICI and how it layers onto U.S. obligations

Portugal's IFICI regime (the successor to the old NHR program) offers a flat 20% Portuguese tax rate on qualifying Portuguese-source employment and self-employment income for new arrivals in designated professions, plus favorable treatment of certain foreign-source income. For Americans, IFICI reduces Portuguese tax liability but does nothing to reduce U.S. tax exposure — the IRS doesn't recognize IFICI as a special category, so income taxed favorably under IFICI in Portugal is still reported at full value to the IRS, with the Foreign Tax Credit calculated based on the (lower) Portuguese tax actually paid. That combination — a low flat Portuguese rate plus a reduced FTC — can sometimes leave a small U.S. tax bill where a higher Portuguese rate would have generated enough credit to zero it out, so IFICI eligibility is worth modeling against your specific U.S. tax situation, not assumed to be a pure win.
