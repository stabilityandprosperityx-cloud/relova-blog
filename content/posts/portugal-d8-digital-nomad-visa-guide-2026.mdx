[Portugal's](/blog/move-to-madeira-portugal-guide-2026) D8 [digital nomad visa](/blog/portugal-d8-visa-application-step-by-step-2026) was built for remote workers and freelancers rather than retirees, and its eligibility rules reflect that: it's judged almost entirely on demonstrated recurring income from work performed outside [Portugal](/blog/nomad-visa-europe-comparison), not on savings alone or a fixed pension.

## Who the D8 is actually designed for

The D8 targets people earning a living remotely — salaried employees of foreign companies who can work from anywhere, freelancers with foreign clients, and business owners drawing income from a company registered abroad. It is explicitly not intended for people planning to work for a Portuguese employer or take local clients once resident; that's a different visa category. What matters for eligibility is that the income source is foreign and that you can document it with contracts, invoices, or pay stubs going back at least three months, alongside a letter from your employer or a portfolio of client agreements if you're self-employed.

## How the income threshold is calculated

[Portugal](/blog/athens-vs-lisbon-expat-guide-2026) recalculates its D8 income floor every year because it's pegged to a multiple of the national minimum wage, and the minimum wage itself rises annually. The visa requires evidence of income equal to four times the minimum wage — a formula that means the euro figure moves whenever the government adjusts wages, which it has done consistently each January in recent years. This is the single most important mechanic to understand about the D8: it is not a fixed number you can memorize once and reuse for years. Applicants are judged against whichever threshold is in force at the time their case is actually assessed — which can mean the figure quoted when you started researching the visa is out of date by the time you file, and out of date again by the time a consulate or AIMA official reviews your file. Always confirm the live figure on the government portal (SEF/AIMA's official channels or your consulate's published requirements) rather than relying on a number from an older article — this guide's companion piece on [applying step by step](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/portugal-d8-visa-application-step-by-step-2026) covers the exact current euro figure and how AIMA applies it based on your appointment date rather than your filing date.

Beyond the recurring monthly income test, most consulates also expect to see savings roughly equivalent to a year's worth of the minimum income threshold, held in an accessible account, as a buffer against income interruption during the transition.

## D8 versus D7: two different visas for two different income types

The D8 is frequently confused with the older D7 visa, but they're built for different financial profiles. The D7 — Portugal's original "passive income" residency visa — was designed for retirees and people living off pensions, dividends, rental income, or investment returns, and its income threshold is pegged to a single multiple of the minimum wage rather than the D8's higher multiple, making it accessible to a wider range of modest fixed incomes. The D8, by contrast, requires active but location-independent earned income and sets its bar meaningfully higher, on the theory that remote workers can typically command higher recurring pay than a retirement income floor.

In practice, the choice between them isn't really a choice — it's dictated by your income type. Someone living entirely off Social Security or a pension should apply under D7, since D8's employment-income documentation requirements don't fit passive income cleanly. Someone earning a salary from a remote job or running a location-independent freelance business should apply under D8, since D7 reviewers may question whether ongoing work income counts as "passive" for their purposes. Mixing income types — partial pension, partial freelance work — is possible but complicates which category you should file under, and is worth a consultation with an immigration lawyer rather than a guess.

## Processing timelines and what happens after approval

Consulate and VFS processing windows for the D8 are generally modeled between one and three months, though this varies meaningfully by consulate location and season — consulates in high-demand jurisdictions can run slower during peak application periods. Once approved, the D8 typically leads to an initial two-year residence permit, renewable, with a pathway to permanent residency or citizenship eligibility after five years of continuous legal residence — the same five-year clock that applies across most of Portugal's residency-based visas. What differs between D8 holders and other visa categories is mainly the ongoing documentation: because D8 status is tied to continued remote income, renewal applications generally expect to see that the income relationship (employer, client base) has remained active and above threshold, not just that you've maintained a Portuguese address.

Because both the income floor and the documentation AIMA expects change from year to year, build in a buffer — 15 to 25% above the stated minimum — when planning your finances for the application, since delays or an unfavorable year-over-year wage adjustment can otherwise put a marginal applicant below the line by the time their case is actually reviewed.
