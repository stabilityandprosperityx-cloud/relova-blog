Knowing you qualify for the D8 is one problem; actually executing the application without a refusal or an avoidable multi-month delay is another. From January 2026, the national minimum wage rose to €920/month, which pushes the D8 income floor (four times that figure) to €3,680/month — up from €3,480 the year before. The detail that catches people out: AIMA judges your finances against whichever threshold is in force on your appointment date, not your filing date, so someone who applied in 2025 under the old €3,480 floor still needs to show €3,680 if their AIMA appointment lands in 2026. For the underlying eligibility mechanics and how D8 differs from D7, see the [Portugal D8 guide](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/portugal-d8-digital-nomad-visa-guide-2026); you'll also need a [NIF](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/portugal-nif-number-guide-2026) and a [Portuguese bank account](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/open-bank-account-portugal-2026) before you can file.

## Step 1: Assemble your consulate file

File at the consulate or VFS Global center covering your country of residence. The core document set is: a valid passport, the completed application form, three months of income evidence at or above €3,680/month, proof of savings around €11,040 for a solo applicant (roughly twelve times the minimum wage), an apostilled and translated criminal record certificate, private health insurance valid in Portugal, and proof of Portuguese housing — a signed lease or a reservation agreement, ideally for a full year. Missing or thin documentation in any one of these categories is the single biggest cause of delay at this stage, so build the file as a complete package rather than filing partial and supplementing later.

## Step 2: Pay the consular fee

Consular fees run roughly €90–120 at filing. This is separate from, and much smaller than, the residence-card fee you'll pay later through AIMA.

## Step 3: Wait out consular processing

Expect 60–90 working days for the consulate to process and issue a decision. This window can run longer during peak filing seasons at busy consulates, so build slack into your planned move date rather than booking a one-way flight against the fastest-case timeline.

## Step 4: Receive and use the visa stamp

Approval produces a visa stamp valid for 120 days — this is your entry window into Portugal, not your actual residence permit. You must enter Portugal within that window; the stamp itself doesn't authorize indefinite stay.

## Step 5: Enter Portugal and prepare your AIMA file

Once in Portugal, your task shifts to assembling the file AIMA (Portugal's immigration agency, which replaced SEF) will need for your residence-card appointment — updated bank statements, your NIF, proof of address, and biometrics readiness.

## Step 6: Book your AIMA appointment immediately

Book the AIMA appointment as soon as you land — don't wait. Backlogs are real: current wait times commonly run 2–4 months from booking to appointment, which is actually an improvement over the 6-plus-month waits reported in 2024 as AIMA worked through its post-SEF transition backlog. Bring your biometrics and updated financial documents to the appointment itself, since bank statements from your original consular filing will likely be stale by the time you're seen.

## Step 7: Pay the residence-card fee

The residence-card fee runs approximately €170 following a fee schedule review in March 2026. Combined with the consular fee from Step 2, total government fees for one applicant land around €260–400 — before translations, apostilles, insurance, and housing costs, which add substantially more.

## Step 8: Receive your card by post

The physical residence card typically arrives by post within 2–12 weeks of your AIMA appointment. Until it arrives, keep your AIMA appointment confirmation and passport stamp as proof of legal status.

## Step 9: Understand your renewal and citizenship clock

Initial D8 residence is granted for two years, renewable for a further three years, with permanent residency or citizenship eligibility opening up after five years of continuous legal residence in Portugal. "Continuous" in practice means spending the majority of each year in the country — commonly benchmarked at 183-plus days annually — and never being absent for more than six consecutive months at a stretch, both of which AIMA can and does check at renewal.

## The refusal and delay triggers worth avoiding

A short lease is the most common self-inflicted refusal trigger: AIMA regularly rejects housing proof for leases under six months, and strongly prefers twelve-month agreements over anything shorter. A missing NISS (Portugal's social security number) is another frequent gap, as is income documentation that doesn't cleanly match your bank statements — if your invoices say one number and your deposits say another, expect questions. Travel insurance is not a substitute for residency-grade health cover; AIMA wants a policy built for residents, not tourists. And since August 2025, tenants have been able to register their own lease (Modelo 2) with Finanças if the landlord hasn't done so — worth doing proactively, since an unregistered lease can stall your file even if the lease itself is otherwise solid. Finally, a plane ticket alone is rarely accepted as proof of lawful entry into Portugal; keep your passport entry stamp and visa documentation together as your primary evidence.
