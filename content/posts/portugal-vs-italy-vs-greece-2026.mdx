Southern Europe's three big relocation draws get lumped together constantly — sun, sea, lower cost of living than Northern Europe — but their actual entry mechanics, tax treatment, and bureaucratic culture diverge sharply enough that picking based on climate alone is a mistake.

## The income math is not remotely comparable

Portugal's D7 passive-income visa sets its floor against the national minimum wage, which puts 2026's requirement around **EUR 920 a month** for a single applicant — the lowest of the three by a wide margin, though it is a *passive*-income test (pensions, dividends, rental income), not built for active remote workers. Italy's digital nomad visa, launched specifically for remote employees and freelancers, asks for **EUR 28,000 a year** (about EUR 2,333 a month), roughly two-and-a-half times Portugal's floor, with the requirement rising to around EUR 33,600 for someone bringing a spouse. Greece's digital nomad visa sets the highest bar of the three at **EUR 3,500 a month net** income, climbing another 20% for a spouse and 15% per child — meaning a couple with one child needs close to EUR 4,800 a month just to clear the threshold, well above what either Portugal or Italy demands.

## Tax regimes: all three had a "golden window" that closed or narrowed

Portugal's classic NHR (Non-Habitual Resident) regime, which once gave arriving expats a flat, low rate on much foreign income, closed to new applicants on **1 January 2024**. Its replacement, IFICI, is narrower — aimed at specific high-skill professions in tech, science, and executive roles — and does not extend blanket benefits to retirees or general remote workers the way old NHR did. Italy runs a separate flat-tax regime for new tax residents (a fixed annual substitute tax rather than a rate on worldwide income) that remains attractive but has been tightened and made costlier in recent budget cycles, so its exact terms need re-confirming with a tax advisor at the time you'd actually relocate. Greece offers its own reduced-rate regime for new tax residents in certain circumstances, but eligibility and duration details are program-specific enough that no single number is safe to repeat without checking the year's actual Greek tax code.

## Bureaucracy: reputation matches reality more than you'd hope

Anecdotally and structurally, Italy's immigration and residency bureaucracy (the Questura system for permits, notoriously long queues for the permesso di soggiorno) has one of the worst reputations in Western Europe for pure administrative friction, independent of which visa route you hold. Portugal's AIMA, the agency that replaced SEF in 2023, has improved processing somewhat but still runs residence-card waits commonly cited in the several-months range, with Lisbon itself typically slower than smaller regional offices. Greece's system has a reputation for improving faster than the other two when you engage a bilingual local lawyer specifically experienced with the digital nomad visa category — DIY applicants report more friction than those working through experienced local counsel, more so than in either Portugal or Italy.

## Citizenship timelines: this is where the gap is starkest

Portugal offers the fastest naturalization clock of the three at **five years** of legal residence plus a comparatively light **A2**-level Portuguese exam. Greece requires **seven years** of legal residence for standard naturalization, with a more demanding language and civics requirement. Italy sets the longest bar at **ten years** of legal residence for standard naturalization — one of the longest timelines in the EU — though marriage to an Italian citizen or Italian ancestry (jure sanguinis) can create an entirely different, much faster path that has nothing to do with the residence-based route described here.
