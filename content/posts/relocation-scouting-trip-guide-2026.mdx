A scouting trip is not a vacation with extra spreadsheets — it's a structured test of whether a place you like as a tourist also works as a place you'd do laundry, see a doctor, and commute from. Treating it as a checklist rather than a holiday is what separates people who move once and stay from people who move, discover a dealbreaker in month three, and move again.

## How long to stay, and why a week isn't enough

A single week almost never surfaces the problems that matter: you'll have seen the highlights and eaten well, but you won't have hit a public holiday that shuts down every government office, dealt with a delivery that never arrives, or noticed that your prospective neighborhood goes dead quiet — or uncomfortably loud — on a weeknight. **Two to four weeks** is the range that consistently works better: long enough to fall into a working routine (if you're remote, actually try working from a local café or coworking space, not just your hotel), short enough to keep costs contained and fit around a job or existing lease back home. If you're choosing between two or three candidate cities, split the time rather than doing separate trips months apart — memory of the first city fades fast, and side-by-side comparison while impressions are fresh is far more useful.

## What to actually test while you're there

Move past restaurants and landmarks and run a short list of concrete tests: ride the public transit system during actual rush hour, not at noon; visit a grocery store and price a normal weekly shop, not a tourist-district market; find and walk into a pharmacy and see how the language barrier plays out; if you have kids, visit the school you're considering during a normal class day, not an open house. Talk to the expat or immigrant community that already lives there — local Facebook groups, Meetup events, or coworking spaces are the fastest way to get honest answers about a landlord's reputation, which neighborhoods to avoid, or which visa lawyer actually returns calls, none of which shows up in a guidebook.

## Budgeting the trip itself

A realistic scouting-trip budget for two to four weeks, covering flights, mid-range lodging, local transport, and meals, commonly runs **USD 2,000–5,000** depending on the destination and how much of it you spend eating out versus cooking. Lodging is the biggest lever: booking a month-long stay through Airbnb or a local long-term rental site typically unlocks a 20–40% discount versus nightly rates, and services like TrustedHousesitters — trading pet or house sitting for free accommodation — can cut lodging costs close to zero in cities with an active sitter community, at the cost of being tied to someone else's schedule and address.

## Turning the trip into a decision, not just an impression

Come home with more than a gut feeling: keep a running note of rent listings you actually toured (not just browsed online), the commute times you measured yourself, and the two or three things that surprised you, good or bad. People who skip a scouting trip entirely and move sight-unseen report meaningfully higher regret rates in relocation surveys — not because the destination was wrong, but because the mismatch between the imagined version of the place and the lived version only shows up once you're already there with a signed lease.

## A simple day-by-day structure to steal

A workable default: spend the first three or four days getting oriented without an agenda — walk, ride transit at random, find your bearings. Spend the middle stretch, the bulk of the trip, living close to a normal routine in your top one or two candidate neighborhoods, working from local cafés or a coworking space if that's part of your plan, cooking at least a few meals from local groceries, and touring three to five real rental listings rather than just browsing online. Reserve the last two or three days for logistics — a visa consultation if one's available locally, a visit to a bank branch to understand account-opening requirements, and a final walk through your top-choice neighborhood at a different time of day than you first saw it, since morning-quiet streets and Friday-night streets are sometimes different places entirely.
