Retiring abroad is less a single decision than a stack of four separate ones — pension logistics, healthcare, tax residency, and visa category — that most people research in the wrong order. This is a planning framework, not a country-by-country guide; for specific destinations, see our dedicated retire-to-Portugal, retire-to-Spain, and retire-to-Costa-Rica guides.

## Start with pension portability, not the destination

Before picking a country, confirm whether your pension actually follows you there in full. US Social Security is paid to beneficiaries in most countries worldwide, but the Social Security Administration maintains a specific list of countries (including Cuba and North Korea, plus a handful of others depending on treaty status) where payments are restricted or require special arrangements — check the SSA's country payment list directly rather than assuming universal portability. UK State Pension is also paid globally, but the crucial catch is "uprating": the annual cost-of-living increase applies automatically only in the UK, the EU/EEA, and countries with a specific reciprocal social security agreement (the US, several Caribbean nations, and a few others) — retire somewhere without that agreement (much of Southeast Asia, Australia, Canada) and your pension gets frozen at whatever rate it was when you left, never rising with inflation. This single fact has derailed more UK [retirement](/blog/retire-to-lisbon-guide-2026) plans than any visa requirement.

## Healthcare: the coverage gap Medicare doesn't fill

US Medicare generally does not cover routine or emergency care outside the United States, with narrow exceptions for specific border-area situations. That means anyone retiring abroad on Medicare needs a genuine standalone plan: either private international health insurance (commonly running USD 200-500/month for a healthy retiree in their 60s-70s, scaling up with age and pre-existing conditions) or enrollment in the destination country's public system where residency-based access is available (Portugal's SNS and Spain's public system both allow paying-in access for legal residents, for example). Research this before, not after, choosing a visa category — some retirement visas require proof of private health coverage as a hard condition of approval, and buying a policy that explicitly names your destination country matters, since many US-based travel policies exclude long-stay or residency-based claims.

## Tax residency: the part people research last and regret first

Where you become a tax resident — typically triggered by spending more than 183 days a year in a country, though rules vary — determines whether your pension, investment income, and any US-source earnings get taxed twice, once, or with credits offsetting the overlap. The US Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) lets US citizens exclude a set amount of foreign-earned income from US tax each year, commonly cited near USD 130,000 for 2026 filings (the figure adjusts annually for inflation, so confirm the current-year number with the IRS before filing) — but critically, the FEIE applies to earned income, not pensions or Social Security, which remain taxable by the US regardless of residency. Bilateral tax treaties between the US or UK and your destination country determine whether pension income gets taxed at source, at destination, or both with a credit; this is genuinely worth a paid hour with a cross-border accountant before you commit to a country, not after.

## Match the visa category to your actual finances, across scenarios

Most countries offer at least one retirement-oriented visa route (Portugal's D7, Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa, Costa Rica's Pensionado program, Panama's Pensionado), each with different minimum income floors, asset-proof requirements, and renewal cycles. Build your budget across at least two or three destination scenarios before committing — a route that clears the income floor easily in one country might be the tightest-margin option in another once local cost of living, required health insurance, and visa renewal fees are stacked against the same pension income. Treat the visa's stated minimum income as a legal floor, not a livable budget; plan your actual monthly spend at 1.3-1.5x that minimum for a realistic cushion.
