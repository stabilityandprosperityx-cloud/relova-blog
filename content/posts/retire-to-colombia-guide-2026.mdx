Colombia's retirement visa system is built around a mechanic that's easy to misread if you're working from an older article: both the [Pensionado](/blog/medellin-vs-bogota-vs-cartagena-guide-2026) and Rentista thresholds are multiples of Colombia's minimum wage (SMMLV, salario mínimo mensual legal vigente), which the government revises every January — and Colombia's minimum wage has risen sharply in recent years, pulling the dollar-equivalent visa thresholds up with it even though the underlying multiplier hasn't changed.

## How the Pensionado and Rentista thresholds actually work

The Pensionado (M-11) visa is Colombia's dedicated route for people living on a foreign pension, and it requires demonstrating monthly pension income of roughly three times the current SMMLV — a formula, not a fixed dollar figure. Because the SMMLV itself rose again for 2026, the dollar-equivalent threshold has moved meaningfully year over year, and it will keep moving with both the peso's annual wage adjustment and the COP/USD exchange rate at the time you apply. This is worth understanding as a mechanic rather than memorizing a specific dollar number from any single source, since figures floating around online frequently reflect a prior year's minimum wage or a stale exchange rate. Confirm the exact current threshold directly through a Colombian consulate or an immigration attorney before you build a financial plan around it.

For applicants without a formal pension, the Rentista visa offers a passive-income alternative — investment income, rental income, or other non-employment income — set at a substantially higher multiple of the SMMLV, commonly cited around ten times the minimum wage. It's the right route for people retiring on investment portfolios or rental property income rather than a traditional pension, but the higher bar means it suits a smaller slice of prospective retirees than Pensionado does. A separate [Digital Nomad Visa](/blog/medellin-digital-nomad-setup-guide-2026) (DNV) route also exists for remote workers still earning active income, generally requiring proof of foreign employment or freelance income around $3,000 monthly — distinct from both retirement routes and intended for people who aren't retired at all but want Colombian residency while working remotely.

## Where retirees actually settle

Medellín remains the dominant retiree destination, and its "eternal spring" climate — a near-constant 65–75°F year-round thanks to its altitude — is a genuine, not just marketed, quality-of-life factor. Three-bedroom apartments in the city's popular El Poblado and Laureles neighborhoods typically run $700–1,200, giving retiree couples real space without San Francisco or Miami-level rent. Cartagena, on the Caribbean coast, trades the temperate climate for sea breezes and colonial architecture, with three-bedroom rentals running higher at $900–1,600 — reflecting both its tourist-driven housing market and the humidity many retirees pay a premium to escape via air conditioning and better-insulated buildings. Bogotá offers a third option for retirees who want a larger capital-city healthcare and cultural infrastructure, generally at rents between Medellín and Cartagena's ranges, at the cost of a cooler, rainier climate and higher altitude than either.

## Healthcare and the citizenship clock

Colombia's private healthcare system is one of its strongest draws for retirees, with hospitals like Clínica Las Vegas and CES in Medellín anchoring a reputation for quality care at a fraction of U.S. costs — a significant factor in Colombia's growing profile as a medical tourism destination generally, not just for retirees. Most Pensionado and Rentista visa holders enroll in Colombia's contributory health system (EPS) once resident, which is generally required to maintain visa status and provides broad access to both public and many private providers.

Naturalization is available after five years of continuous legal residency for most visa categories, including Pensionado and Rentista holders — a timeline that positions Colombia as a reasonably fast route to a second passport compared to many Western European alternatives, provided you maintain the residency and language requirements along the way. A realistic couple's budget — rent, food, private supplemental insurance, transport, and modest discretionary spending — commonly lands in the $1,500–2,500 monthly range excluding luxury travel, though Medellín and Bogotá skew toward the lower end of that range and Cartagena toward the higher end given its housing costs.

## What to verify before committing

Because both visa thresholds move with the SMMLV and are frequently quoted in outdated dollar terms online, the single most important step before committing to Colombia as a retirement destination is confirming the live, current-year threshold directly with a consulate or licensed immigration attorney — not a blog post, including this one — and running your specific pension or investment income against it at the actual exchange rate in effect when you plan to apply.
