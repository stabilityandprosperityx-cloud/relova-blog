Costa Rica runs two separate retirement lanes, and mixing them up is the single most common planning mistake. **Pensionado** status requires a **lifetime pension of at least USD 1,000 a month** from a foreign government, military, or a recognized private pension fund — critically, it has to be a *pension*, not general investment income, and you must exchange the full USD 1,000 monthly into colones through a regulated Costa Rican bank. **Rentista** status is the route for people without a qualifying pension: it asks for **USD 2,500 a month in documented, stable income verified over at least 24 months**, or an alternative **USD 60,000 deposited in a Costa Rican bank** with a commitment letter guaranteeing USD 2,500 monthly disbursements over two years. Both tracks require a minimum of **four months physical presence per year** to maintain status, and both follow the same structural timeline: **two years of temporary residency, then eligibility for permanent status after three years** total.

## The territorial tax system is the real headline

Costa Rica taxes on a **territorial basis** — meaning foreign pensions, foreign dividends, foreign capital gains, and remote-work income earned outside the country are **not taxed in Costa Rica regardless of your residency status**. This is arguably the bigger draw than either visa threshold, and it's why so many Pensionado and Rentista holders end up structuring their finances to keep income sources clearly foreign-sourced. It doesn't mean zero tax exposure — Costa Rican-sourced income (local employment, a local business, Costa Rican rental property) is taxed under the normal progressive schedule — but for a retiree living on a foreign pension or portfolio, the practical local tax bill on that income is genuinely zero.

## CAJA and the healthcare number people underestimate

Once you're a legal resident, enrollment in **CAJA**, the national healthcare system, becomes mandatory, not optional, and premiums are income-based rather than flat — figures commonly cited start **around $74 a month** for lower-income retirees and scale up from there based on declared income, sometimes substantially for higher earners. Private supplemental insurance on top of CAJA is common and typically runs another $50–150 a month depending on age and coverage, since CAJA wait times for specialists and elective procedures push many expats toward a hybrid public-private approach rather than relying on the public system alone.

## Inversionista and the shifting investment floor

There's a third, less-discussed route: **Inversionista** status, requiring a minimum **USD 150,000** investment in Costa Rican real estate, a registered company, qualifying securities, or sustainable tourism and forestry projects. That reduced $150,000 threshold is time-limited — current guidance points to an **expiration around July 2026**, after which the historical **USD 200,000 minimum** may return unless the government extends the lower figure again. If you're considering the investor route, confirm the live threshold with an immigration attorney before wiring funds; this is exactly the kind of number that shifts on a legislative calendar you won't see coming from a blog post.

## Central Valley versus coast: the retirement decision inside the decision

Most Pensionado and Rentista retirees end up choosing between the Central Valley — Escazú, Atenas, Grecia, and similar towns near San José offering cooler climate, better hospital access, and shorter drives to the international airport — and the Pacific or Caribbean coasts, which trade that convenience for beach access and a noticeably higher cost of living in expat-dense pockets like Tamarindo or Nosara. Healthcare access is the variable that matters most as retirees age: Central Valley towns sit close to San José's private hospitals (CIMA and Clínica Bíblica among the names most frequently cited by expats), while coastal living means longer transport times for anything beyond routine care — a trade-off worth weighing seriously rather than choosing on climate alone.

Costa Rica in 2026 is a **$1,000-pension-or-$2,500-income, territorial-tax, four-months-a-year** proposition. Get the Pensionado-versus-Rentista distinction right before you start assembling apostilled documents — the wrong track means restarting a paperwork process that already takes months.
