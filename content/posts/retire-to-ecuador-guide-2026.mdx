Ecuador dollarized its economy decades ago, which is the single fact that shapes almost everything else about retiring there: no currency risk, no exchange-rate math on your pension check, and pricing that's directly comparable to what you'd see back home rather than converted through a fluctuating peso, lira, or lek.

## The [Pensionado](/blog/move-to-quito-ecuador-guide-2026) visa income threshold

Ecuador's [Pensionado](/blog/retire-to-colombia-guide-2026) visa, the country's dedicated retiree route, sets its income requirement as a multiple of the Salario Básico Unificado (SBU), Ecuador's official minimum wage, which the government revises annually. For 2026, the SBU stands at $482, and the Pensionado threshold is set at three times that figure — $1,446 monthly in verified pension income. This is a meaningful increase from the lower thresholds — commonly cited around $800 — that circulated in earlier years, and it's worth flagging explicitly: if you're working from an older guide or forum post quoting $800, that figure is out of date. Ecuador's minimum wage, like most Latin American economies, has risen steadily, and the visa threshold rises in lockstep every January. Reassuringly for most American retirees, the average U.S. Social Security retirement benefit — running above $2,000 monthly as of 2026 SSA estimates — clears the current Pensionado threshold comfortably even for those who claimed benefits early at 62, though it's worth confirming your specific benefit amount against the live threshold before applying rather than assuming.

A separate Rentista visa exists for non-pension passive income — investment returns, rental income — generally set at a similar or somewhat higher multiple of the SBU depending on current regulation; verify the exact current figure with an immigration attorney, since Ecuador's visa categories and thresholds have been revised multiple times in recent years. For those preferring an investment-based route entirely independent of ongoing income proof, an investor visa is available with a real estate or fixed-deposit investment commonly cited around $30,000 — a fixed-capital alternative to the income-based Pensionado and Rentista routes.

## Cuenca versus Quito: altitude, climate, and cost

Cuenca has topped International Living's retirement rankings repeatedly for a reason that becomes obvious on arrival: spring-like weather nearly year-round at 2,550 meters of altitude, a walkable colonial center, and genuinely low housing costs — three-bedroom rentals commonly run $500–800, among the most affordable options for a full-size family apartment anywhere in the hemisphere. Its retiree population is large and well-established enough that English-language services, retiree meetup groups, and expat-oriented healthcare providers are easy to find, which matters more than people expect in the first year of a move.

Quito, Ecuador's capital, sits at a similar high altitude but offers a larger, more diversified healthcare system and broader cultural and dining infrastructure, at a modest premium — three-bedroom rentals typically run $600–1,000. It suits retirees who want capital-city depth (specialist medical care, international flight connections, a larger expat and diplomatic community) over Cuenca's smaller-city charm, at the cost of a bigger, busier city to navigate day to day.

## Healthcare and the senior discount system

IESS, Ecuador's public social security health insurance system, is open to legal residents including Pensionado visa holders, with enrollment commonly running around $80 monthly once you're in the system — a genuinely affordable option relative to most Western health insurance, though coverage depth and English-language service vary by facility, and many retirees supplement or substitute IESS with private insurance for faster access or specialist care. Ecuador also extends a widely used senior discount (carné de discapacidad and tercera edad benefits) of roughly 50% off transport, entertainment, and select healthcare and utility costs for qualifying seniors — a real, ongoing budget benefit once you have your resident's ID (cédula) and the applicable carné in hand.

## Taxes: territorial system, but verify sourcing

Ecuador generally operates on a territorial tax basis, meaning foreign-sourced pension income is typically not taxed by Ecuador — a meaningful advantage over worldwide-taxation systems elsewhere in the region. That said, "generally" is doing real work in that sentence: the specific sourcing rules for different income types (pension versus investment income versus any local Ecuadorian earnings) matter, and it's worth a consultation with a Quito-based accountant familiar with both Ecuadorian tax law and your home country's treaty position before assuming your full pension arrives tax-free.

## Building a realistic budget

A comfortable couple's budget in Ecuador — housing, food, transport, insurance, and modest discretionary spending — commonly lands in the $1,500–2,500 monthly range, with Cuenca skewing toward the lower end given its rent advantage and Quito toward the higher end.
