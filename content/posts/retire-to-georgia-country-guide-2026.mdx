[Georgia](/blog/georgia-american-expat-guide-2026) — the [country](/blog/georgia-country-beginners-guide-2026), not the U.S. state — pitches retirees on a combination that few destinations match: a visa-free entry policy generous enough to remove immigration paperwork as the first hurdle, and a territorial-leaning tax system with a specific, genuinely low-friction appeal for retirees who still earn a bit of consulting or freelance income on the side.

## Why the visa-free entry changes the calculus

Citizens of many Western countries, including the U.S., UK, and most of the EU, can enter Georgia and stay for up to 365 days without applying for any visa at all — a strikingly generous policy compared to the 90-day Schengen-style limits most European countries impose. For retirees, this means you can essentially "try before you buy": land, rent an apartment, and live in Georgia for a full year before you need to make any formal residency decision, which is a meaningfully lower-commitment on-ramp than most retiree visa programs elsewhere that require income documentation and a formal application before you've even seen the country. Long-term residency beyond that year typically requires either a residence permit tied to property ownership, income, or another qualifying category — worth researching well before your visa-free year runs out if you intend to stay longer.

## The 1% tax regime that draws in retirees with side income

Georgia's Individual Entrepreneur (IE) "Small Business Status" tax regime is arguably the country's single biggest draw for a specific kind of retiree: anyone who does a bit of paid consulting, translation, tutoring, or freelance work on the side, rather than living entirely off a fixed pension. Registered Small Business Status holders pay roughly 1% tax on qualifying business turnover up to an annual threshold, a genuinely remarkable rate compared to almost anywhere else in the world, and the registration process itself is comparatively fast and low-bureaucracy by regional standards. It's worth being precise about what this regime covers, though — it applies to registered business/self-employment turnover, not to foreign pension income itself, which is generally treated separately under Georgia's tax rules. Retirees living purely off a foreign pension with no local business activity should consult a Georgian accountant on how that pension income is actually treated, since the 1% regime's appeal is specifically for those layering in active side income, not a blanket exemption on retirement income broadly.

## Where retirees settle: Tbilisi's Vake versus Batumi

Vake, one of Tbilisi's most established and walkable residential districts, is the default choice for retirees wanting quiet, tree-lined streets, proximity to good private clinics, and an easy taxi or walk into the capital's restaurant and cultural scene — three-bedroom apartments here typically run $600–1,200. Batumi, Georgia's Black Sea resort city, offers a very different profile: modern high-rise towers with sea views, a beach-town pace, and generally lower rents at $400–800 for a comparable three-bedroom, at the cost of higher humidity and a more seasonal, tourist-driven local economy outside peak months. Many retirees who prioritize budget and coastal living choose Batumi; those who want capital-city healthcare depth, an international airport with more direct connections, and a larger expat community choose Vake or another central Tbilisi district.

## Healthcare: private, and worth budgeting for deliberately

Georgia's public healthcare system exists but is genuinely uneven in quality and English-language capacity outside the capital, so most retirees budget for private care from the outset rather than treating public healthcare as a real fallback. Private GP visits commonly run $20–40 — remarkably affordable by Western standards — and networks like GHG (Georgia Healthcare Group) and Aversi operate clinics and pharmacies across Tbilisi and other major cities with reasonably modern facilities. For anything beyond routine care, comprehensive private international health insurance is worth carrying, since specialist and emergency care quality can vary meaningfully by facility.

## Culture, safety, and realistic monthly costs

Georgian social life leans heavily on its wine culture — Georgia claims one of the world's oldest winemaking traditions, commonly dated to roughly 8,000 years, centered on traditional qvevri clay-vessel fermentation — and the supra, a long-form feast with formal toasting traditions that's genuinely central to Georgian hospitality rather than a tourist performance. Street crime against seniors and foreigners generally stays low by regional and even Western standards, even though Georgia's broader geopolitical environment has had periods of political tension worth staying informed about, particularly given the country's location bordering Russia. A comfortable couple's monthly budget — rent, food, private insurance, and regional flights included — commonly lands in the $1,200–2,000 range, among the more affordable options for retirees wanting a European-adjacent lifestyle outside the EU itself.
