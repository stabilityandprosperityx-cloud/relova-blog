Retiring to [Italy](/blog/move-to-puglia-guide-2026) as a non-EU citizen without local employment almost always runs through the Elective Residency Visa (Visto per Residenza Elettiva), a route built specifically for people living on pensions, investments, or other passive income rather than a salary. Getting it right in 2026 means two separate pieces have to line up: the income test that gets you the visa, and the regional tax election that determines what [Italy](/blog/move-to-sicily-guide-2026) actually takes from that income once you're a resident.

## The Elective Residency Visa income test

Consulates want to see stable, documented passive income — pensions, rental income, dividends, annuities — not savings alone, and not employment income, since working in Italy on this visa is prohibited. Practical benchmarks used by Italian consulates put the floor at roughly EUR 32,000 a year for a single applicant, with an additional allowance (commonly cited around EUR 6,200) per dependent, so a retired couple typically needs to show closer to EUR 38,000 combined. These are consulate-level guidelines rather than a single published statute, and individual consulates (New York, Los Angeles, London, and so on) have been known to apply slightly different documentation standards, so confirm the current figure with the consulate covering your jurisdiction before you assemble bank statements. The application fee itself is modest — around EUR 116 — but the visa can take up to 90 days to process, consulate appointment slots often book out four to eight weeks in advance, and you'll need a signed long-term lease or property deed in Italy before you can even apply, since proof of accommodation is a hard requirement.

## The 7% flat tax: where it applies and what changed in 2026

Once resident, retirees relocating to Italy's south get access to a genuinely attractive optional regime: a flat 7% tax on all foreign-sourced income — pensions, foreign rental income, foreign investment income — for up to ten years, instead of Italy's ordinary progressive rates that can reach 43%. The regime is geographically restricted to comuni (municipalities) in Sicily, Calabria, Sardinia, Campania, Basilicata, Abruzzo, Molise, and Puglia, plus a handful of central Italian towns still rebuilding after the 2009 and 2016 earthquakes. A 2026 legislative update raised the population cap for eligible towns from 20,000 to 30,000 residents, effective from April 2026, which meaningfully widened the list of qualifying municipalities in the south — worth rechecking against the official comune list even if a town wasn't eligible when you last looked. The regime requires you not to have been an Italian tax resident in the five years prior to the move, and electing it means all your foreign income is taxed at 7% with no possibility of foreign tax credits, so it suits people with meaningfully sized foreign pensions more than those relying mostly on Italian-source income.

## What it actually costs to live on

Where you land inside that eligible zone changes your monthly numbers substantially. A three-bedroom in inland Sicily or rural Puglia commonly runs EUR 450-800 a month, well below what the same square footage costs in Rome, Milan, or [Florence](/blog/move-to-florence-guide-2026). Realistically, a retired couple living comfortably in a mid-sized southern town — groceries, utilities, transport, health top-up insurance, occasional travel — should budget somewhere in the EUR 1,500-2,500 a month range, before accounting for the 7% tax itself. That combination — a favorable flat rate on foreign income plus genuinely low regional living costs — is the core financial case for this route, but it depends entirely on getting the visa income test and the tax election paperwork right in the correct order: residency first, tax election within the required window after that.
