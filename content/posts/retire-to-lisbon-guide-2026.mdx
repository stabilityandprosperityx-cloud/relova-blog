**Retiring to [Lisbon](/blog/lisbon-american-taxes-banking-2026)** runs almost entirely through the D7 visa, and the single biggest mistake retirees make is assuming the old 10% flat pension tax still applies to them — it generally does not anymore.

## The D7 income floor moves with the minimum wage, and it is not your real budget

Portugal's D7 "passive income" visa requires proof of regular income — pension, rental income, dividends, or similar — of at least the Portuguese minimum wage, which is EUR 920 a month (EUR 11,040 a year) for 2026. A spouse or additional adult dependent adds 50% of that figure, and each dependent child adds 30%, so a retired couple needs to show roughly EUR 1,380 a month combined, not EUR 920 doubled. That number is a legal floor for visa approval, not a livable budget: consulates and SEF/AIMA case officers generally want to see income comfortably above the minimum, and in practice most retired couples target EUR 2,000–3,000 a month all-in once housing, private health insurance, and everyday costs are included.

## The NHR pension tax break mostly does not apply to new retirees anymore

This is the detail that trips up more retirees than any other: Portugal's old Non-Habitual Resident (NHR) regime, which taxed foreign pension income at a flat 10%, closed to new applicants at the end of 2023. It was replaced by a narrower regime — commonly called NHR 2.0 or IFICI — that targets people in specific high-value professional activities (science, technology, engineering, certain executive and academic roles), not passive retirees living off a pension. Standard D7 applicants relying on pension, investment, or rental income generally do not qualify for IFICI, and foreign pension income for new arrivals is now taxed under Portugal's ordinary progressive income tax brackets rather than a flat concessionary rate. Anyone who registered under the old NHR regime before the 2023 cutoff, or who fell under a documented transitional exception, may still be finishing out their original 10-year NHR window — but that is a legacy position, not something newly available in 2026. This is worth confirming directly with a Portuguese accountant (contabilista certificado) before budgeting around a tax rate that may no longer be on the table.

## Healthcare enrollment for retirees runs through two overlapping systems

Once residency is granted, retirees can register with Portugal's public Serviço Nacional de Saúde (SNS) through their local health center (centro de saúde), which requires a Portuguese address, NIF (tax number), and residence documentation — a process that commonly takes several weeks to get a permanent médico de família (family doctor) assigned, though urgent and walk-in care is accessible sooner. Retirees from EU/EEA countries with an S1 form (typically UK state pensioners under the post-Brexit agreement, or other EU pensioners) can have SNS access effectively funded by their home system. Non-EU retirees, including most Americans, generally need private international health insurance as part of the D7 application itself and often keep it running alongside SNS registration, since private clinics offer materially shorter wait times for specialists — a meaningful factor for an older population weighing routine versus urgent care needs.

## Retiree-oriented neighborhoods trade commute time for calm

Cascais, roughly 30 minutes from central [Lisbon](/blog/athens-vs-lisbon-expat-guide-2026) by train along the coast, is the most established retiree hub: flat, walkable, English widely spoken in shops and clinics, and a genuinely large existing international retiree community — three-bedroom homes there commonly run EUR 1,800–2,500 a month, a premium over central [Lisbon](/blog/lisbon-ultimate-setup-guide-2026) in exchange for quiet streets and beach access. Oeiras, between Lisbon and Cascais on the same train line, offers a similar profile at somewhat lower prices. Within Lisbon itself, retirees tend to gravitate toward Restelo and parts of Lapa — quieter, more residential districts than the nightlife-heavy Bairro Alto or Alfama — for the combination of flat streets, established medical clinics, and lower stair-climbing demands than the city's older hillside neighborhoods.
