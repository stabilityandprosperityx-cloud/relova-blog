## Why Retirees Choose Mexico's Tax Regime

**Retiring to [Mexico](/blog/move-to-mexico-city-guide-2026) in 2026** puts you in the world's largest cross-border retirement corridor: an estimated 1.5 million American retirees now live in the country, drawn by a mix of proximity, culture, and a tax regime that is genuinely favorable for people living off foreign pensions. [Mexico](/blog/move-to-mexico-guide-2026)'s territorial approach to taxing residents generally means foreign-sourced pension and retirement income is not taxed locally when structured correctly, though "structured correctly" is doing real work in that sentence — get a cross-border accountant to confirm how your specific pension type is treated before assuming you owe nothing.

## The Temporary Resident Visa Process

The legal basis for staying is the temporary resident visa, applied for at a Mexican consulate in your home country before you travel. Consulates set their own income or savings thresholds, and while the exact figure varies by consulate and shifts periodically, many 2026 checklists cite monthly income near $2,600, or an equivalent lump-sum savings alternative for people who don't want to prove recurring income. The temporary resident visa is initially valid for one year and renewable inside [Mexico](/blog/move-to-oaxaca-mexico-guide-2026) for up to three more years, after which permanent residency becomes available in many common scenarios — so budget for roughly four years on the temporary track before you're free of renewal paperwork, though some applicants with sufficiently high savings can sometimes qualify for permanent residency from the outset.

## Healthcare and IMSS Enrollment

Healthcare is one of the pleasant surprises of retiring in Mexico. Once you hold a CURP (the national population registry number, obtained early in your residency process), voluntary enrollment in IMSS, the public health system, is remarkably inexpensive — often landing in the $400–500 per year range for essentially full basic coverage, though pre-existing conditions can affect what's covered and many retirees pair IMSS with a private supplemental policy for faster access to specialists. Private healthcare quality in Mexico's major expat hubs and big cities is generally excellent and inexpensive by US standards even without insurance, which takes some of the pressure off getting every detail of coverage perfect.

## Housing Costs by Region

Housing costs vary enormously by region, and this is where most of the "how much does retiring in Mexico actually cost" answer lives. Lake Chapala and its lakeside town of Ajijic — the country's largest and longest-established gringo retirement hub — offer three-bedroom homes for $600–1,000 a month, making it one of the most budget-friendly options for retirees who want an established expat community, English-speaking services, and a mild climate. San Miguel de Allende, the colonial-architecture darling that has become something of a status destination, carries a real premium: three-bedroom rentals run $800–1,400. Puerto Vallarta, the beach option with international airport access and a large LGBTQ+-friendly expat community, sits similarly high at $900–1,600. For a couple, a realistic all-in monthly budget — housing, food, insurance, some domestic help, and private dental care, which remains a bargain relative to the US — tends to land in the $2,000–3,500 range depending on which of these regions you pick and how much you lean into imported goods versus local markets.

The practical throughline is that Mexico rewards people who do their homework on the visa income threshold and health coverage before committing to a specific town, since the cost-of-living swing between Chapala and San Miguel alone can be nearly double.

## Climate Differences Between Highlands and Coast

Climate and geography also deserve real weight in the decision, since Mexico's regions differ dramatically. The central highlands, including Lake Chapala and San Miguel de Allende, sit at elevation and enjoy a famously mild, spring-like climate year-round — a major reason both towns became retiree magnets in the first place, since neither humidity nor extreme heat is a daily concern. Coastal Puerto Vallarta trades that mild climate for beach access and a hot, humid tropical pattern with a pronounced rainy season, which suits retirees prioritizing ocean proximity over temperature comfort. Anyone choosing between the highlands and the coast should visit during both the dry and wet seasons before committing to a lease, since a town's character can shift substantially between January and August.

## CURP, Banking, and Vehicle Import Rules

Legal and administrative details also merit planning ahead of arrival. Temporary and permanent residents are generally required to obtain a CURP soon after arrival, since it unlocks not just IMSS enrollment but everyday necessities like opening a Mexican bank account and signing utility contracts. Retirees bringing a vehicle across the border should research Mexico's temporary import permit rules carefully, as leaving a foreign-plated car in the country past the permitted window can trigger fines or impoundment — a detail that catches out more newcomers than the visa income threshold itself.
