The D7 "passive income visa" remains the standard [retirement](/blog/retire-to-lisbon-guide-2026) route into Portugal for non-EU nationals, and the number everyone quotes — the minimum monthly income floor — is not a fixed figure. It is pegged to the Portuguese national minimum wage, which rose again for 2026, putting the D7 income floor at roughly **EUR 920 a month** for a single applicant. Because this figure moves with the minimum wage every January, treat any number you read (including this one) as needing a re-check against the current official value the month you actually file, not a number to bank a retirement budget on a year in advance.

## The income test wants recurring, not lump-sum, proof

Consulates evaluating a D7 application want evidence the income is stable and passive: pension statements, Social Security award letters, rental income agreements, dividend or annuity statements. A retiree relying on a state pension plus a private pension typically stacks both to clear the threshold, and dependents add roughly 50% of the base floor for a spouse or additional adult, and 30% for a minor child — so a retired couple should plan around something closer to **EUR 1,380 a month** combined as the legal floor, well below what most people actually need to live comfortably in [Lisbon](/blog/move-to-lisbon-uk-citizen-guide-2026) or Porto specifically, versus smaller cities and the interior where the floor and a realistic lifestyle budget sit much closer together.

## NHR is closed — IFICI is the successor, and it mostly does not help retirees

Portugal's classic Non-Habitual Resident regime, which many retirees used for a flat, favorable tax rate on foreign pension income, closed to new applicants on **1 January 2024**. Its replacement, IFICI (the "NHR 2.0," sometimes called the Tax Incentive for Scientific Research and Innovation), is narrowly targeted at highly skilled professionals in specific fields — engineering, medicine, IT, scientific research, executive roles in the innovation economy — and generally does **not** extend to standard D7 retirees living on pension or investment income. Foreign pension income for most new D7 arrivals is now taxed under Portugal's ordinary progressive income tax brackets, not a flat preferential rate. Anyone telling you IFICI gives retirees a flat 10% on pension income in 2026 is describing the old NHR regime that no longer applies to new applicants — verify your specific eligibility with a Portuguese tax advisor rather than assuming continuity with pre-2024 retiree tax planning.

## Healthcare: SNS eligibility is not immediate

Once legally resident, you become eligible to register with Portugal's public health service, the SNS (Serviço Nacional de Saúde), through your local health center (centro de saúde) using your residence permit and a Número de Utente — but registration and actually being assigned a family doctor are two different steps, and the second one can take considerably longer in Lisbon and Porto than in smaller municipalities. Most retirees carry private health insurance through at least the first year, both because it is a D7 application requirement in the first place and because relying solely on SNS during the registration gap is not a realistic bridge for anyone with ongoing medical needs. US citizens should also confirm Medicare generally does not cover care received in Portugal, so private coverage or the SNS system, once active, is doing real work, not serving as a backup.

## The five-year clock to citizenship

Legal residency under the D7 counts toward Portugal's citizenship-by-naturalization path, which requires **five years** of legal residence plus a Portuguese-language exam at the **A2** level (basic, not conversational fluency) — a materially lower bar than Germany's or France's language requirements, and part of why Portugal remains a popular long-term settle-and-naturalize destination for retirees who want an EU passport eventually rather than just residency.
