Choosing how to move money internationally as an expat comes down to three variables that trade off against each other: the visible fee, the invisible FX markup buried in the exchange rate, and how fast the money actually lands. Wise remains the reference point most people compare everything else against, with a transparent mid-market-rate-plus-fee model that typically works out to roughly 0.4–1% all-in depending on the currency corridor — the fee is shown before you commit, which is the main reason it's become the default for rent payments and freelance invoicing. Revolut's free tier bundles a limited number of fee-free currency exchanges each month (commonly five, up to around £1,000 in volume) before switching to a small markup, making it a reasonable secondary option for occasional smaller transfers but not a primary tool for large one-off payments like a security deposit.

## Legacy wires versus fintech rails

A traditional SWIFT bank wire is still what most banks default to, and it's usually the most expensive option once you count both the flat sending fee (commonly $15–45) and the correspondent-bank deductions that can shave additional amounts off the amount that actually arrives — the sender rarely controls or even sees this second cost. Payoneer, popular with freelancers who get paid through marketplaces (Upwork, Fiverr) or platforms, typically runs around 2% above the mid-market rate for currency conversion, which is worse than Wise but often unavoidable if a client platform only supports Payoneer payouts. Airwallex, aimed more at freelancers running an actual business than individuals, generally lands in the 0.5–1% range and adds useful business banking features (multi-currency accounts, virtual cards) that pure transfer services don't offer.

## What US persons specifically need to track

Moving money abroad doesn't change US filing obligations, and two thresholds matter regardless of which transfer service is used: FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) triggers once the aggregate balance across all foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point in the year, and FATCA's Form 8938 has its own separate thresholds for US residents (starting around $50,000 at year-end for single filers) that rise substantially for Americans who qualify as bona fide residents abroad. These are reporting requirements, not taxes in themselves, but the penalties for missing them are real — confirm your exact filing obligation with a US CPA who has expat experience rather than assuming a domestic accountant's software covers it correctly.

## Reading the exchange rate, not just the fee

The single most common way people overpay on international transfers isn't the visible fee — it's an exchange rate quietly marked up above the real mid-market rate, a practice that traditional banks rely on heavily since it doesn't show up as a line-item charge. Before any transfer over a few hundred dollars, it's worth checking the actual mid-market rate (Google or XE both show it) against what a provider is quoting, and treating the difference as the real cost of the transfer rather than trusting a "no fee" claim at face value. A "zero fee" transfer with a 3% rate markup is meaningfully more expensive than a $10 flat fee with a rate that matches the mid-market rate closely — the two need to be compared together, not separately.

## Timing large transfers around rent and deposits

For one-off large transfers — a security deposit, a down payment, moving a full emergency fund — it's worth planning the transfer a few days ahead of the deadline rather than same-day, both because larger transfers sometimes trigger additional identity verification that can add a day or two, and because currency volatility means the rate on the day of initiation can differ meaningfully from the rate a landlord or agent quoted when the lease was signed. Some providers, including Wise and a handful of forex specialists, offer rate locks or forward contracts for people who want to fix a rate ahead of a known future payment — a tool worth knowing about even if it's only used once for a specific deposit or tuition payment.
