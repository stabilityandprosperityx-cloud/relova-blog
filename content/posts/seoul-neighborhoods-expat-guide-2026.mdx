Choosing a Seoul neighborhood is really choosing a rent structure first and a lifestyle second, because [Korea](/blog/south-korea-digital-nomad-visa-guide-2026)'s deposit system changes the math more than the map does. Most listings you will see quoted to foreigners are **wolse** (monthly rent plus a modest deposit), but the market default for [Korea](/blog/move-to-busan-guide-2026)ns is still **jeonse** — a lump-sum deposit, often **50–80% of the unit's value**, paid up front in exchange for zero monthly rent, refunded in full when you leave. Almost no expat arrives with enough cash to jeonse a first apartment, so wolse is the realistic starting point: expect a deposit of roughly **10–20 times the monthly rent** rather than the one-month deposit common in Europe or North America.

## Itaewon and Haebangchon carry the highest English-fluency premium

**Itaewon** is still the most international pocket of the city — embassy row, English-speaking clinics, and a nightlife scene built around decades of foreign residents — and it prices accordingly, roughly **₩900,000–1,400,000** a month for a one-bedroom with a manageable deposit. Just up the hill, **Haebangchon (HBC)** offers a quieter, more residential version of the same expat infrastructure for **₩700,000–1,200,000**, and it is the neighborhood most returning expats mention as the one they wish they had started in rather than found in year two.

## Yeonnam-dong and Hongdae trade nightlife noise for café density

**Hongdae** is the classic young, loud, nightlife-driven district near Hongik University, popular with students and digital nomads under 30, running **₩700,000–1,300,000**. A few blocks over, **Yeonnam-dong** has become the preferred alternative for remote workers who want Hongdae's café culture and walkability without the weekend noise — similar pricing, **₩800,000–1,300,000**, but a calmer street-level feel once you're off the main strip.

## Gangnam is a different city on the same subway map

**Gangnam** is Seoul's corporate and financial core, and it prices like one: **₩1,300,000–2,500,000+** for a one-bedroom, a **30–50% premium** over comparable units north of the river. It makes sense if an employer is covering housing or the job itself is in Gangnam or nearby Yeoksam — the commute-time savings are real in a city where cross-town trips can eat 45–60 minutes even on excellent transit. Without a housing allowance, it is hard to justify as a first-year default.

## The subway is the actual neighborhood-selection tool

Seoul's metro runs **23 lines** across the wider network (including Incheon and Gyeonggi extensions), one of the densest urban rail systems anywhere, and most seasoned expats pick a home based on subway-line access before they pick a district's reputation. A unit two minutes from any line on the 2, 5, or 9 lines will generally beat a "nicer" neighborhood that requires a bus transfer. **Seodaemun/Muak**, at **₩600,000–1,000,000**, is the budget-and-nature option — cheaper rent with direct access to mountain trails, at the cost of being a longer ride from the Itaewon/Hongdae corridor most new arrivals gravitate toward socially.

## Try before you sign a long lease

Korean leases are typically **one or two years**, and breaking one early is expensive and administratively painful for a foreigner without a local guarantor. The standard advice from relocation veterans — trial two or three neighborhoods through short-term monthly housing (goshiwon, share houses, or serviced studios) before committing to a standard wolse contract — is worth taking seriously here specifically because the deposit sizes make an early exit costly, not just annoying.
