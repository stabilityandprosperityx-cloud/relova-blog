Shipping a household internationally is one of the few relocation costs that moves with global freight markets rather than local rent inflation — a quote you got last year is not a reliable anchor for this year's price, since ocean freight rates swing with fuel costs, container availability, and seasonal demand in ways rent simply doesn't.

## Full-container pricing varies more by route than by size

A **20-foot FCL (full container load)** — enough for a one- to two-bedroom household — currently runs roughly **$1,600–2,500** on a US-to-Europe route and a wider **$900–2,100** on US-to-Asia routes, where competition and volume vary more by specific port pair. A **40-foot container**, doubling your capacity, does not double the price: it commonly lands in a similar or only modestly higher band on the same routes, which is why movers with a genuinely full household often find the 40-foot the better value per cubic foot even when they don't need the full capacity. Rates on less-traveled trade lanes, like UK-to-Australia, run noticeably higher — quoted around **$1,900–2,800** for a 20-foot equivalent — reflecting lower shipping volume and fewer competing carriers on that corridor. Get quotes from at least two freight forwarders on your specific route rather than trusting a generic global average, since route-specific variation is larger than most people expect.

## LCL makes sense below roughly a single room's worth of goods

**LCL (less-than-container load)**, where your goods share a container with other shipments, is the standard option once your inventory drops below around 500 cubic feet — meaningfully less than a full studio's worth of furniture. Below that threshold LCL is usually cheaper than paying for a full container you don't need; above it, the math commonly flips, since a partially-filled container often costs close to a full one once handling and consolidation fees are added. Transit time is the real trade-off: LCL shipments typically take longer than FCL because your goods wait for the container to consolidate and then get handled twice — once in, once out — at the consolidation warehouse.

## Airfreight is the fast, expensive exception

For a small volume that genuinely cannot wait for ocean transit — documents, essential electronics, a first-week survival kit — airfreight runs roughly **$8–15 per kilogram** depending on route and carrier, which is why most movers use it sparingly for a small express shipment while the bulk of their household goes by sea. Doing the math on a full household by air versus a fraction of that cost by container makes the case for airfreight obvious once you see the two numbers side by side.

## Insurance and duty-free relief are the two line items people skip

Marine cargo insurance for household goods typically runs **1–3% of declared value**, and it is worth paying regardless of how careful your mover seems — most international carriers' standard liability coverage, absent a separate policy, is minimal and calculated by weight rather than actual value, which means a damaged high-value item is barely compensated without it. Separately, most countries offer **duty-free import relief** for household goods brought in as part of a genuine relocation, but it is conditional, not automatic: you typically need to show proof of prior residence abroad, a matching change-of-residence visa or permit, and goods that were owned and used before the move rather than newly purchased for import. The paperwork requirements and qualifying time-abroad thresholds differ by destination country, so confirm the specific relief conditions with that country's customs authority before you ship — assuming duty-free treatment and then facing an unexpected import bill at the port is one of the most common and expensive surprises in this process.
