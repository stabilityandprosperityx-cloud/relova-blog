The H-1B lottery has actually gotten *more* winnable on paper — and simultaneously more expensive to bank on, depending on when you read this. After USCIS moved to beneficiary-centric selection (one entry per person rather than one per registration), duplicate filings collapsed and the FY2026 selection rate reportedly reached **35.3%**, up sharply from the sub-20% odds candidates faced a few cycles earlier when multiple-employer gaming inflated the applicant pool. That is the good news. The complication is a presidential proclamation from **September 21, 2025** that introduced a **$100,000** fee on new H-1B petitions filed for beneficiaries outside the US without a valid H-1B already in hand — a fee that does not apply to renewals, amendments, or anyone already holding H-1B status. Its legal status has been genuinely unsettled through 2026: a DC district court upheld it in December 2025, a Massachusetts court vacated it as an unlawful tax in June 2026, and an appeals court halted collection in July while the case proceeds — meaning the fee's actual applicability could still change again by the time you read this. Confirm current status directly on uscis.gov before assuming either outcome.

## Switzerland pays the best in Europe if the cantonal math works

Swiss developer salaries commonly land around **CHF 120,000** a year for a solid mid-level role in Zurich or Geneva — genuinely the strongest developer comp in Europe — but Switzerland is not in the EU, so non-EU/EFTA hires go through a cantonal quota system rather than a single national visa, and quota availability varies by canton and year. Combined with a cost of living that eats a meaningful share of that CHF 120,000 (Zurich rents rival or exceed most EU capitals), the net advantage over Germany or the Netherlands is real but smaller than the headline number implies.

## Germany's Blue Card shortage-occupation rate favors developers specifically

Software developers qualify for Germany's **shortage-occupation** EU Blue Card threshold, set at **€45,934.20** for 2026 rather than the €50,700 standard-occupation bar — a meaningful gap that many candidates never check before assuming they need the higher number. Combined with a Blue Card path to permanent residence in as little as **21 months**, Germany remains one of the more realistic mid-career entry points into the EU for developers without a Big Tech-level offer.

## Singapore's Employment Pass bar rose again for 2026

Singapore's **Employment Pass** minimum qualifying salary moved to **SGD 6,000** a month for general sectors in 2026 (up from SGD 5,600), and **SGD 6,600** for financial services roles. Those figures are the floor for young graduates in their 20s — the actual bar for candidates in their 30s and 40s is benchmarked against local market pay for similar experience and commonly runs **SGD 10,000–12,000**, so a generic "SGD 6,000" headline understates what an experienced developer typically needs to clear.

## The UAE's zero personal tax is the simplest comparison to get wrong

The UAE levies no personal income tax at all, which sounds like a straightforward net-pay win over Germany's or Switzerland's income tax bite — and often is — but the comparison only holds if you also account for employer-sponsored health insurance quality, the lack of a public pension contribution building toward anything, and the absence of a long-term residency track for most standard employment visas. Treat the UAE as a strong multi-year savings play rather than a like-for-like substitute for a European Blue Card route.
