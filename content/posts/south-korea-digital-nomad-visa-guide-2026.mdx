**South Korea [digital nomad visa](/blog/move-to-busan-guide-2026) 2026** is the **F-1-D “[Workation](/blog/seoul-neighborhoods-expat-guide-2026)”** visa—and as of **30 June 2026** it is a **permanent program**, not a pilot. The pilot ran from January 2024 through May 2026; the permanent rules raise the maximum stay to **three years** (previously a 1+1 two-year pattern) and lower income thresholds for younger applicants living **outside the Seoul capital region**.

## Permanent program and longer stays

On **30 June 2026**, F-1-D Workation became permanent. Maximum stay is now **three years**. Processing is typically **10–15 days**. You still may not earn from Korean companies or Korean-source clients—this is remote work for a **foreign** employer or foreign company you own. ## Income thresholds by age and region

Standard income evidence is **double GNI per capita**—about **$65,800–66,000/year** (roughly **₩88.1 million**). Applicants aged **18–34** who will live **outside the Seoul metropolitan area** (or in designated depopulating regions) can qualify at **1× GNI per capita**—about **half** the standard bar (~**$33,000**). Age alone is not enough; the regional residence plan matters.

## Who qualifies and how to switch status

You must be **18+**, show at least **one year** of experience in the same industry, and prove employment by (or ownership of) a **foreign** company. Freelancers without a clear foreign-company tie face a harder path. You can often **change status to F-1-D from inside Korea** while on visa-free **B-1/B-2** stay—no mandatory exit required for that switch when rules allow.

## Insurance, work bans, and tax

Private insurance must cover at least **₩100,000,000** (~**$75,000**) for medical care and repatriation. Korean-source work remains banned throughout the visa's duration — this is strictly for people paid by a foreign entity. On tax, the sketch is: under **183 days** of presence, only Korean-source income is typically taxed, so most F-1-D holders staying short-term owe nothing locally on their foreign salary. Once you cross into Korean tax residency (**183+ days** in a year), foreign income not remitted into Korea can generally stay outside Korean tax for the **first five years** of residency under current framing — but this is exactly the kind of provision that gets revisited, so confirm the live rule with a Korean tax professional before you structure payroll or move significant funds.

Put together, the F-1-D programme now offers meaningfully more flexibility than its pilot phase: a three-year ceiling instead of the old 1+1 pattern, processing in 10–15 days, and a genuine income-threshold discount for younger applicants willing to live outside the Seoul metro area. That regional discount is arguably the most interesting design choice in the permanent programme — Korea is using its nomad visa as a tool to steer newcomers toward depopulating regions rather than concentrating everyone in Seoul, and applicants aged 18–34 who commit to a non-Seoul base can qualify at roughly half the standard income bar.

The one-year work-experience requirement is worth flagging separately because it trips up more applicants than the income threshold does. Immigration officers want to see documented, verifiable employment or business ownership in the same field for at least a year before application — a recent graduate with strong freelance income but no formal employment history in that industry may struggle to satisfy this even if their earnings clear the GNI bar comfortably. Gathering employment letters, contracts, or incorporation documents ahead of time, rather than scrambling for them once an appointment is booked, tends to be the difference between a smooth application and a frustrating back-and-forth with the consulate or immigration office.

It's also worth understanding what F-1-D does not give you: it is explicitly not a path to Korean employment or Korean-source income, and it carries no direct route to permanent residency the way some European nomad visas do. Treat it as what it is — a genuinely well-designed long-stay visa for people who want to live in Korea while working for employers or clients based elsewhere — rather than as a stepping stone to Korean citizenship, since that expectation will lead to disappointment when the three-year ceiling arrives without a renewal path attached.
