**Registering as autónomo in [Spain](/blog/retire-to-spain-guide-2026)** is the standard route for [freelancers](/blog/move-to-seville-guide-2026) who want to invoice clients legally rather than operate in a gray zone, and the process is more mechanical than mysterious once you understand the moving parts: Social Security registration (RETA), quarterly income tax prepayments, VAT, and — for many new arrivals — a favorable digital-nomad tax regime layered on top.

The Social Security piece is usually what draws people's attention first, because Spain runs a well-publicized discount for new registrants. RETA contributions, ordinarily calculated on a sliding scale tied to your declared income band, are commonly reduced to a flat rate near €230 a month under the Tarifa Plana scheme for a set window after you first register — a significant break versus the standard RETA rate, which lands closer to €292 a month at the entry tier and climbs from there as declared income rises. The reduced rate is time-limited, so budget for the jump back to standard contributions once the discount period expires; the exact duration and phase-out structure have shifted in past reforms, so confirm the current window when you register rather than assuming last year's terms still apply.

On income tax, autónomos make quarterly advance payments toward their annual IRPF liability using Modelo 130, and the standard prepayment rate is 20% of net profit — though many new freelancers benefit from a reduced 7% rate on their invoices' withheld tax for their first three years of activity in various circumstances, which meaningfully eases early cash flow. VAT (IVA) sits at the standard 21% rate for most domestic B2B and B2C services, filed quarterly via Modelo 303, though certain exports and intra-EU B2B services can be zero-rated under reverse-charge rules — worth understanding early if your client base is international.

Most autónomos, especially in their first year, hire a gestoría — a local accounting and admin service — to handle the quarterly VAT and social security filings, and typical gestoría fees run €50–150 a month depending on transaction volume and how much hand-holding you want. This is one of the few "optional" costs in the Spanish freelance system that is, in practice, close to mandatory for anyone without fluent Spanish and prior experience with Hacienda's filing portals.

For newcomers who qualify, the Beckham Law special regime is worth investigating separately from ordinary autónomo registration: it offers a flat 24% income tax rate for up to six years for qualifying new tax residents, versus Spain's ordinary progressive rates that can exceed 45% at higher income bands — though eligibility rules (largely built around employees and certain company directors rather than typical freelancers) mean many autónomos won't qualify and should not assume it applies to their situation. If you're weighing whether autónomo registration or Spain's separate digital nomad visa income route (cited around €2,334 a month) fits your situation better, see the dedicated [Spain Digital Nomad Visa](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/spain-digital-nomad-visa) guide — the two are related but not interchangeable, since the DNV is an immigration status while autónomo is a tax/social-security registration that EU citizens and existing residents use regardless of visa type.

All told, most autónomos should model an all-in tax and social security burden somewhere in the 30–35% range of gross income once RETA, IRPF, and VAT administration are accounted for — lower during the Tarifa Plana discount window, higher once it expires. Register through the Importass online portal (Social Security's digital system) or your regional tax office before issuing your first invoice; operating without registration exposes you to backdated contributions and penalties if discovered.

Timing your registration matters more than most first-time autónomos realize. RETA contributions are typically owed from your registration date regardless of whether you've invoiced anything yet that month, so registering on the 1st versus the 28th of a given month can materially change your first bill — some freelancers deliberately time registration around the calendar to optimize this, though the savings are modest enough that it shouldn't override getting registered promptly once you have real client work lined up. It's also worth knowing that RETA contributions, unlike ordinary employee payroll deductions, fund your eventual Spanish state pension and unemployment-adjacent benefits (the paro autónomo), so the monthly payment isn't purely an administrative cost — it's building toward a benefit, even if that framing rarely makes the invoice easier to write.
