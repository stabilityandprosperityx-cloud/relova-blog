**The NIE (Número de Identidad de Extranjero)** is [Spain](/blog/move-to-valencia-spain-guide-2026)'s foreigner identification number, and it is genuinely unavoidable: you cannot open a bank account, sign a rental contract, register with a doctor, buy a car, sign an employment contract, or pay taxes in [Spain](/blog/move-to-malaga-guide-2026) without one. It is not a residency permit or a visa — it is simply an identifier that every foreigner interacting with Spanish bureaucracy needs, whether you're a tourist buying property, a digital nomad on a [Barcelona-based](/blog/move-to-barcelona-nomad-guide-2026) visa, or a retiree settling in for good.

There are two main ways to get one. You can apply at a Spanish consulate before you move, which is often the calmer option since consulate appointment slots, while still competitive, tend to be less oversubscribed than the domestic system. Or you can apply in Spain itself at a Comisaría de Policía (specifically its Oficina de Extranjería) or a designated police station handling foreigner services. The catch with applying domestically is appointment availability: in major cities like Madrid and Barcelona, booking slots through the online cita previa system can involve waits of four to eight weeks during busy periods, as demand from the steady influx of new arrivals regularly outstrips available appointment capacity. Smaller cities and towns — particularly in less popular regions — tend to have significantly shorter waits, sometimes just days, which is why some relocation advisors suggest booking your NIE appointment in a smaller nearby city rather than fighting for a slot in the capital.

The application itself is straightforward once you have an appointment: you'll need your passport, a completed EX-15 form stating your reason for needing the number (property purchase, work, study, etc.), and proof of the fee payment via Modelo 790 — a nominal government charge commonly around EUR 9.84, paid at a bank before your appointment (some offices also accept payment on-site, but it's safer to pay in advance and bring the stamped receipt). Processing at the appointment itself is usually quick, and many applicants receive their NIE certificate the same day or within a few business days by mail or pickup.

Once issued, your NIE follows a fixed format: a letter (typically X, Y, or Z for foreigners, though other prefixes exist depending on how the number was generated), followed by seven digits, and a final control letter — for example, X1234567L. This number stays with you permanently and is distinct from the TIE (Tarjeta de Identidad de Extranjero), the physical residency card that residency-visa holders separately need to apply for after arrival; the NIE is just the number, while the TIE is the card that proves your specific residency status. Don't confuse the two when researching requirements, since guides and government pages sometimes use the terms loosely.

Given the appointment bottleneck in major cities, the practical move for most newcomers is to book the NIE appointment as early as possible in your planning timeline — ideally before you've locked in permanent housing — since so much else (bank account, phone contract, lease signing) is gated behind having the number in hand.

A few common mistakes are worth flagging specifically. First, don't confuse the consulate application route with the in-Spain route and assume the process or timeline is identical — consulates typically process applications faster precisely because demand is lower, but you'll need to already know your travel dates and purpose clearly enough to fill out the paperwork before you've even landed. Second, double-check which police station or Oficina de Extranjería actually covers your intended address before booking — some cities split NIE processing across multiple stations by postal code, and booking the wrong one can mean starting the appointment search over. Third, the EX-15 form requires you to state a specific reason for requesting the NIE (property purchase, opening a business, employment, etc.), and that stated reason should genuinely match your situation — officers have been known to ask follow-up questions or request supporting documents when the stated purpose seems inconsistent with the applicant's visa status or evident plans.

Finally, once you have your NIE, treat the certificate itself carefully: it's a simple paper document, easily lost or damaged, and while the number itself is recorded in Spanish government systems, replacing a lost physical certificate involves its own bureaucratic trip. Photograph it, keep the original somewhere safe, and bring a photocopy rather than the original to routine errands like opening bank accounts once you have it in hand.
