**Moving to Spain as a UK citizen in 2026** means navigating a legal landscape reshaped by Brexit: you are now a third-country national for immigration purposes, and the easy freedom-of-movement era that older British expats in Spain still remember is gone for anyone arriving fresh. The two realistic visa routes are the same ones any non-EU national faces — the non-lucrative visa (NLV), for people living off pensions or savings without working, citing roughly €2,400 in monthly resources in many current consulate briefings, and the digital nomad visa (DNV), for remote workers employed by or contracting for non-Spanish companies, cited near €2,334 monthly. Consulate-specific requirements and document checklists vary enough between UK-based Spanish consulates that verifying with the specific mission handling your application is worth the extra step rather than assuming a figure quoted for one consulate applies everywhere.

One of the sharper post-Brexit realities concerns pensions: UK state pensions paid to people who become Spanish residents *after* Brexit are frozen at their initial rate, without the annual CPI uprating that pensioners in the UK (or EU countries with reciprocal arrangements) receive. This is a meaningfully different situation from, say, [Portugal](/blog/move-to-lisbon-uk-citizen-guide-2026), and it is one of the more consequential planning details for retirees weighing Spain against EU-based alternatives — a frozen pension erodes in real terms every year you live abroad. It's worth noting this only affects new post-Brexit movers; UK citizens who were already resident in Spain before the end of the transition period and hold Withdrawal Agreement (WA) status retain broader protections, including continued pension uprating and many of the rights they had as EU citizens, so if you moved before Brexit took full effect your situation differs substantially from someone arriving today.

Healthcare access follows a similar two-track pattern. New arrivals without WA protections generally need private health insurance to qualify for their visa in the first place, and only gain access to Spain's public system (via the SIP card) once properly registered as residents and, in many cases, contributing to Spanish social security or holding qualifying private cover. The UK's Global Health Insurance Card (GHIC) can bridge gaps for emergency treatment during short stays or transitions but is not a substitute for full coverage as a resident. On driving, UK licences must be exchanged for a Spanish one within a set window after establishing residency — commonly framed as a two-year deadline — after which driving on the UK licence alone becomes legally risky; the exchange process runs through the DGT (Spain's traffic authority) and has had periods of bureaucratic backlog worth planning around.

Spain remains, by a wide margin, one of the most popular relocation destinations for British nationals — estimates put the resident British population above 500,000, a scale that means English-language services, British-oriented estate agents, and expat community infrastructure are genuinely abundant in coastal regions like the Costa del Sol and Costa Blanca. That same popularity, though, means housing competition in the most Brit-favored towns is fierce, with rental and purchase prices in those pockets often running ahead of comparable Spanish towns without the same expat concentration. For a deeper look at the income and renewal mechanics behind the non-lucrative route specifically, the [Spain Non-Lucrative Visa guide](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/spain-non-lucrative-visa-guide-2026) covers that path in more depth.

Tax residency deserves separate attention from immigration status, since the two are easy to conflate but legally distinct. Spending more than 183 days a year in Spain generally makes you a Spanish tax resident regardless of what visa you hold, which brings worldwide income into scope for Spanish tax purposes, subject to the UK-Spain double taxation treaty. UK citizens moving pension income, investment portfolios, or property rental income into this new tax picture should get a cross-border financial review before relocating rather than after, since decisions made under UK tax assumptions — including around ISAs, which lose their UK tax-advantaged status once you're a Spanish tax resident — can carry costly consequences if unwound too late.

Finally, the paperwork sequence matters as much as any individual number. Whichever visa route you pursue, budget for a genuine multi-month process: gathering apostilled documents, background checks, and proof of funds from the UK side, submitting through the relevant Spanish consulate, and then, once in Spain, registering for your TIE residence card and NIE number before you can open a bank account or sign a long lease. Rushing this sequence, or assuming the process moves as fast as a fellow British expat's experience from a few years ago, is one of the most common sources of frustration among UK citizens relocating to Spain today.
