**Starlink as a nomad's internet backup** solves the fiber-and-4G-fails problem better than almost anything else on the market, but the plan you pick and the country you're standing in both matter more than the marketing suggests.

## Roam and Residential are built for different failure modes, not different speeds

Starlink's Roam plan, priced around $50 a month plus roughly $599 for the portable dish kit, is licensed for use in any country where Starlink operates, including mobile use while traveling — the plan built specifically for people who relocate every few weeks or months. Residential, at closer to $120 a month with a cheaper hardware option, is licensed to a fixed service address and is technically meant to stay largely stationary; using a Residential plan outside its registered country can trigger service restrictions or account flags, even though the hardware itself works the same everywhere the satellite constellation reaches. For anyone crossing borders regularly, Roam is the compliant choice even though it costs more per month — the cheaper Residential plan is a false economy the moment you leave its registered country. Real-world speeds commonly land in the 100–250 Mbps range with 20–40 ms latency, comfortably enough for video calls and cloud-based work even where fiber or reliable 4G isn't available.

## Coverage exceeds 150 countries, but the exclusions are not random

Starlink's service map now covers more than 150 countries and territories, but a specific set of countries — China, Russia, and a substantial portion of the Middle East and North Africa — remain unlicensed for the service, generally because those governments restrict or ban unlicensed foreign satellite internet providers outright rather than Starlink choosing not to operate there. Bringing a Starlink kit into one of these restricted countries, even for personal use, can range from a confiscated dish to a genuine legal problem, so route planning around China, Russia, and MENA needs to account for this as a hard constraint, not just a coverage gap to work around with a VPN.

## VPN legality is a separate question from Starlink itself

Because Starlink routes traffic through its own satellite backbone rather than a local ISP, some nomads assume it sidesteps local internet restrictions, including VPN bans — it does not. VPN legality is set by the country you are physically in, not by which internet provider you are using, and several countries with restricted Starlink availability also separately restrict or ban VPN use for residents and visitors alike. Treat the two as entirely independent compliance questions: whether Starlink is licensed to operate in a country, and whether VPN use is legal there, need to be checked separately before relying on either.

## Power draw is the hidden cost line most people underestimate

A Starlink dish pulls meaningfully more continuous power than a phone hotspot or even a typical laptop charger — commonly in the 50–75W range for the standard kit under normal conditions — which matters a great deal for van-lifers, boat-based nomads, or anyone running off a portable power station rather than fixed mains electricity. Budgeting for a larger battery bank or a supplementary solar setup before relying on Starlink as a primary connection, rather than discovering the power gap after arrival, avoids the common failure mode of a dish that works perfectly but only for a few hours before the battery dies.

## Customs and import rules deserve a check before the dish ever ships

Bringing Starlink hardware across a border is not universally routine — some countries treat satellite communication equipment as a restricted import requiring a permit or prior notification to a telecoms regulator, separate from the question of whether Starlink service itself is licensed there. Ordering hardware to ship directly to a destination country, rather than carrying a purchased kit through customs from elsewhere, is generally the smoother path where Starlink is officially available, since the company handles the country-specific registration on its end. Where it is not officially available but coverage technically reaches the area, carrying a dish in and using it anyway sits in a legal grey zone that varies enormously by country and is worth researching specifically rather than assuming coverage equals permission.
