**Actually working from [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-first-week-checklist-2026) in 2026** is less about visas — 365-day visa-free entry for 90-plus passports settles that question for most nationalities — and much more about whether your internet holds up during a client call, whether you can find a desk that isn't fully booked by 10am, and how badly the time-zone gap with your clients bites into your working hours. This is the practical setup layer that sits underneath the legal and tax questions covered elsewhere: it assumes you already know you can stay, and focuses on making the actual remote-work day function.

Connectivity in [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-honest-guide-2026) is generally good but uneven by neighborhood and by connection type. Coworking spaces run on dedicated business-grade lines and typically deliver a steady 100+ Mbps with minimal dropouts, which is why most serious remote workers end up basing their working hours there rather than relying purely on home Wi-Fi. Home broadband is more of a mixed bag — apartment-building wiring varies enormously by age and renovation history, and speeds can swing anywhere from 20 to 100 Mbps with occasional dropouts, especially in older Soviet-era buildings in Saburtalo compared to newer developments. The sensible approach almost everyone converges on is keeping a local eSIM or SIM as a mobile hotspot backup — Georgian mobile data is cheap and reasonably fast — so a home router outage doesn't take out a client call.

On coworking specifically, Tbilisi's scene has matured well past "a few cafés with decent Wi-Fi." Terminal, one of the city's most established coworking operators, has multiple locations and a strong community of both local and international remote workers, with day passes and monthly memberships aimed squarely at the digital nomad crowd. Fabrika — technically a hostel-meets-creative-complex in a converted Soviet sewing factory in the Old Town area — has become almost as much a coworking landmark as a place to stay, with its courtyard and open workspaces functioning as an informal networking hub for the city's remote-work community. Beyond those two anchors, a growing number of smaller boutique coworking spots have opened across Vake and Saburtalo, generally cheaper than the flagship options and worth checking if proximity to your apartment matters more than amenities.

The timezone question deserves real attention before you commit to a long stay, because it shapes your entire working day. Georgia sits at GMT+4, which creates a workable but not ideal overlap with US clients — East Coast mornings land in Tbilisi's evening, meaning anyone servicing US business hours will be working into the night rather than a standard 9-to-5. European clients are far easier: Tbilisi runs 2–3 hours ahead of Western Europe (accounting for daylight saving differences), giving a solid morning-to-early-afternoon overlap with London, Paris, or Berlin that lets you front-load calls and leave afternoons for deep work. If your client base is US-heavy, plan your daily schedule around that gap deliberately rather than discovering it after you've settled in — many nomads shift to a later start and later finish specifically to catch US morning meetings in the Tbilisi evening.

Neighborhood choice for connectivity and coworking access tends to favor Vake and Vera, both of which cluster newer apartment stock, reliable infrastructure, and walking-distance access to the city's better coworking options and café culture, over Saburtalo's more residential, further-out feel — though Saburtalo compensates with lower rent for anyone prioritizing budget over a five-minute coworking commute.

For connectivity as a backup plan rather than a primary line, Georgian eSIMs and physical SIMs from Magticom, Silknet, or Cellfie are cheap and simple to set up, and worth activating on day one regardless of how good your apartment's Wi-Fi turns out to be. A dual-connection setup — home broadband plus a mobile hotspot as failover — is standard practice among longer-term remote workers here, since even a reliable connection can go down during scheduled maintenance or, less predictably, during heavy rain, which occasionally disrupts overhead cabling in older parts of the city. Cafés remain a viable backup option too, particularly in Vera and around Rustaveli Avenue, though weekday afternoons can get crowded with other remote workers competing for both power outlets and a stable signal.
