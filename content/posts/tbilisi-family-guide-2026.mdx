**Moving to [Tbilisi](/blog/move-to-georgia-russian-ukrainian-2026) with kids** in 2026 is a genuinely different exercise from the [solo digital nomad setup](/blog/tbilisi-digital-nomad-setup-guide-2026) most guides to the city focus on — the questions that matter shift from coworking Wi-Fi to school admissions, pediatric care, and whether a neighborhood has anywhere for a child to actually play. The good news for relocating families is that Tbilisi delivers on most of these fronts at a fraction of the cost of Western Europe, though it does require accepting some real trade-offs around language and infrastructure that don't come up for adult-only movers.

Schooling is the first and biggest decision. Georgian public schools are free but instruction is heavily in Georgian (Kartvelian), which makes them a poor fit for most expat families unless a longer-term stay and genuine language immersion is the goal. In practice, nearly all expat families route their children into one of Tbilisi's private international schools. The British School of Georgia is the most established Western-curriculum option, with annual tuition commonly cited near $8,000 — worth confirming directly against the current fee schedule since international school pricing shifts year to year and varies by grade level. QSI International School, following an American curriculum, sits meaningfully higher at around $12,000 annually, reflecting its accreditation and standardized American college-prep track. Cambridge International School offers a smaller, more boutique alternative at roughly $5,000 a year — a genuinely useful option for families wanting an English-medium British-curriculum education without QSI's price tag, though smaller schools naturally offer a narrower range of extracurriculars and grade levels than the bigger institutions. Even at the high end, all three options sit dramatically below equivalent international school tuition in London, Zurich, or Singapore, which is a meaningful part of Tbilisi's appeal for cost-conscious relocating families.

Healthcare for children runs on a similar public/private split to the adult system. Iashvili Children's Hospital is the main public pediatric facility and the default for emergencies, but most expat families default to private pediatric care for routine visits, where a private pediatrician appointment commonly runs $20–40 — inexpensive by Western standards and generally staffed with English-speaking doctors in the private clinics that cater to the expat and diplomatic community, with the Evex hospital network being one of the larger private providers offering pediatric services across multiple Tbilisi locations.

On neighborhoods, Vake consistently comes out as the top pick for families: it combines relatively safe, walkable streets with genuine green space, including Vake Park, one of the city's largest and best-maintained parks, making it a natural draw for anyone prioritizing playground access and outdoor space for kids. Saburtalo is the budget alternative — noticeably cheaper rent, but its Soviet-era apartment blocks often mean older lifts, less consistent building maintenance, and a more residential, less walkable feel that some families find less convenient with young children in tow. Mtatsminda, the hillside district anchored by the funicular and park of the same name, is another weekend draw for families thanks to its amusement park and green space, even if it's less commonly a primary residential choice.

Budget for a family of four running a comfortable but not extravagant lifestyle — housing, groceries, some extracurricular clubs, and the occasional weekend trip out to the Racha mountain region or similar — typically lands in the $2,000–3,500 monthly range, though this swings meaningfully based on which school tier you choose, since tuition alone can eat a significant chunk of that budget once you're paying for more than one child. One genuine climate consideration worth planning around: Tbilisi summers are hot, with July and August highs regularly hitting 35–38°C, which makes air conditioning and access to a pool (whether through a residential building, gym membership, or dedicated swim club) less of a luxury and more of a practical necessity for families with young kids during peak summer.
