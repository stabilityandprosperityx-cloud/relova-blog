## What's Still Genuinely Great

**The honest version of moving to [Tbilisi](/blog/move-to-kutaisi-georgia-guide-2026)** in 2026 starts by separating what's still genuinely great from what the "cheapest city in Europe" marketing conveniently leaves out. The good parts are real: this is still a city of exceptional natural wine, a genuinely world-class techno scene built around venues like Bassiani that draw international attention, and jaw-dropping Caucasus hiking within a few hours' drive. None of that has changed. What has changed is the cost narrative — the "cheap forever" pitch that circulated heavily a few years ago is no longer accurate, and anyone planning a move on outdated cost assumptions is going to be unpleasantly surprised.

## Rent Has Risen Sharply Since 2021

Rent is the clearest example. A one-bedroom in Vera, one of the most popular expat neighborhoods for its walkability and café culture, now commonly runs $500–900 a month, compared to roughly $150–250 back in 2021. That's an 80–120% increase, driven largely by the demand spike that followed 2022, when a large wave of relocating workers — many from Russia — arrived in a short window and put sustained pressure on the rental market. Prices haven't fully corrected since, and while [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-budget-guide-2026) remains cheap relative to Western Europe, the "$150 apartment" stories that still circulate online are years out of date and will set unrealistic expectations for anyone budgeting a move today.

## The One Percent Tax Regime, With Caveats

On the money side, Georgia's one-percent tax headline — the Individual Entrepreneur plus Small Business Status regime taxing turnover at 1% up to a generous annual threshold — remains real and remains a genuine draw for freelancers and remote business owners who can document their income cleanly. But "cleanly" carries more weight than it used to: banking compliance has tightened noticeably, and individual entrepreneurs sometimes face additional KYC documentation requests that weren't as common a few years ago. This isn't a dealbreaker, but it does mean the account-opening and registration process takes more patience than the breezier accounts from 2019–2021 suggest.

## Political Protests and EU Candidacy

The honest guide also has to acknowledge Georgia's political situation, because it's not background noise — it's something anyone settling in should understand. Protests through 2024 and into 2025, centered on a controversial foreign-influence law and broader tensions over the country's EU accession path, have been a real and visible feature of life in central [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-digital-nomad-setup-guide-2026) at various points, and the country's EU candidacy status has effectively stalled as a result. None of this makes Tbilisi unsafe for day-to-day expat life — the protests have generally been concentrated and largely peaceful in the areas that draw international coverage — but it is a reminder that Georgia sits at a genuine geopolitical fault line between Russia and the West, and that reality shapes both the news cycle and, at times, the mood of the city in ways a purely lifestyle-focused guide would gloss over.

## Air Quality and Traffic

On day-to-day quality of life, air quality is a fair middle-of-the-road complaint: Tbilisi's AQI often sits in the 50–80 range, which is acceptable and far from the worst cities in the region, but noticeably worse than the Alpine-clean air some newcomers expect from photos of the surrounding mountains. Traffic has also worsened as car imports have boomed, and central streets can feel genuinely congested at peak hours in a way that undercuts the city's otherwise excellent walkability in the core districts.

## The Bottom Line on Value

None of this changes the fundamental value proposition. Nightlife, restaurants, and hospitality remain exceptional value against Western European prices, and a realistic nomad budget of $1,200–2,000 a month still buys a comfortable life that would cost multiples of that in most EU capitals. The honest read is simply that Tbilisi in 2026 is a good, still-affordable base with real and growing frictions — rising rents, tighter banking, and political uncertainty — that deserve a place in your planning rather than being left out of the pitch.
