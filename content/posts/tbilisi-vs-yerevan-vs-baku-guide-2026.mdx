**Choosing a South Caucasus base among [Tbilisi](/blog/move-to-georgia-russian-ukrainian-2026), [Yerevan](/blog/move-to-yerevan-guide-2026), and Baku** means weighing three countries that are all genuinely affordable by Western standards but differ meaningfully on how easy they are to enter, how long you can legally stay, and how the local tax and business environment treats a newcomer. For most first-time movers to the region, legal stay length and entry friction end up mattering more than the modest cost-of-living gaps between the three.

Georgia remains the easiest and most-used entry point into the region for a reason: its 365-day visa-free stay for most Western passports is the longest uncomplicated legal stay on offer anywhere in the South Caucasus, and it pairs with the 1% turnover tax available to registered Individual Entrepreneurs — a combination that has made [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-vs-batumi-expat-guide-2026) the default landing spot for remote workers exploring the region. One-bedroom rentals in [Tbilisi](/blog/tbilisi-budget-guide-2026) typically run USD 400–700, middle of the pack among the three capitals but justified by the city's much more developed remote-work and expat infrastructure.

Armenia offers a shorter but still generous entry: most passports get up to 180 days visa-free, roughly half of Georgia's allowance, which makes it a workable mid-range option for people not committing to a full year but wanting more flexibility than a standard 90-day Schengen-style stay. Yerevan has built a genuinely growing tech and startup ecosystem in recent years, partly fueled by an influx of relocating workers and companies since 2022, and the city carries a reputation for relative political stability compared to some of its neighbors. Rents in Yerevan run USD 400–650, comparable to Tbilisi, making the cost decision between the two cities close to a wash — the real differentiator is the shorter visa-free window and a smaller, though rapidly growing, expat and remote-work community.

Azerbaijan is the outlier of the three on both entry mechanics and cost. Rather than a long visa-free stay, most visitors need an e-visa, which is inexpensive (commonly cited around USD 26) and processes quickly online — a genuinely fast entry option, but one that comes with a shorter permitted stay than either Georgia or Armenia, making Baku better suited to shorter stints than a full-year base unless you pursue a more formal residency route. Cost of living is Baku's clearest draw: one-bedroom rents run USD 235–410, the lowest of the three capitals, and Azerbaijan's tax treatment can favor foreign-source income in some cases, though the specifics depend heavily on your residency and income structure and are worth confirming with a local advisor rather than assumed from general guidance.

Put simply: Georgia wins on ease and length of stay, Armenia offers a reasonable middle ground with a genuinely interesting and growing tech scene, and Azerbaijan wins on raw cost but asks you to work within a tighter visa window. For most newcomers exploring the region for the first time, Tbilisi's combination of long visa-free stay and mature expat infrastructure makes it the sensible starting point, with Yerevan and Baku worth considering as either a complement or an alternative once you understand which trade-offs matter most to your specific situation.

Culturally, the three cities offer genuinely distinct experiences rather than interchangeable "cheap Caucasus city" options. Tbilisi has built the most visible international nightlife and creative scene of the three, anchored by its wine culture and a techno scene that draws visitors from across Europe. Yerevan carries a slower, more intellectual and artistic character, with a strong café culture and a diaspora-fueled cosmopolitanism that shows up in its restaurant scene and growing arts community. Baku is the most modern and infrastructure-heavy of the three on the surface — oil wealth has funded striking contemporary architecture and a polished city center — though it also carries the most restrictive political environment of the three, worth weighing for anyone sensitive to freedom-of-expression considerations in their day-to-day life.

Connectivity between the three cities is another practical factor: overland travel between Tbilisi and Yerevan is straightforward via a well-used bus and shared-taxi corridor, making it realistic to base in one and visit the other regularly. The Georgia–Azerbaijan border crossing is also functional for overland travel, but Armenia and Azerbaijan share a closed border due to the long-unresolved Nagorno-Karabakh conflict, meaning direct travel between Yerevan and Baku is not possible without routing through a third country — a geopolitical reality worth understanding if your plans involve regularly moving between all three.
