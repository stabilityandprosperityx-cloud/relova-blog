Most people choose a nomad base by flight time from their old city. The number that actually determines whether the job survives is **overlap hours** — how many hours a day your working window intersects your team's, your clients', or your time-sensitive collaborators'. Get that number wrong and you are not managing jetlag, you are managing a slow-motion resignation.

## Do the overlap math before you book anything

If your core team runs US Eastern (UTC-5, UTC-4 in summer) and you base in **Lisbon (UTC+0, UTC+1 with EU daylight saving)**, you get a genuinely workable overlap in your early afternoon against their morning — roughly a five-to-six-hour window if you're willing to start work by 1–2pm local and run into early evening. Base in **Bangkok or Bali (UTC+7/+8)** against that same US Eastern team, and your overlap collapses to a sliver in your late evening or their late night — workable for asynchronous roles, brutal for anything with daily standups. **Mexico City and Bogotá (UTC-6 and UTC-5)** solve this differently: they sit close enough to US time zones that a normal 9-to-5 local day maps almost directly onto US business hours with only a one-to-two hour shift, which is why Latin America has become the default "stay close to US clients" nomad region rather than a cost play alone.

## Daylight saving time will move your overlap twice a year

The European Union still shifts clocks on the **last Sunday of March and the last Sunday of October** — the European Parliament voted in 2019 to end the practice, but member states never agreed on implementation, so the twice-yearly change remains EU law as of 2026 and there's no confirmed end date to plan around. The United States shifts on different dates (second Sunday of March, first Sunday of November), which means for roughly two to three weeks each spring and autumn, US and EU clocks are offset by an hour more or less than the rest of the year — a scheduling trap that catches recurring meetings set up months in advance. Most of Asia, most of Africa, and countries like India, Georgia, and Turkey do not observe daylight saving at all, so if your team spans a DST country and a non-DST one, your overlap window itself moves seasonally even though nobody involved changed location.

## Async-first employers change the whole equation

Companies that build genuinely asynchronous workflows — GitLab's public handbook is the most-cited example, built around documented decisions, recorded video updates, and meetings as a last resort rather than a default — remove most of the overlap problem entirely. If you're negotiating a remote arrangement before a move, the real question to ask isn't "what hours do you expect me online," it's whether the team's default communication mode is synchronous (Slack-and-meeting-heavy, overlap-dependent) or asynchronous (documentation and recorded-update-heavy, largely overlap-independent). The answer changes which time zones are viable for you by an order of magnitude, and it is a far more honest predictor of your quality of life than any "best cities for digital nomads" ranking.

## Build a floor, not just a base

Rather than picking one city, decide your **minimum viable overlap** in hours per working day, then rule out or rule in bases against that number. Four hours of genuine overlap is workable for most collaborative roles; two hours is workable only if your role is largely asynchronous already; zero overlap generally only works for fully deliverable-based freelance or contract work where nobody needs you live. Once you have that floor, the time-zone map narrows fast — and it usually rules out the "opposite side of the planet" dream base for anyone with a synchronous day job, however good the cost of living looks on paper.

## The jetlag tax is real even without changing continents

Even a modest 3–4 hour time-zone shift imposes a measurable adjustment period — circadian rhythm typically needs roughly a day per hour of shift to fully re-align, which is why a nomad who relocates every few weeks across meaningfully different zones is running a low-grade sleep deficit more often than they realize. Building in a few no-meeting buffer days around any relocation that crosses more than two or three time zones is a small scheduling habit that saves a surprising amount of the fatigue people otherwise blame on "nomad burnout."
