Expats fly more than almost anyone else — home visits, visa runs, regional weekends — which makes the points-and-miles game worth systemizing rather than improvising. The catch is that most travel-hacking advice online is written for people living in one country with one home airport, and a chunk of it stops working once you're routing flights and mail through two or three countries a year.

## Sign-up bonuses swing hard and expire fast

The **Chase Sapphire Preferred**, a staple travel card for US-based expats who keep a US address for banking, normally carries a bonus in the **60,000–80,000 point** range after meeting a minimum spend. In mid-2026 Chase ran a rare **100,000-point** promotional offer — only the third time in the card's history — that ended on 30 July 2026 and has since reverted to the standard tier. That swing matters for one reason: any "sign up now for 100k points" advice you find online dated before August 2026 is describing an expired promotion, not the current baseline. Always check the live offer on the issuer's own page before applying, since the headline bonus is the most time-sensitive number in this entire category.

## Award search tools save more than they cost

Two paid tools do the heavy lifting that used to take hours of manual airline-site searching. **Seats.aero** aggregates award availability across dozens of programs for roughly **$10 a month**, letting you set alerts for a specific route and cabin rather than checking manually. **ExpertFlyer** runs a similar **$10 a month** and specializes in seat-map and fare-class inventory tracking — useful for confirming a specific upgrade or award seat actually exists before you burn a redemption trying to book it. For anyone booking more than two or three long-haul award trips a year, the combined $20 a month is usually recovered many times over in cash value versus paying full fare or missing available award space entirely.

## Error fares are real but genuinely rare

Airline pricing mistakes — a transatlantic business-class fare typo'd into economy pricing, for instance — do surface periodically, and when they do the discount against normal pricing is often **30–70%**. The realistic approach is not to hunt for these actively but to follow one or two dedicated error-fare alert accounts and be ready to book within the window before the airline catches and voids the fare. Airlines are not obligated in every jurisdiction to honor a mistaken fare once discovered, though enforcement varies by country and has been the subject of ongoing consumer-protection litigation — treat a booked error fare as a pleasant possibility, not a guaranteed trip, until it survives ticketing.

## Hidden-city ticketing sits in a genuine legal gray zone

Hidden-city ticketing — booking a itinerary with a connection in your actual destination and simply not flying the final leg — is not illegal under any criminal or civil statute most travelers will ever encounter, and a landmark ruling in ongoing Lufthansa and American Airlines litigation has kept the practice broadly legal to book. It is, however, a clear breach of every major airline's contract of carriage, and airlines have pursued individual travelers for the fare difference, clawed back frequent-flyer miles, or in rarer cases suspended loyalty accounts entirely. It also breaks cleanly if you check a bag (it travels to the original ticketed destination, not where you get off) or if you're on a round-trip itinerary and skip an outbound leg, since airlines commonly cancel the return automatically. Treat it as a targeted, occasional tool for one-way point-to-point savings, not a routine strategy, given that the downside — an account suspension — can wipe out years of accumulated points in a program you actually rely on.
