Naturalisation in the UK is a two-stage process, and most of the risk sits in stage one, not stage two. You first need **Indefinite Leave to Remain (ILR)** — permanent settled status — which for most work and family visa routes requires **five years of continuous lawful residence**. The rule that trips up more applicants than any other is the **180-day absence limit**: you cannot spend more than 180 days outside the UK in *any rolling 12-month window* during that five-year period, not per calendar year. A long remote-work stint abroad or an extended family visit that seems fine in isolation can push you over the line once IRCC-style rolling-window math is applied — check your actual travel history against a rolling calendar before you assume you qualify, don't just count total days per year.

## The "Earned Settlement" reform hasn't landed — but watch it

The UK government ran a consultation on extending the standard qualifying period for settlement from five years to **ten**, which closed on 12 February 2026. As of the most recent guidance, no change has taken effect and the current five-year rules still apply — but the consultation signals the direction of travel, and anyone mid-way through their qualifying period should watch for an implementation date rather than assume the five-year rule is locked in through their entire application window.

## Naturalisation itself: fee, test, and the good-character bar

Once you hold ILR, you can apply for [citizenship](/blog/dubai-uk-citizen-guide-2026) after a further 12 months (or immediately, in some spouse-of-British-citizen cases). The naturalisation application fee is **£1,839** — a number that has climbed steadily and is worth confirming on gov.uk before you budget, since Home Office fees change with little notice. You'll also need to pass the **Life in the UK test**: 24 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes, with a **75% pass mark** (18 of 24 correct), booked at an approved test centre for a **£50** fee per sitting. Beyond the test, the Home Office assesses "good character" — this checks criminal record, immigration compliance history (including any past overstays or breaches), and financial conduct such as undischarged bankruptcy or unpaid tax. A minor visa breach years earlier can resurface here even if it caused no problem at the time.

## English language proof most applicants already have

If your immigration route to ILR already required an English-language test (most work visas do), you typically don't need to resit it for naturalisation — but the accepted evidence has specific validity windows and approved-provider lists that change, so confirm your existing certificate or degree still counts as current evidence rather than assuming an old pass carries over automatically.

## Budget and timeline beyond the headline fee

Add certified translations for any non-English documents, biometric enrolment costs, and — if you're applying through a solicitor rather than doing it yourself — legal fees that can run into four figures for a straightforward case. Processing for naturalisation applications commonly takes a few months once submitted, but the real time sink is almost always upstream: assembling five years of clean travel records, employment continuity, and tax evidence before you even file.

The throughline for 2026: map your ILR and [citizenship](/blog/move-to-scotland-guide-2026) timeline as one project from day one of your visa, track absences on a rolling basis rather than annually, and treat the £1,839 fee as the smallest line item compared to what a documentation gap costs you in delay.
