Moving abroad on a vegan or fully plant-based diet is less about finding “the perfect vegan city” and more about matching cuisine culture, retail density, and social norms to how strictly you eat. Skip the generic top-ten list. Start with **where life is easy versus hard**, then **prepare before you fly**, then **operate on the ground** in restaurants, shops, and social meals.

## Where the move is easier — and where it is harder

Easiest clusters in 2026 still look familiar: large Western European capitals such as **Berlin, London, and Amsterdam**, where vegan labelling, dedicated restaurants, and supermarket alternatives are mainstream. **Thailand** and parts of Buddhist-influenced Southeast Asia remain surprisingly workable because vegetarian temple food traditions translate into everyday menus — you still need to watch for fish sauce and egg, but the baseline literacy around non-meat plates is higher than first-time visitors expect.

Harder clusters are cuisines organised around meat and dairy as hospitality itself: parts of Eastern Europe, the Caucasus, and meat-forward Asian regions where “vegetarian” may still mean chicken stock or butter. Rural areas almost anywhere compress choice further, especially outside tourist season when specialty products disappear from shelves.

The useful framing is not morality ranking — it is logistics. A strict vegan in a dairy-heavy food culture spends more time cooking at home and explaining boundaries. A flexible plant-based eater in Berlin barely notices. Be honest about which person you are before you sign a lease in a small inland town.

City size multiplies options. Capitals and large university cities almost always stock plant milks and basic vegan staples now. Secondary cities can, but weekend stockouts are real. If your job is remote, prefer a metro with multiple supermarket chains over a picturesque village that photographs well for Instagram.

## Practical preparation before you relocate

Learn key ingredient phrases in the local language — “no meat, no fish, no egg, no dairy/butter/ghee,” plus words for honey, gelatin, and lard if relevant. Phone translators fail in noisy restaurants and with older servers who will not read a screen. A ten-phrase card in your notes app beats improvisation at the counter.

Install **HappyCow** (or your preferred map) and check coverage for your exact neighbourhood before you commit to a flat. Sparse pins in the suburb you liked on a housing site are a warning. Dense pins near a metro stop are a quality-of-life feature equal to fibre speed.

Pack a short transition kit if you take specialty supplements (B12, specific protein powders) that may be regulated or expensive locally. Research whether pharmacies carry fortified plant milks or whether you will import for the first quarter.

If you share housing, state dietary needs in flatmate interviews the way you would state quiet hours. Kitchen cross-contamination fights destroy housemate relationships faster than rent disputes. Clarify shelf space and labelled oils early.

## On the ground: restaurants, shops, social situations

In large cities almost anywhere, supermarket plant milks and basic vegan SKUs exist; in small towns and countryside, expect gaps. Build a “home base” grocery list in week one: which chain has oat milk, which market has tofu, which bakery is reliably egg-free. Rotate two reliable restaurants for work lunches so decision fatigue does not push you into accidental non-vegan defaults when deadlines hit.

Coworking communities and expat networks in nomad hubs — **Lisbon, Bali, Mexico City** — often maintain unofficial vegan maps better than tourist blogs. Ask in the Slack/WhatsApp in your first week; locals filter for kitchens that actually understand the brief.

Social meals need a script. In cultures where shared food is the social glue — the same dynamic we cover in guides on building a social life abroad — refusing the central dish can read as rejecting the relationship. Soften that with specifics: bring a shareable plant dish, offer to host next time, and explain briefly without a TED talk. Patience beats purity contests with new colleagues.

Travel weekends inside your new country deserve the same prep as the original move: check HappyCow for the secondary city, learn regional dish names that hide animal products, and keep snacks for train delays in meat-only station belts.

Vegan relocation works when you treat food access as infrastructure — language cards, retail density, and social scripts — not as a lifestyle aesthetic you will “figure out later.” The goal is a boring, repeatable week of meals that protects both your ethics and your calendar.
