**visa fees comparison 2026** is less about the stamp fee and more about the **income floor** behind it. More than **65 countries** now offer some form of digital-nomad permission. Thresholds range from Georgia’s **€0 / 365-day visa-free** stay to Thailand LTR’s high band near **$80,000/year**. Most programs still want **3–6 months** of bank statements.

## How to read nomad “fees” in 2026

Government stamp costs are often small next to proof-of-funds. Croatia (~**€2,540/month**), Spain (~**€2,850**), Italy (~**€2,700–2,800**), and Portugal D8 (~**€3,280–3,680** depending on source) sit in a similar European band. Dubai cites **$3,500** (employee) / **$5,000** (business) with ~**$334** in fees. Korea’s F-1-D spans roughly **$33,000–66,000/year** by age/region; Japan’s designated activities track sits near **$67,000–70,000/year** for up to **six non-renewable months**. Malaysia DE Rantau is often framed near **$2,000/month**; Mexico and Colombia sit lower but vary by consulate. Broader context: [digital nomad visas](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/digital-nomad-visa-complete-guide-2026) and [Europe nomad comparison](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/nomad-visa-europe-comparison).

## Who actually leads to permanent residence

Among popular picks, **Portugal, Spain, Italy, and Croatia** are the ones most often described as leading toward PR/citizenship after about **five years** (Croatia usually via a separate post-nomad path). UAE, Japan, South Korea, and Thailand LTR are typically **temporary** without a direct PR handoff—UAE needs a separate Golden Visa story. Fast PR options elsewhere: [permanent residency routes](https://blog.relova.ai/blog/permanent-residency-abroad-fastest-routes-2026).

A few patterns stand out from that table. The gap between "cheap" and "expensive" nomad programmes has less to do with the government stamp fee — which rarely exceeds a few hundred dollars anywhere — and everything to do with the income floor you have to document, and those floors span an enormous range from Georgia's effectively nonexistent bar to Thailand's LTR programme sitting near $80,000 a year for its high-income pensioner track. Europe's mainstream nomad visas (Croatia, Spain, Italy, Portugal) cluster together in a fairly narrow €2,500–3,700 monthly band, which makes them broadly interchangeable on income grounds and means the real decision between them usually comes down to tax treatment, PR pathway, and lifestyle fit rather than qualification difficulty. Asian and Middle Eastern options diverge much more sharply: Dubai's tiered system separates employees from business owners with different thresholds, while Japan's programme remains the outlier of the group — a high income bar paired with a strict six-month, non-renewable ceiling that makes it better suited to a long working holiday than a genuine relocation.

The PR question is worth dwelling on because it changes what a nomad visa is actually for. Portugal, Spain, Italy, and (via a separate follow-on path) Croatia are the programmes most consistently described as leading toward permanent residency or citizenship after roughly five years of qualifying residence, which is why so many long-term relocation planners default toward the European options even when the income floor is higher than an Asian alternative. The UAE, Japan, South Korea, and Thailand's LTR track, by contrast, are structured as long-stay work arrangements rather than immigration pathways — the UAE requires a wholly separate Golden Visa application if permanent status is the goal, and Japan's non-renewable structure makes the question moot entirely. If your five-year plan includes eventually holding an EU passport or long-term residency rather than perpetual visa renewals, that distinction should weigh more heavily in your decision than the headline income number.
