US citizens abroad do not lose the federal ballot because they moved. The **Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act (UOCAVA, 1986)** is the statute that keeps overseas civilians and uniformed voters in the federal election. 2026 is a **midterm** year: every **435** House seats and **33 of 100** Senate seats are on the ballot. That calendar is the reason to treat absentee voting as a dated project, not a civic slogan.

This guide is a 2026 workflow: who is covered, how to file the **Federal Post Card Application (FPCA)**, how to actually receive and return a ballot, and what to do if you also hold another citizenship.

## Who can vote from abroad in 2026

UOCAVA covers US citizens living outside the United States who remain eligible to vote in federal elections. It is a **right to an absentee process**, not a guarantee that your state will make returning the ballot effortless. States still run elections; UOCAVA sets the federal floor.

A federal estimate puts roughly **2.8 million** non-military US citizens overseas in the eligible pool. Participation is far thinner. After the **2022 midterms**, about **95,000** of those ballots came back — around **3.4%**. The gap is mostly process friction: late registration, paper mail that misses the deadline, and confusion about which address still “counts” as your voting residence. Wanting to vote is not the bottleneck. Completing the loop is.

If you last voted in a US state, that state is usually still your voting jurisdiction unless you have established a new US domicile. Confirm the rule with your state election office rather than guessing from a Facebook group. Name changes, a new overseas street, and a move between countries all force a fresh FPCA (see below).

Military and overseas civilian rules overlap in the FPCA itself, but local clerks treat them as different files. Use the overseas-civilian path unless you are actually covered as uniformed service.

## How to register: file an FPCA (every year)

The **FPCA** is the single federal form that both registers you (where required) and requests an absentee ballot. Get it through a US embassy or consulate, or through the federal voting assistance channels those posts point you to. File it again:

- at the start of **every calendar year**, and
- whenever your **address or legal name** changes.

Do not recycle last year’s PDF with a new date scribbled in the corner. Clerks reject stale data.

File early enough that the clerk can mail or email materials **at least 45 days** before the federal election — that 45-day window is the UOCAVA mailing floor, not a suggestion. For 2026 midterms, work backwards from Election Day in November: if you wait until October, you are already in the failure pattern that produced the 3.4% return rate.

Keep a dated copy of what you sent (scan or email archive). When a clerk says they never received the form, that file is the only proof you have.

## How you actually get the ballot — and how you send it back

Once the FPCA is accepted, the state should send the ballot on the 45-day clock. **Choose electronic delivery** wherever your state allows it. Paper packets crossing oceans are how midterm ballots die in transit.

Return rules are **state-specific**. Some states accept an electronic return. Most still want a **wet-ink ballot** mailed to the US or handed to a US embassy/consulate for pouching. Read the instructions that arrive with *your* ballot, not a blog post about a different state.

Practical sequence for 2026:

1. Confirm your last US voting residence and eligibility.
2. Submit a new FPCA as soon as the calendar year opens — and again after any move.
3. Select email/online ballot delivery if offered.
4. Print, sign, and return by the method your state lists; use a trackable service if you are mailing.
5. Check your state’s ballot-tracker if one exists; do not assume silence means success.

If you miss the federal 45-day send, you can still sometimes vote a **Federal Write-In Absentee Ballot** as a backup. That is an emergency tool, not a plan.

## If the United States is not your only citizenship

Most other democracies run an overseas analogue through their foreign ministry or consular network: a register of electors abroad, a postal pack, or in-person voting at a mission. Those systems are **not** UOCAVA. Deadlines, ID rules, and whether you can vote in national versus local races all differ.

If you hold two passports, complete **each** country’s process separately. Do not assume an FPCA does anything for a non-US election, and do not assume a consular stamp from another country registers you in Florida.

Start with the embassy of the country whose election you care about, then put both calendars on one sheet so the 2026 US midterm and any home-country vote do not collide with travel.

Voting from abroad in 2026 is paperwork with a clock. File the FPCA, pick electronic delivery, and treat the return method as a state rule — that is the whole game.
